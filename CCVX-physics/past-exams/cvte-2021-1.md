## Opgave A — Planck (Q1–Q7)

**Subdomains:** [P05](../P05-gravitation/) (L2 gravity + orbit), [P10](../P10-electromagnetic-radiation-and-stars/) (Wien, EM spectrum). **BINAS:** g, k_W.

### Q1 (1p) — why the CMB map can't be shot from the ground
**Solution:** the atmosphere blocks part of the microwave band (water vapour and oxygen absorb right there) and, worse, ground noise competes with a signal of 0.001% contrast — precision cosmology goes to orbit.
**Common mistakes:** "clouds block light" thinking (this is about microwave transparency and interference, not weather).
**Points:** 1 correct reason (1).

### Q2 (2p) — max model value of a_g,earth
**Solution:** in the model's range (x ≥ R_earth) a_g,earth = G·M_earth/x² is largest at the smallest x: at x = R_earth → the familiar surface value **9.8 m/s²**.
**Common mistakes:** maximising at x = 0 (outside the model range: physically the model only runs from the surface outward).
**Points:** at x = R_earth (1), 9.8 m/s² (1).

### Q3 (3p) — which of the four graph continuations is right
**Solution:** the Sun's gravity does not care about the Earth's presence: the a_g,sun curve runs *straight through* (undisturbed inverse-square). The Earth's pull measures distance from its own centre: positive x means rightward, so with the sign convention the contribution must **flip sign at x = 0** — exactly graph **IV**.
**Common mistakes:** both curves sign-flipping (only the Earth flips: the Sun reference point is far to the left); both straight (the Earth term is truly local).
**Points:** a_g,sun unaffected (1), sign flip of a_g,earth over x = 0 (1), IV (1).

### Q4 (4p) — derive T = 2π√(r/a)
**Solution:** orbit balancing: F_gravity = F_mpz → m·a = m·v²/r (the body's gravity acceleration a = GM/r² plays the role). Circular speed: v = 2πr/T. Substituting: a = v²/r = 4π²r/T² → solve for T: T² = 4π²r/a → **T = 2π√(r/a)**. ∎
**Common mistakes:** v = 2πd/T with the diameter (the orbit radius!); forgetting the square root when inverting.
**Points:** F_g = F_mpz (1), both formula choices (1), v = 2πr/T (1), completion (1).

### Q5 (2p) — why L2's longer arm lets the same T stand
**Solution:** T = 2π√(r/a_res): T *equal* with r *larger* only if a_res is also larger. At L2, outside Earth's orbit, the satellite is pulled sunwards by *both* the Sun (slightly weakened at the bigger distance) **and** the Earth (adding in the same direction): the resultant a_res exceeds the plain solar value Earth's, so the same period survives on a larger radius. (NB: the CvTE scoring granted these 2 points to everyone due to the question's wording; the physics above is the intended route.)
**Common mistakes:** arguing "bigger speed compensates"; forgetting that both pulls point sunward *at L2* (they add up there, not cancel).
**Points:** a_res must grow with r for same T (1), the added pull at L2 (1).

### Q6 (3p) — L2's distance from Earth's centre
**Solution:** Earth's own period reads off the sun-only curve at x = 0: T_earth = 3.16·10⁷ s (one year in seconds ✓ consistent). L2 is where the combined curve T_sun+earth(x) hits that same period: dropping down to the axis at the crossing reads **x = 1.5·10⁹ m** (±0.1·10⁹ m) — the real L2 point, about four lunar distances beyond us.
**Common mistakes:** reading x where the two curves *cross* (that is not the condition: the condition is T_combined(x) = T_earth(x=0)); using 365 days instead of reading the sun-only value at x = 0.
**Points:** T_earth read at x = 0 (1), matching level on the combined curve (1), completion (1).

### Q7 (3p) — temperature of the cosmic background
**Solution:** the printed Planck curve peaks at λ_max = 1.05 mm. Wien: T = k_W/λ_max = 2.898·10⁻³/1.05·10⁻³ = **2.76 K** (accepted window 2.40–2.80 K depending on your read; the famous value is 2.7 K).
**Common mistakes:** taking mm as the unit of the answer in K (believe me, this happens); inverting k_W.
**Points:** λ_max (1), Wien (1), completion (1).

## Opgave B — Cirkelgolf (Q8–Q13)

**Subdomains:** [P01](../P01-oscillations-and-waves/) (standing waves on a loop, interference), syllabus-A skills (proportionality, transformation, significance). **BINAS:** nothing numeric.

### Q8 (2p) — why the wave speed can't be uniform
**Solution:** the photo shows antinode spacings (half wavelengths!) that vary *around the same loop* while the oscillation frequency is one fixed value everywhere. With v = f·λ and f constant, different λ at different spots forces v to differ too → the loop's wave speed is position-dependent.
**Common mistakes:** calling frequency position-dependent (one driver, one fork: f is global along the loop!).
**Points:** v = fλ with fixed f (1), λ varies along the loop (1).

### Q9 (4p) — average wave speed at 69 Hz
**Solution:** five antinodes circle the loop → the circumference carries 5 half-wavelengths: 5·λ/2 = π·d = π·0.245 = 0.770 m → λ_mean = 0.770/2.5 = 0.308 m. Average speed: v = f·λ = 69·0.308 = **21 m/s**.
**Common mistakes:** 5 full wavelengths instead of halves; the diameter instead of the circumference; reading f as angular frequency.
**Points:** 5·½λ (1), circumference (1), v = fλ (1), completion (1).

### Q10 (3p) — why the top host always holds an antinode, and why odd counts only
**Solution:** the driver launches two runners in both directions with the same phase at the attachment (node). Both cover the same path to the top in the same time → they arrive *in phase* there: constructive interference, so the top can only hold a **belly**. Counting the alternating landmarks from bottom (K) around to bottom+top pattern: with a forced node at the driver and a forced antinode at the top, the alternating toothcomb can only close with **an odd number of antinodes** — even counts would put a node on top, contradicting the belly requirement.
**Common mistakes:** attributing the top's belly to local clamping instead of equal path times; drawing an even-count ring (it cannot fit those two boundary anchors).
**Points:** equal arrival phase (1), constructive condition (1), odd count conclusion (1).

### Q11 (3p) — show not-proportional with a numbered check
**Solution:** proportional means equal ratios: with series 1 (3 antinodes, 19 Hz) vs series 4 (9 antinodes, 2.6·10² Hz): antinode factor = 9/3 = 3.0 but frequency factor = 260/19 = 13.7. Not equal → **no proportional relation**. (The series curve is proudly nonlinear — Q12 shows it is quadratic.)
**Common mistakes:** one ratio computed and the argument dropped; comparing series 2 & 3 with f in different notations (compute both ratios raw!).
**Points:** proportionality condition (1), both ratios computed (1), conclusion (1).

### Q12 (5p) — coordinate-transformation axes + the constant c
**Solution:** the proposed fit is f = c·n², a y = a·x: the axes must be **horizontal: n² (–)**, **vertical: f (Hz)**. The straight trend line through the origin has slope c = 350/110 = **3.18 s⁻¹** (±0.05 s⁻¹). Unit: [f]/[n²] → s⁻¹ (Hz), dimensionless n² — 3 significant figures as the question demands.
**Common mistakes:** n on the x-axis (that would curve again); c in s⁻² or with n still inside; rounding to 2 or 4 digits — the 3-figure bill is explicit here.
**Points:** n² axis (1), f axis (1), slope = c (1), unit (1), completion (1).

### Q13 (3p) — how many significant figures does a fitted c deserve?
**Solution:**
- Roland's veto fails because **the antinode count is a discrete count** — counted quantities (like 3, 5, 7 exact bellies) carry no measurement error and do not constrain significance at all.
- Arno's defence holds: c comes from a straight trend line fitted through *all* measured points after the transformation — the fitting across many 2-figure frequencies can sharpen the constant, so writing 3 significant figures is defensible. (Statistics instead of individual point-reading.)
**Common mistakes:** treating counts as measurements with the usual significance rules; hunting for the *one* measurement that "gives" 3 figures instead of the trend fit.
**Points:** count ≠ significance-bearing (1), multiple-points basis (1), trend-line sharpening (1).

## Opgave C — Alfanuclidetherapie (Q14–Q20)

**Subdomains:** [P02](../P02-medical-imaging-and-radiation/) (radiation, decay), [P07](../P07-electric-and-magnetic-fields/) (momentum link), units work. **BINAS:** t½ of Ac-225 (10.0 d), u versus m_e.

### Q14 (2p) — LET has the unit of a force
**Solution:** LET = −dE/dx: [E]/[x] = J/m = N·m/m = **N**. Straight cancellation. ∎
**Common mistakes:** inserting keV and μm into the conversion needlessly (the joule is a newton-metre, full stop).
**Points:** E as N·m (1), completion (1).

### Q15 (2p) — why slower alphas punch harder per µm (before the Bragg peak)
**Solution:** energy loss brakes the particle along its path; plotting LET against depth, the LET *rises* with depth — the draw reads "`the deeper inside, the lower the speed, and per the given relation higher LET`": they co-vary until the LET curve's top (Bragg peak) where the particle dies.
**Common mistakes:** discussing total dose instead of the per-length rate; reading the graph axes backwards.
**Points:** LET rises with depth (pre-peak) (1), speed falls with depth (1).

### Q16 (4p) — the other two faces of "I is slower than II"
**Solution:** apart from the entry value itself:
- **Area under LET(x)**: the total area under each curve mirrors the energy the particle dumps ≈ its entry kinetic energy: curve I carries the smaller area → lower E_k in.
- **Range** (*dracht*): slower particles surrender sooner: I's runs deep only 48 μm against II's 86 μm → I started slower.
**Common mistakes:** mixing the LET curve's height at one depth for the full-energy comparison; treating long range as strong (long range = faster entry here).
**Points:** area ↔ entry energy (1+1), range comparison (1), link range ↔ entry speed (1).

### Q17 (2p) — what a freed daughter costs the patient
**Solution:** torn loose from the carrier, the daughter wanders *through the body* and, itself being radioactive, irradiates **healthy tissue** wherever it lands (kidneys, liver's mercy — the therapy's target discipline dies with the delivery, plus eventually less dose at the tumour).
**Common mistakes:** "the tumour dose just drops" (true but insufficient: the roaming source is the *patient* harm the question aims at).
**Points:** spreading through the body (1), healthy tissue irradiated (1).

### Q18 (5p) — derive p = √(2E_k·m) and the alpha-to-beta 85×
**Solution:** E_k = ½m·v² → m·v² = 2E_k → v = √(2E_k/m); then p = m·v = √(2E_k·m). ∎ For equal energies p ∝ √m: p_α/β = √(m_α/m_e) = √(4.00/5.49·10⁻⁴) = √7286 = **85×**. Also the insight earns its points: recoil trauma ∝ momentum, and the alpha carries 7000 electron masses.
**Common mistakes:** E_k via Boltzmann/kT (irrelevant here); the ratio squared (taking √(4/5.49e-4) for 85 is the asked route — p ratio = √ of the mass ratio!).
**Points:** E_k + p = mv (1), derivation clean (1), p ∝ √m at equal E (1), masses looked up (1), computation (1).

### Q19 (4p) — mass of Ac-225 per polymerosome
**Solution:** N from the activity: N = A·t½/ln 2 = 1.0·10²·(10.0·24·3600)/0.693 = 1.0·10²·8.64·10⁵/0.693 = 1.25·10⁸ nuclei. Mass: N·(225 u) = 1.25·10⁸·225·1.66·10⁻²⁷ = **4.7·10⁻¹⁷ kg** per som. Fifty femtograms of poison per bubble — nuclear medicine works with such dust.
**Common mistakes:** 10 days not converted to seconds; atom mass in u left unconverted.
**Points:** A = (ln2/t½)N (1), t½ looked up (1), m = N·m_Ac (1), completion (1).

### Q20 (4p) — why only alpha counts here, and the 4.8× dose factor
**Solution:**
- Only alpha matters in the dose budget because (1) alphas carry the huge radiation weighting factor (*w_R = 20* against 1 for beta) **and** (2) their LET is so high that all energy is delivered per chain *locally* — the equivalent dose H = w_R·D magnifies exactly the α share.
- Counting the four alpha decays in the printed dominant chain to Bi-209: 5.8 + 6.3 + 7.1 + 8.4 = 27.6 MeV per Ac-225 versus the 5.8 MeV of the parent's solo alpha: **27.6/5.8 = 4.8×** more α energy (and thus ~4.8× the dose equivalent) when the daughters stay caged.
**Common mistakes:** averaging the energies instead of summing (it is four *independent* decays for one single parent nucleus!); counting beta steps in the dose (the trillionth-level β yields a sliver of LET compared to 20×-weighted α).
**Points:** w_R argument (1), energy argument (1), the summed versus first comparison (1), computation (1).

## Opgave D — Zonnepanelen (Q21–Q25)

**Subdomains:** [P06](../P06-electric-circuits/) (series/parallel panels, P = UI), [P11](../P11-quantum-world/) (band gap solar cell), [P10](../P10-electromagnetic-radiation-and-stars/) (photon energy). **BINAS:** h, c, e.

### Q21 (3p) — verify the figure's current at the power max
**Solution:** from the panel's (P,U) curve at U = 0.50 V: P_max reads 1.35 W. Then I = P/U = 1.35/0.50 = **2.7 A** — matching the printed claim. ∎
**Common mistakes:** mixing up the axes (P against I instead of U); reading at 0.40 or 0.60 V.
**Points:** P = UI (1), reading within margin (1), completion (1).

### Q22 (5p) — 36 V from six 12-V panels, and the current drawn
**Solution:**
- 24 cells of 0.50 V in series per panel: U_paneel = 24·0.50 = **12 V**. ∎
- 36 V needs 3 panel voltages in series; the six panels then form **two strings of 3 panels in parallel** (3 panels in series ×2 branches), each branch contributing 2.7 A under the same illumination.
- Resulting current: I = 2·2.7 = **5.4 A**. (Maximum Power Point lived at 0.50 V/cell: the whole array then gives 36 V × 5.4 A ≈ 1.9·10² W.)
**Common mistakes:** panels parallel in fours (24 V instead of 36 V); computing current with the source current doubled per *series* step (series adds voltage, not current!).
**Points:** U-panel 12 V (1), 3-in-series layout (1), equal panel currents (1), current rule (1), completion (1).

### Q23 (2p) — band-gap excitation versus the photoelectric effect
**Solution:** kin: **one photon releases one electron** above a threshold energy in both. Difference: in the photoelectric effect the electron **leaves the material outright**; in a semiconductor cell it *stays inside*, freed to the conduction band (plus its leftover hole) where it can do electric work — that difference is what makes currents harvestable.
**Common mistakes:** answering "semiconductor has a lower threshold energy" (incidental, not the mechanism); saying they are unrelated except photons (the paper explicitly asks for the kin).
**Points:** the shared one-photon–one-electron (1), the stay-inside-versus-leave distinction (1).

### Q24 (3p) — longest photon able to lift Si's gap
**Solution:** the threshold photon lands exactly at the gap: E_gap(Si) = 1.10 eV → λ = hc/E = 1240/1.10 = 1.13·10³ nm = **1.13·10⁻⁶ m** — everything *shorter-wavelength* than this IR limit can run the cell.
**Common mistakes:** taking 1.10 eV as already in joules; reading the wrong table row (CdTe 1.58 sneaks in visually).
**Points:** photon-energy gap link (1), eV → J (1), completion (1).

### Q25 (4p) — ordering the three thin layers on the silicon
**Solution:** sunlight enters from the top, and each layer serves best *first at the highest still-unharvested photon energy*: the layer with the **largest band gap goes on top** (absorbs the most energetic photons, passes the rest), and the stack steps down from there — while every chosen material must still see some harvestable photons above silicon. From the table, the materials above Si (1.10 eV) are ZnS (3.60), ZnSe (2.70), CdTe (1.58): so the stack is **top: ZnS — middle: ZnSe — lower: CdTe — Si on the bottom**. (Ge, InSb and PbSe sit *below* Si's gap: photons above their tiny gaps are already eaten by the layers above or by Si, so those candidates have nothing meaningful left to catch in this stack.)
**Common mistakes:** ascending the stack small-gap-up (then cheap low-energy photons waste the top material while the Si below thermalises the UV); adding Ge as the bottom layer here without the reason.
**Points:** the three right materials (1), top-down gap order (1), gap ↔ photon energy (1), the top-catches-most-energetic insight (1).
