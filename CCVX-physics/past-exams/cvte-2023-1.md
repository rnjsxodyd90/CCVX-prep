## Opgave A — Langlaufen in klassieke stijl (Q1–Q4)

**Subdomains:** [P03](../P03-force-and-motion/) (spring, friction, (v,t) areas), [P04](../P04-energy-and-interaction/) (work from (F,s)). **BINAS:** g.

### Q1 (4p) — distance in one stroke cycle from the (v,t)
**Solution:** one cycle runs from ≈ 1.97 s to ≈ 4.10 s (2.13 s), starting at 0.30 m/s and peaking at 2.43 m/s — split the area into a rectangle plus a triangle: s = 0.30·2.13 + ½·2.13·2.13 = 0.64 + 2.27 = **2.9 m** (±0.3 m). Distance = area under a (v,t), cycle boundary = where the pattern repeats.
**Common mistakes:** reading the peak v as the average; using the full graph instead of one cycle; climbing "2 significant figures" to three.
**Points:** area insight (1), cycle time (1), area method (1), completion + significance (1).

### Q2 (4p) — does this camber ski suit an 80 kg skier?
**Solution:** on both skis each one carries 40 kg: F = 40·9.81 = 392 N per ski. Spring model with C = 1.0·10⁵ N/m: compression u = F/C = 392/1.0·10⁵ = 3.9·10⁻³ m = 3.9 mm. That exceeds the camber height 3.0 mm → the middle touches the snow even gliding on two skis → wrong tool for this skier (he needs stiffer skis). The one-ski side works fine (784 N ≫ presses it, which is the point of the camber design).
**Common mistakes:** using 80 kg per ski (the weight *shares*); comparing force with height instead of compression with camber height; no conclusion stated.
**Points:** weight sharing (1), F = Cu (1), completion (1), comparison + verdict (1).

### Q3 (4p) — matching energy diagrams, and which ski costs more work
**Solution:**
- Reading axes: as force rises, the middle height h *drops* — measured from the camber, the depression s *grows*: the (F,s) diagram must rise. Of the two rising candidates (b and d), the amateur ski needs a *larger force* for the same s than the professional ski (from fig 7 at equal h: F_amateur > F_prof), so its curve must run **higher**: that is diagram **b** (amateur above professional).
- Work = area under the (F,s) curve. The amateur curve lies above everywhere → more area → **the amateur ski needs the most work** to press flat.
**Common mistakes:** mirroring the axes (reading s as height, not depression); comparing works at equal F instead of over the full stroke.
**Points:** h↔s link (1), F-comparison at equal s (1), area = work (1), conclusion (1).

### Q4 (4p) — is f_d proportional to mass or to speed?
**Solution:** graph 9 slopes of F_w versus F_N at fixed speeds: the slope is f_d (from F_w = f_d·F_N). At one fixed speed the line goes through the origin — so F_N (and thereby m) is proportional to F_w at fixed f_d; the slopes themselves do *not* change with mass → **no mass–f_d proportionality** (f_d is a materials+speed constant at fixed v). Comparing the two speed series: the slope at 15 m/s is roughly twice the slope at 5 m/s while 15 is three times 5 → not proportional → **no v–f_d proportionality either** (but f_d does grow with v).
**Common mistakes:** concluding proportionality between m and f_d from proportionality between F_w and F_N; claiming no dependence at all because it is not exactly proportional.
**Points:** slope = f_d (1), m ∝ F_N (1), the 15-vs-5 comparison (1), conclusions (1).

## Opgave B — Cappuccino (Q5–Q8)

**Subdomains:** [P06](../P06-electric-circuits/) (circuits, power, resistivity), [P04](../P04-energy-and-interaction/) (efficiency). **BINAS:** ρ of nichrome (1.10·10⁻⁶ Ω·m).

### Q5 (2p) — wiring in the steam button
**Solution:** with the steam button ON, the water thermostat must be bypassed so the 120 °C thermostat governs; with it OFF the water thermostat rules: draw one wire from the steam button to the line *between* main switch and water thermostat, and one from the button to the line *between* water and steam thermostat — the button then bridges over the water-only branch exactly as the boiler design intends.
**Common mistakes:** shorting out the whole heater (draws the supply straight through); leaving the water thermostat in series permanently (then it never reaches 120 °C).
**Points:** wire 1 (1), wire 2 (1).

### Q6 (1p) — where the safety thermostat sits
**Solution:** the safety thermostat must cut the heater element *only*: place it **in series with the heating element** in its branch (so the lamp, on its own branch, can still report power while the element is dead).
**Common mistakes:** placing it in series with the lamp (warning light dies with it) or in parallel.
**Points:** element branch (1).

### Q7 (3p) — show the element is 63 Ω
**Solution:** useful 0.80 kW at η = 0.95 → drawn P_in = 0.80·10³/0.95 = 842 W. P = U²/R → R = U²/P = 230²/842 = **63 Ω**. ∎
**Common mistakes:** applying η upside down (×0.95 instead of ÷); computing I via 0.80 kW directly (that is the *useful* power, and the R follows from the *drawn* power).
**Points:** η use (1), P = U²/R insight (1), completion (1).

### Q8 (4p) — Silvia (solid nichrome bar) or Mara (slim wire in a tube)?
**Solution:** measured/derived: R = 63 Ω over a 0.60 m spiral length in nichrome (ρ = 1.10·10⁻⁶ Ω·m): A = ρ·ℓ/R = 1.10·10⁻⁶·0.60/63 = 1.05·10⁻⁸ m² → r = √(A/π) = 5.8·10⁻⁵ m → the conductor diameter would be **1.2·10⁻⁴ m ≈ 0.1 mm** — far slimmer than the visibly solid heating bar. So the real conductor must be a thin wire *inside* a protective tube: **Mara is right**.
**Common mistakes:** stopping at A (scored 3/4 — say who is right!); using the rod's visible diameter for ℓ; ρ of constantan or copper from the wrong BINAS row.
**Points:** ρ relation from BINAS (1), A = πr² with d = 2r (1), completion (1), conclusion (1).

## Opgave C — Poollicht (Q9–Q15)

**Subdomains:** [P05](../P05-gravitation/) (escape velocity), [P07](../P07-electric-and-magnetic-fields/) (Lorentz circles), [P10](../P10-electromagnetic-radiation-and-stars/) (photon colour), [P02](../P02-medical-imaging-and-radiation/)-style density reasoning. **BINAS:** G, M_sun, R_sun, h, c, e, colour table.

### Q9 (4p) — minimum speed to escape the Sun
**Solution:** energy balance to "infinity at rest": E_k + E_g = 0 at launch: ½m·v² = G·m·M/R with M = M_sun (1.988·10³⁰ kg) and r = R_sun (6.963·10⁸ m) from BINAS: v = √(2GM/R) = √(2·6.674·10⁻¹¹·1.988·10³⁰/6.963·10⁸) = **6.174·10⁵ m/s** (four significant figures as asked; with other printings of M_sun you may land on the neighbouring digit — fine).
**Common mistakes:** forgetting the factor 2 (a square-root slip = off by √2); using the Earth–Sun distance instead of the Sun's radius as r.
**Points:** E_k + E_g = 0 (1), both formulas (1), BINAS constants (1), completion + significance (1).

### Q10 (2p) — average plasma speed over the trip
**Solution:** v_gem = Δx/Δt = 1.5·10¹¹/1.7 = 8.8·10⁹ m/h = **8.8·10⁶ km/h** (2.5·10⁶ m/s) — the big picture: escaping stuff decelerates from the Q9 value to roughly this cruise speed.
**Common mistakes:** km vs m unit mixing (the question wants km/h; give both to be safe).
**Points:** v_gem = Δx/Δt (1), completion (1).

### Q11 (1p) — where the Earth's magnetic "N" sits in the bar drawing
**Solution:** compass needles point north with their own north ends into the geographic north — opposite poles attract — so the pole *near geographic north* is magnetically a **south** pole; the Earth's bar-magnet north end belongs to the geographic **south** side. Mark N at the bottom of the drawing's Earth.
**Common mistakes:** echoing "geographic north = magnetic north" literally (it is the opposite pole of the magnet picture!).
**Points:** the N placed at the geographic-south end (1).

### Q12 (3p) — Lorentz force direction on a spiralling particle
**Solution:** the circling part of the motion needs a centre-seeking force: F_L points to the loop's middle. With B and the drawn perpendicular velocity component, a hand rule (working with the + direction, then flipping) shows the current direction is opposite to the velocity — **the particle is negative**.
**Common mistakes:** applying the left-hand rule but forgetting the sign flip for a negative carrier; drawing F_L tangentially instead of centripetally.
**Points:** F_L direction (1), I direction with the hand rule (1), conclusion (1).

### Q13 (2p) — why the circle shrinks poleward
**Solution:** field lines converge towards the poles, so B grows there. The Lorentz force F_L = Bqv grows with B, and with r = mv/(qB) the same speed bends tighter: circle radius shrinks near the pole (the particles mirror-bounce poleward — the aurora's address).
**Common mistakes:** attributing the shrinking to energy loss (the Lorentz force does no work!); vague "the force is different" without B or the formula.
**Points:** B grows (1), F_L/r consequence (1).

### Q14 (4p) — the aurora's dominant colour from 2.22 eV photons
**Solution:** λ = hc/E = 6.626·10⁻³⁴·2.998·10⁸/(2.22·1.602·10⁻¹⁹) = 1.986·10⁻²⁵/3.556·10⁻¹⁹ = 5.58·10⁻⁷ m = **559 nm** — the BINAS colour table puts that on the green–yellow edge, the famous auroral green of the 558 nm oxygen line.
**Common mistakes:** forgetting the eV → J conversion; calling it "blue" out of a mood (read the table!); computing frequency as asked wavelength.
**Points:** E = hc/λ (1), constants (1), eV conversion (1), colour call (1).

### Q15 (3p) — why green low, magenta lower, red high
**Solution:**
- Above 300 km the figure shows N₂ is far rarer than O: almost no O–N₂ collisions, so excited O atoms just radiate their own red — *the dominant process dies for lack of partners*. (Above 300 km: red dominates, and it is thin anyway.)
- Below 100 km the figure flips: N₂ vastly outnumbers O → excited O atoms keep colliding with N₂ and never get to emit red; N₂'s own cascade paints it **magenta**.
**Common mistakes:** inverting the two regimes; not reading the *ratio* O:N₂ in the figure but counting absolute densities only.
**Points:** N₂ rare high (1), collision probability low there (1), magenta low (1).

## Opgave D — Boomwhackers (Q16–Q20)

**Subdomains:** [P01](../P01-oscillations-and-waves/) (standing waves, open-open pipes), syllabus-A skills (significant figures, error bars, coordinate transformation). **BINAS:** speed of sound at 20 °C (343 m/s).

### Q16 (4p) — acoustic length of tube 1
**Solution:** the spectrum (fig 2) marks the fundamental at ≈ 2.7·10² Hz (margin 0.2·10²). With v = 343 m/s at 20 °C: λ = v/f = 343/2.7·10² = 1.27 m. An open–open tube's acoustic length is half a wavelength: L_a = λ/2 = **0.64 m** (2 significant figures).
**Common mistakes:** using ℓ = λ or ℓ = ¼λ (both ends OPEN here!); reading the first overtone as the fundamental; drowning the answer in digits the 2-figure f cannot support.
**Points:** f from the figure (1), v = λf with 343 (1), ℓ = n·½λ (1), completion + significance (1).

### Q17 (2p) — do the antinodes sit inside or outside the tube ends?
**Solution:** formula (1) gives L_a = L + 2·0.31·d — the acoustic length *exceeds* the physical tube length on both sides: the standing wave's antinodes protrude slightly **outside** the open ends (that is exactly the end correction every organ builder knows).
**Common mistakes:** assuming the correction shrinks length (it adds); connecting it to one end only (factor 2 × 0.31·d for two ends).
**Points:** L_a > L (1), conclusion (1).

### Q18 (3p) — does a better caliper really sharpen the wavelength?
**Solution:** no. Two independent blocks: (a) the tube's own length L is measured to the mm — an *addition* keeps the weakest decimals of both terms, so the correction term's gain cannot rescue the sum; (b) the experimental factor 0.31 is only known to 2 significant figures, so the correction term 2·0.31·d stays capped there too. Either way the acoustic length's precision is set by its bottleneck, not by the caliper: **Jelle is wrong**.
**Common mistakes:** reasoning "more precise measurement → more precise everything"; ignoring the 0.31 factor's own 2-figure limit.
**Points:** addition rule or multiplication rule correctly invoked (1), the bottleneck identified (1), conclusion (1).

### Q19 (2p) — why the wavelength error bars are invisible
**Solution:** length and diameter are measured to ~1 mm, so the computed wavelength's error is of that same order — at the drawn graph scale (hundreds of mm per box) a ±1 mm whisker is smaller than the plotted point itself: physically invisible on this graph.
**Common mistakes:** confusing the *frequency* error bars (drawn, 20 Hz) with the *wavelength* error; claiming no error exists.
**Points:** size of the error (1), compared against the grid/point (1).

### Q20 (4p) — coordinate transformation and the two limit lines
**Solution:** with v = λ·f, f = v·(1/λ): label the horizontal axis **1/λ (m⁻¹)**. The slope Δf/Δ(1/λ) is then the sound speed. Draw the two straightest lines through the origin that just stay inside all error bars: v_min = 593/1.80 = 3.3·10² m/s, v_max = 600/1.72 = 3.5·10² m/s (2 figures). A beautiful honest uncertainty band instead of a fake point estimate.
**Common mistakes:** axes labelled λ on the x side (that curves); slopes measured from a single best-fit line, ignoring the error bars; forgetting to pass through the origin (v = λf has no intercept).
**Points:** axis label + unit (1), both extreme lines inside all bars (1), slope = v insight (1), both determinations + significance (1).

## Opgave E — Ramsauer en Townsend (Q21–Q25)

**Subdomains:** [P11](../P11-quantum-world/) (de Broglie, energy well, resonance), [P02](../P02-medical-imaging-and-radiation/)-family thinking (half-value thickness). **BINAS:** h, m_e, e.

### Q21 (2p) — why the beam fades with distance
**Solution:** in the hard-sphere picture the standing gas atoms knock electrons *out of the beam direction* at each collision — a scattered electron no longer counts in the forward intensity. The longer the path through the gas, the more collisions happen, so fewer electrons remain on course: intensity falls with distance.
**Common mistakes:** claiming energy loss (the model's collisions are elastic: electrons stay fast but change direction); reasoning macroscopically instead of via the collision model as asked.
**Points:** collision → out of beam (1), more path → more collisions (1).

### Q22 (3p) — d½ from the graph and checking λ
**Solution:**
- Mark on the (T,x) graph the x where T = 0.5: that is the half-value thickness d½ (≈ λ·ln 2 in this exponential world).
- Check the printed λ: at x = λ the formula gives T = e⁻¹ = 0.368; read that point on the graph: (λ, 0.368) lands exactly on the curve ✓ — the printed λ is correct.
**Common mistakes:** reading d½ at T = λ's own height instead of T = 0.5; computing T at x = d½ instead of x = λ for the check.
**Points:** d½ marked (1), x = λ substituted (1), T computed + graph comparison (1).

### Q23 (3p) — de Broglie wavelength of a 1.0 eV electron
**Solution:** p = √(2m·E_k): λ = h/p = 6.626·10⁻³⁴/√(2·9.109·10⁻³¹·1.0·1.602·10⁻¹⁹) = 6.626·10⁻³⁴/√(2.916·10⁻⁴⁹) = 6.626·10⁻³⁴/5.400·10⁻²⁵ = 1.2·10⁻⁹ m = **1.2 nm** — about four times the xenon atomic diameter, exactly the right scale for atom-scale diffraction.
**Common mistakes:** λ = h/(mv) with v forgotten (square-root of E!); J ↔ eV slip (the 1.0 eV must convert to 1.6·10⁻¹⁹ J).
**Points:** λ = h/p (1), p = √(2mE) link (1), completion (1).

### Q24 (4p) — why there are several resonant electron energies
**Solution:** the chain: resonance condition (2) says L = n·λ_II/2, so *several* wavelengths λ_II = 2L/n resonate (n = 1, 2, 3, …). Each λ fixes a momentum via de Broglie (p = h/λ), and each momentum a kinetic energy in the atom region (E_kin = p²/2m). Formula (3) then shifts the scale: E_elek = E_kin − E_put. So a *series* of input energies E_elek each meets its own n — several resonant peaks in the (T, E) graph, exactly what Ramsauer and Townsend observed.
**Common mistakes:** stopping at "waves resonate in atoms" without the n-series; dropping the E_put shift (resonances sit at E_kin, not at E_elek!).
**Points:** λ-series from (2) (1), de Broglie link (1), momentum→E_kin (1), shift via (3) (1).

### Q25 (4p) — the well depth E_put in eV
**Solution:** the first T = 1 peak sits at E_elek = 1.0 eV and the paper assigns it n = 1. The electron's kinetic energy inside the atom region is then the box's ground energy with L equal to the atom's 0.22 nm diameter:

E_kin = E₁ = h²/(8·m·L²) = (6.626·10⁻³⁴)²/(8·9.109·10⁻³¹·(2.2·10⁻¹⁰)²) = 1.25·10⁻¹⁸ J = 7.8 eV.

With E_kin = E_elek + E_put: E_put = 7.8 − 1.0 = **6.8 eV**. (Deeper wells would need faster electrons to reach the same standing wave — the same energy bookkeeping as every squarish well in [P11](../P11-quantum-world/).)
**Common mistakes:** using the atom's *radius* for L; forgetting that it is n = 1, not the n of the graph's later peaks; significance dropped (exactly 3 significant figures expected: 6.78/6.8 by rounding of the data — the question warns).
**Points:** box formula with n = 1 (1), E_elek = 1.0 eV taken (1), completion (1), significance (1).
