## Exam rules (as in the real sitting)

- **Time: 3 hours (180 minutes).** Utrecht-style written exam, open questions; partial credit for correct steps — always show your working: formula, substitution, result with unit.
- You may use **BINAS** (5th edition or later, English edition allowed) **with no notes in it**, one simple scientific calculator (no graphing calculator), pen and pencil (pencil only for graphs), a set square and a watch (no smartwatch).
- Constants not in the question should be taken from BINAS. Use **g = 9.81 m/s²**, unless stated otherwise. Give answers with a sensible number of significant figures and always with a unit.
- This exam has **16 questions**, total **72 points**. Your grade now = 1 + 9 × (score/72). Time guide: about 2½ minutes per point.
- The [marking scheme](mock-exam-1-marking-scheme.md) gives the points per step. Check your work only after your full attempt.

### phys-mock1-q01 | points 3 | outcome P1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A block (m = 0.50 kg) hangs from a spring (C = 2.0·10² N/m) and oscillates harmonically with amplitude 3.0 cm.

a. Compute the period.
b. Compute the maximum speed of the block.

#### Answer

a. T = 0.31 s; b. v_max = 0.60 m/s

#### Solution

a. T = 2π√(m/C) = 2π√(0.50/2.0·10²) = 2π·√2.5·10⁻³ = 0.314 ≈ 0.31 s.
b. v_max = 2πA/T = 2π·0.030/0.314 = 0.6014 ≈ 0.60 m/s.

### phys-mock1-q02 | points 4 | outcome P1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A church bell rings at 440 Hz. A cyclist rides straight towards it at 12 m/s through still air (speed of sound 343 m/s).

a. Explain, with the arrival of wavefronts, why she hears a pitch higher than 440 Hz even though the wavefront spacing in the air is unchanged.
b. Compute the wavelength of the sound in the still air.
c. Compute the frequency she hears, by counting how many wavefronts she meets per second.

#### Answer

a. the spacing stays the same, but she rides into the fronts, so they reach her more often; b. λ = 0.780 m; c. f′ ≈ 4.6·10² Hz (455 Hz)

#### Solution

a. The source stands still, so the soundspeed and frequency set an unchanged spacing λ in the air. The listener moves towards the source and therefore meets successive wavefronts sooner in time: more fronts per second reach her ear → a higher heard frequency (the Doppler effect, new in the 2025 syllabus).
b. λ = v_sound/f = 343/440 = 0.780 m.
c. Closing speed through the wavefront field: 343 + 12 = 355 m/s; fronts met per second: f′ = 355/0.780 = 4.6·10² Hz (455 Hz).

### phys-mock1-q03 | points 4 | outcome P3 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A motorcycle's ride is recorded: uniform acceleration from 0 to 15 m/s in 5.0 s, then 10 s at 15 m/s, then a uniform deceleration to 6.0 m/s in 2.5 s, then 5.0 s at 6.0 m/s.

a. Compute the distance of each phase and the total distance.
b. Compute the average velocity over the whole ride.

#### Answer

a. 37.5 m, 150 m, 26.25 m, 30 m; total 2.44·10² m; b. v_gem = 10.8 m/s

#### Solution

a. Areas under the (v,t) graph: ½·5.0·15 = 37.5 m; 10·15 = 150 m; ½·(15 + 6.0)·2.5 = 26.25 m; 5.0·6.0 = 30 m. Total 243.75 ≈ 2.44·10² m.
b. v_gem = 243.75/22.5 = 10.8 m/s.

### phys-mock1-q04 | points 5 | outcome P3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A sledge (m = 12 kg) on snow is pulled by a rope with F = 6.0·10¹ N at 25° above the horizontal.

a. Compute the horizontal and the vertical component of this pull.
b. Compute the normal force of the snow on the sledge.
c. The friction force is 30 N. Compute the acceleration of the sledge.
d. The sledge starts from rest. Compute its speed after 4.0 s.

#### Answer

a. 54 N and 25 N; b. F_N = 92 N; c. a = 2.0 m/s²; d. v = 8.1 m/s

#### Solution

a. Horizontal: F·cos 25° = 6.0·10¹·0.906 = 54 N (54.4 N); vertical: F·sin 25° = 25 N (25.4 N).
b. F_N = F_z − F_vertical = m·g − 25.4 = 12·9.81 − 25.4 = 92 N (the pull lifts the sledge a little).
c. F_res = 54.4 − 30 = 24.4 N → a = F_res/m = 24.4/12 = 2.03 ≈ 2.0 m/s².
d. v = a·t = 2.03·4.0 = 8.1 m/s.

### phys-mock1-q05 | points 4 | outcome P4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A fireworks ball is launched straight up at 25 m/s. Neglect air resistance.

a. Compute the maximum height above the launch point.
b. Compute its speed when it passes 12 m above the launch point.
c. Compute the total time until it is back at launch height.

#### Answer

a. h = 32 m (31.9 m); b. v = 2.0·10¹ m/s; c. t = 5.1 s

#### Solution

a. ½mv² = mgh → h = v²/(2g) = 625/19.62 = 31.9 ≈ 32 m.
b. Energy: ½v₀² = ½v² + g·Δh → v² = 625 − 2·9.81·12 = 390 → v = 19.7 ≈ 2.0·10¹ m/s (same up and down at that height).
c. Up and down are symmetric: t = 2·v₀/g = 2·25/9.81 = 5.1 s.

### phys-mock1-q06 | points 4 | outcome P6 | difficulty medium

#### Question

An electric kettle (2.0 kW) runs for 3.0 minutes to heat 0.80 kg of water from 12 °C to boiling (100 °C; c_water = 4.18·10³ J/(kg·K)).

a. Compute the electric energy taken in.
b. Compute the heat the water needed.
c. Compute the efficiency of the kettle.

#### Answer

a. E = 3.6·10⁵ J; b. Q = 2.9·10⁵ J; c. η = 82%

#### Solution

a. E = P·t = 2.0·10³·180 = 3.6·10⁵ J.
b. Q = m·c·ΔT = 0.80·4.18·10³·88 = 2.94·10⁵ ≈ 2.9·10⁵ J.
c. η = E_nuttig/E_in = 2.94·10⁵/3.6·10⁵ = 0.817 ≈ 82%.

### phys-mock1-q07 | points 5 | outcome P6 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A 12 V battery feeds a bulb A (6.0 Ω) in series with a parallel pair of bulbs B (18 Ω) and C (36 Ω).

a. Compute the replacement resistance of the parallel pair. (Use the rule as printed in the current syllabus: 1/R_tot = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …)
b. Compute the total current delivered by the battery.
c. Compute the voltage across bulb A and across the parallel pair.
d. Which bulb burns brightest? Justify your answer with power computations.

#### Answer

a. R_pair = 12 Ω; b. I = 0.67 A; c. U_A = 4.0 V, U_pair = 8.0 V; d. bulb B

#### Solution

a. 1/R_pair = 1/18 + 1/36 = 3/36 = 1/12 → R_pair = 12 Ω (conductance route: G = 0.0556 + 0.0278 = 0.0833 S → 12 Ω, same).
b. R_tot = 6.0 + 12 = 18 Ω → I = 12/18 = 0.667 ≈ 0.67 A.
c. U_A = I·R_A = 0.667·6.0 = 4.0 V; U_pair = 12 − 4.0 = 8.0 V (check: I·R_pair = 0.667·12 = 8.0 ✓).
d. Branch currents: I_B = 8.0/18 = 0.44 A, I_C = 8.0/36 = 0.22 A. Powers: P_A = U_A·I = 4.0·0.667 = 2.7 W; P_B = 8.0·0.44 = 3.6 W; P_C = 8.0·0.22 = 1.8 W. Bulb B converts the most power → burns brightest.

### phys-mock1-q08 | points 5 | outcome P5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

You may *weigh the Earth* with the Moon. The Moon circles the Earth at mean distance r = 3.844·10⁸ m with orbital speed v = 1.02·10³ m/s (G = 6.674·10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², BINAS).

a. Derive, from the requirement F_g = F_mpz, the Earth's mass in terms of v, r and G, and compute it in kg.
b. Compute the Moon's orbital period in days, and comment on the known lunar cycle of about 27⅓ days.
c. Explain why the Moon's own mass does not enter this computation.

#### Answer

a. M = v²·r/G = 6.0·10²⁴ kg; b. T ≈ 27.4 d (matching the ~27⅓-day sidereal lunar period); c. m cancels on both sides of F_g = F_mpz

#### Solution

a. Gravity provides the centripetal force: G·M·m/r² = m·v²/r → M = v²·r/G = (1.02·10³)²·3.844·10⁸/6.674·10⁻¹¹ = 4.0·10¹⁴/6.674·10⁻¹¹ = 6.0·10²⁴ kg.
b. T = 2πr/v = 2π·3.844·10⁸/1.02·10³ = 2.368·10⁶ s = 2.368·10⁶/8.64·10⁴ = 27.4 d — matches the sidereal month (~27.3 d) beautifully: the orbit really does clock the Moon.
c. Both F_g = G·M·m/r² and F_mpz = m·v²/r are proportional to m, so the Moon's mass divides out of the requirement — any test mass at this r with this v orbits identically. Only M_Earth matters.

### phys-mock1-q09 | points 5 | outcome P2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

For a kidney scintigram at 15:00 a technetium dose of Tc-99m (t½ = 6.0 h) with activity 2.0·10⁸ Bq is needed. (1 h = 3.6·10³ s.)

a. Tc-99m is an excited, metastable nucleus (⁹⁹ᵐ₄₃Tc). Give its decay equation to Tc-99, and name the only kind of radiation produced in this step.
b. Compute the activity the lab must prepare at 03:00 the night before.
c. Compute the number of Tc-99m nuclei in that prepared amount (use A = (ln 2/t½)·N).

#### Answer

a. ⁹⁹ᵐ₄₃Tc → ⁹⁹₄₃Tc + ⁰₀γ, only gamma; b. A = 8.0·10⁸ Bq; c. N = 2.5·10¹³ nuclei

#### Solution

a. Isomeric transition: the excited nucleus only sheds energy; A and Z unchanged: ⁹⁹ᵐ₄₃Tc → ⁹⁹₄₃Tc + ⁰₀γ. Pure gamma, no particle.
b. From 03:00 to 15:00 is 12 h = 2 half-lives → A_prepare = A_needed·2² = 2.0·10⁸·4 = 8.0·10⁸ Bq.
c. t½ = 6.0·3.6·10³ = 2.16·10⁴ s; N = A·t½/ln 2 = 8.0·10⁸·2.16·10⁴/0.693 = 2.5·10¹³ nuclei.

### phys-mock1-q10 | points 4 | outcome P10 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In a star's core, fusing hydrogen into helium converts about 0.71% of the hydrogen's mass into radiation (given).

a. Compute with E = Δm·c² (c = 3.00·10⁸ m/s) the energy released when 1.0 kg of hydrogen is completely fused.
b. Petrol has a calorific value r_m = 44 MJ/kg (given). Compute how many kilograms of petrol release the same energy as 1.0 kg of hydrogen fuel.
c. Give, with your factor from (b), a one-line conclusion on why fusion research keeps trying.

#### Answer

a. E = 6.4·10¹⁴ J; b. m_petrol ≈ 1.5·10⁷ kg (≈ 15 kilotonnes); c. per kg of fuel, fusion liberates ~10⁷ times more energy than chemistry

#### Solution

a. Vanished mass: Δm = 0.0071·1.0 = 7.1·10⁻³ kg. E = Δm·c² = 7.1·10⁻³·(3.00·10⁸)² = 6.39·10¹⁴ ≈ 6.4·10¹⁴ J.
b. m_petrol = E/r_m = 6.39·10¹⁴/4.4·10⁷ = 1.45·10⁷ ≈ 1.5·10⁷ kg — about 15 000 tonnes of petrol against one kilogram of hydrogen.
c. The mass–energy conversion E = mc² (explicit on the syllabus since 2025) makes fusion ~1.5·10⁷ times denser per kilogram than chemical fuels — that ratio is the whole point of chasing it.

### phys-mock1-q11 | points 5 | outcome P8 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A sauna stove heats its stones (m = 40 kg, c = 0.90·10³ J/(kg·K), given) from 20 °C to 80 °C with a 6.0 kW electric element. The cabin has a wooden wall (λ = 0.13 W/(m·K), given) of A = 20 m² and d = 0.10 m; the temperature difference across that wall in operation is 60 K.

a. Compute the heat needed for the stones.
b. Compute the shortest possible heating time of the stones (no losses).
c. Compute the heat current through that wall in operation, and comment in one sentence on what this means for the stove's cycling.

#### Answer

a. Q = 2.2·10⁶ J (2.16·10⁶ J); b. t = 6.0 min; c. P_wall = 1.6·10³ W — the stove must keep replacing that ~1.6 kW continuously, so it cycles in and out rather than switching off

#### Solution

a. Q = m·c·ΔT = 40·0.90·10³·60 = 2.16·10⁶ ≈ 2.2·10⁶ J.
b. Ideal: t = Q/P = 2.16·10⁶/6.0·10³ = 360 s = 6.0 min (losses make it longer in reality).
c. P = λ·A·ΔT/d = 0.13·20·60/0.10 = 1.56·10³ ≈ 1.6·10³ W. That continuous leak is about a quarter of the element's power, so the thermostat cycles the stove to keep topping it up — insulation quality decides the electricity bill (see [P08](P08-matter-heat-and-gases/)).

### phys-mock1-q12 | points 4 | outcome P8 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A sealed camping-gas canister is rated "do not expose above 50 °C". At 7.0 °C the gas is at 6.0·10⁵ Pa (absolute, given). Treat the volume as constant and the gas as ideal.

a. Compute the pressure if the canister reaches 57 °C in a car in the sun.
b. Explain with the kinetic molecular model why the pressure rises with temperature (and why the warning exists).

#### Answer

a. p = 7.1·10⁵ Pa; b. faster molecules hit the walls harder and more often; p ∝ T in kelvin — the rating guards the can against a burst

#### Solution

a. Constant V and n: p/T = constant → p₂ = p₁·T₂/T₁ with kelvin first: 280 K and 330 K → p₂ = 6.0·10⁵·330/280 = 7.07·10⁵ ≈ 7.1·10⁵ Pa.
b. Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the molecules; heating speeds them up, so wall collisions arrive more often and harder — p rises proportionally with the absolute temperature. Past the rating, that rise plus metal fatigue is a bursting can: hence the warning.

### phys-mock1-q13 | points 5 | outcome P9 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A light ray goes from air into a glass plate (n = 1.55) at an angle of incidence of 58°.

a. Compute the angle of refraction inside the glass.
b. Compute the critical angle of this glass with respect to air.
c. A second ray travels *inside* the same glass and meets the glass–air boundary at 50° to the normal. Describe and justify what happens.

#### Answer

a. r = 33°; b. g = 40° (40.2°); c. total internal reflection: 50° > g, so the ray bounces back into the glass instead of exiting

#### Solution

a. sin i/sin r = n → sin r = sin 58°/1.55 = 0.848/1.55 = 0.547 → r = 33°.
b. sin g = 1/n = 1/1.55 = 0.645 → g = 40.2 ≈ 40°.
c. 50° > 40.2°, beyond the critical angle: no refraction is possible (sin r would need to exceed 1), so the ray undergoes total internal reflection at the boundary — the glass acts as a perfect mirror there.

### phys-mock1-q14 | points 5 | outcome P10 | difficulty hard

#### Question

The star of question type "red giant" Z has its Planck peak at λ_max = 8.3·10⁻⁷ m and radiates a total power of 110 P_sun (Sun: T = 5.8·10³ K, P_sun = 3.85·10²⁶ W).

a. Compute the surface temperature of Z (k_W = 2.898·10⁻³ m·K).
b. Using P = σ·A·T⁴, compute the ratio A_Z/A_Sun of the surface areas, and from it the ratio of the radii R_Z/R_Sun.

#### Answer

a. T = 3.5·10³ K; b. A_Z/A_Sun ≈ 8.4·10², R_Z/R_Sun ≈ 29

#### Solution

a. Wien: T = k_W/λ_max = 2.898·10⁻³/8.3·10⁻⁷ = 3.49·10³ ≈ 3.5·10³ K.
b. Equal σ: A ∝ P/T⁴ → A_Z/A_Sun = (P_Z/P_Sun)·(T_Sun/T_Z)⁴ = 110·(5800/3492)⁴ = 110·7.61 = 8.4·10² ≈ 8.4·10². R ratio = √8.4·10² = 28.9 ≈ 29 — a cool star can only dump 110 solar powers by being enormous (the red-giant identity).

### phys-mock1-q15 | points 5 | outcome P11 | difficulty hard

#### Question

A proton (m = 1.673·10⁻²⁷ kg) is confined to a nuclear-sized, infinitely deep one-dimensional box of width L = 5.0·10⁻¹⁵ m.

a. Compute the ground-state energy in J and in MeV (h = 6.626·10⁻³⁴ J·s, e = 1.602·10⁻¹⁹ C).
b. The proton drops from level n = 2 to the ground state. Compute the photon energy and wavelength, and name the radiation kind.
c. An electron in a nano-box (L = 1.0·10⁻⁹ m) has level spacings of about 1 eV. Explain with the box formula why the nuclear well's spacings dwarf those by about seven orders of magnitude.

#### Answer

a. E₁ = 1.31·10⁻¹² J = 8.2 MeV; b. ΔE = 24.6 MeV, λ = 5.0·10⁻¹⁴ m, gamma; c. E ∝ 1/(m·L²): the proton is heavier (÷1836) but the box is 2·10⁵ × smaller — squared, that is ×4·10¹⁰, so ≈ 2·10⁷ times larger spacings

#### Solution

a. E₁ = h²/(8·m·L²) = (6.626·10⁻³⁴)²/(8·1.673·10⁻²⁷·(5.0·10⁻¹⁵)²) = 4.39·10⁻⁶⁷/3.35·10⁻⁵⁵ = 1.31·10⁻¹² J = 1.31·10⁻¹²/1.602·10⁻¹⁹ = 8.19·10⁶ eV ≈ 8.2 MeV.
b. E₂ = 4E₁ → ΔE = 3E₁ = 3.94·10⁻¹² J = 24.6 MeV; λ = hc/ΔE = 6.626·10⁻³⁴·3.00·10⁸/3.94·10⁻¹² = 5.0·10⁻¹⁴ m: gamma radiation.
c. Box energies scale as 1/(m·L²). Electron box → nuclear box: m ×1836 pulls the spacing down by ~2·10³; L: (1.0·10⁻⁹)/(5.0·10⁻¹⁵) = 2·10⁵, squared = 4·10¹⁰ upwards. Net ≈ 2·10⁷ — that is exactly why nuclear transitions emit MeV photons where nano-boxes emit infrared.

### phys-mock1-q16 | points 5 | outcome P7 | difficulty hard

#### Question

In a mass spectrometer a proton (m = 1.673·10⁻²⁷ kg, charge +e) enters a homogeneous field B = 1.5 T perpendicularly at v = 3.0·10⁶ m/s.

a. Compute the Lorentz force on the proton.
b. Compute the radius of its circular path.
c. Derive that the time for half a circle, T½ = π·m/(q·B), is independent of the speed, and name why that is useful in a spectrometer.

#### Answer

a. F_L = 7.2·10⁻¹³ N; b. r = 2.1·10⁻² m; c. T½ = πm/(qB) ≈ 2.2·10⁻⁸ s, independent of v — every charge with the same m/q crosses in the same time, a clean timing window

#### Solution

a. F_L = B·q·v = 1.5·1.602·10⁻¹⁹·3.0·10⁶ = 7.209·10⁻¹³ ≈ 7.2·10⁻¹³ N.
b. The Lorentz force plays centripetal force: B·q·v = m·v²/r → r = m·v/(q·B) = 1.673·10⁻²⁷·3.0·10⁶/(1.602·10⁻¹⁹·1.5) = 2.09·10⁻² ≈ 2.1·10⁻² m.
c. T½ = πr/v = π·(m·v/(q·B))/v = π·m/(q·B): the speed cancels (radius grows exactly with v), so the half-circle time is set by m/q and B alone — ≈ 2.2·10⁻⁸ s here. In a spectrometer that means all particles with one m/q arrive together: time gates separate the isotopes.
