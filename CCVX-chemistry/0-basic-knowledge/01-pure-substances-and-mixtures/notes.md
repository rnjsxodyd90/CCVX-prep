# sb1–sb5 — Pure Substances and Mixtures: notes

These are English explanations of every learning outcome in [README.md](README.md), and every topic is *drafted*: written from the CCVX and CvTE syllabi and standard VWO chemistry, not yet checked against an open source text or exam question (see [PLAN.md](../../PLAN.md) for the statuses).

Dutch terms are in brackets. A code such as *(CvTE M1.4)* points to the matching specification in the [CvTE 2027 syllabus](../../syllabus/cvte-syllabus-scheikunde-vwo-2027.pdf); Binas tables are named by subject, because their numbers differ between editions.

## sb1 Pure substances and mixtures

- A pure substance (*zuivere stof*) consists of one substance only. At micro level it contains one kind of particle: only H₂O molecules in pure water, only Cu atoms in pure copper, and in a salt such as sodium chloride only Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in a fixed ratio. *(CvTE M2.1)*
- A pure substance has fixed substance properties (*stofeigenschappen*), such as melting point, boiling point, density, solubility, colour and smell. They identify the substance; Binas lists the melting and boiling points of many substances. *(CvTE M2.1)*
- A mixture (*mengsel*) consists of two or more substances, so at micro level of two or more kinds of particles: air is mainly N₂ and O₂ molecules, and salt water contains H₂O molecules, Na⁺ ions and Cl⁻ ions. *(CvTE M2.1)*
- The substances in a mixture keep their own properties and aren't bonded in a fixed ratio, so the composition can vary, the properties of the mixture depend on it, and the mixture can be separated by physical methods (sb5).
- A pure substance can be an element or a compound (sb6 in [02-elements-and-compounds](../02-elements-and-compounds/notes.md)). The micro-level picture is CCVX B2.3 in [B02](../../B-substances-and-materials/B02-properties-and-models/notes.md).
- **Trap:** everyday "pure" means "nothing added" or "clean". Tap water, mineral water, sea water, air, milk and steel are all mixtures in the chemical sense.
- **Trap:** two kinds of atoms in a formula don't make a mixture. Water (H₂O) is one substance, because all its molecules are the same; a mixture of hydrogen gas and oxygen gas (H₂ and O₂ molecules) is a mixture of two substances.

## sb2 Phase transitions

- A substance can be solid, liquid or gas: these are its phases or states of matter (*fasen, aggregatietoestanden*), written with the state symbols (s), (l) and (g) (sb10 in [02-elements-and-compounds](../02-elements-and-compounds/notes.md)). A phase transition (*fase-overgang*) changes the phase, not the substance. *(CvTE M1.3)*

| Phase transition | Dutch | Change | Heat |
|---|---|---|---|
| melting | *smelten* | solid to liquid | absorbed |
| solidification (freezing) | *stollen* | liquid to solid | released |
| evaporation | *verdampen* | liquid to gas | absorbed |
| condensation | *condenseren* | gas to liquid | released |
| sublimation | *sublimeren* | solid directly to gas | absorbed |
| deposition | *rijpen* | gas directly to solid | released |

- The transitions come in opposite pairs, and each one releases as much heat as its opposite absorbs. A pure substance melts and solidifies at the same temperature, its melting point (*smeltpunt*), which is also its freezing point (*stolpunt*).
- Evaporation happens at any temperature at the surface of a liquid: a puddle dries up. Boiling (*koken*) is evaporation throughout the liquid, with bubbles of vapour, at the boiling point (*kookpunt*). Condensation is the reverse, as when water vapour forms droplets on a cold window.
- Examples with state symbols: ice melting, H₂O(s) → H₂O(l); water evaporating, H₂O(l) → H₂O(g); solid carbon dioxide (dry ice) subliming at normal pressure, CO₂(s) → CO₂(g); hoarfrost (*rijp*, which gives deposition its Dutch name) forming on a cold night, H₂O(g) → H₂O(s). Iodine crystals give a violet vapour on gentle heating, which deposits as crystals again on a cold surface.
- At micro level the particles stay the same: only the bonds between the particles are broken (melting, evaporation) or formed (solidification, condensation). In a molecular substance these are Van der Waals bonds (molecular bonds, *vanderwaalsbindingen*) and hydrogen bridges (*waterstofbruggen*), never the atomic bonds (covalent bonds, *atoombindingen*) inside the molecules. *(CvTE M3.4)* Which bonds, and how their strength sets the boiling point, is in [B03](../../B-substances-and-materials/B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md) and [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- **Trap:** boiling water doesn't decompose. The bubbles are water vapour, H₂O(g), not hydrogen and oxygen; decomposing water is a chemical reaction, such as electrolysis (sb13 in [04-reactions](../04-reactions/notes.md)).
- **Trap:** deposition is *rijpen* in Dutch. And "condensation" also names a type of reaction, the condensation reaction (*condensatiereactie*) in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md), in which two molecules join while a small molecule such as H₂O splits off; it has nothing to do with the phase transition.

## sb3 Melting point and melting range

- A pure substance melts at one temperature, its melting point (*smeltpunt*), and at a given pressure boils at one temperature, its boiling point (*kookpunt*). While it melts or boils the temperature stays constant although heat is still supplied, because the heat is used to break the bonds between the particles: a heating curve (temperature against time) has a horizontal part. *(CvTE M2.2)*
- A mixture melts over a range of temperatures, its melting range (*smelttraject*), and boils over a boiling range (*kooktraject*). The temperature keeps rising during melting or boiling, because the composition of what is left keeps changing. *(CvTE M2.2)*
- Example: pure water boils at 100 °C at normal atmospheric pressure. Salt water starts to boil above 100 °C, and its boiling temperature keeps rising as water boils off and the solution becomes more concentrated.
- This is how you tell a mixture from a pure substance, and a purity test for a product: compare its measured melting point with the value in Binas. An impure solid usually starts to melt below the melting point of the pure substance and melts over a range. *(CvTE M2.2)*
- Strictly, a few mixtures of one particular composition melt or boil at a single temperature (eutectic mixtures and azeotropes); the syllabus rule "mixture means a range" leaves these out. The relationship is CCVX B2.2 in [B02](../../B-substances-and-materials/B02-properties-and-models/notes.md).
- **Trap:** a horizontal part in a heating curve doesn't mean no heat is being added; the heat goes into the phase transition instead of raising the temperature.

## sb4 Types of mixtures

- Solution (*oplossing*): a substance, the solute (*opgeloste stof*), is spread as separate molecules or ions between the particles of a solvent (*oplosmiddel*). A solution is clear (*helder*), though it may be coloured, and homogeneous (*homogeen*): the same throughout. Nothing settles, and it passes through a filter unchanged. Examples: sugar or salt in water, blue copper(II) sulfate solution, carbon dioxide in sparkling water. *(CvTE M2.3)*
- Dissolving in water is written with (aq): NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) and C₆H₁₂O₆(s) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq). A solution is saturated (*verzadigd*) when no more of the substance dissolves at that temperature, and unsaturated (*onverzadigd*) when more could dissolve. *(CvTE M1.3, M2.3)*
- Suspension (*suspensie*): small particles of a solid spread through a liquid without dissolving. It's cloudy (*troebel*) and heterogeneous (*heterogeen*); on standing the solid settles, and it can be filtered. Examples: chalk powder shaken with water, muddy water, a medicine labelled "shake before use". *(CvTE M2.3)*
- Emulsion (*emulsie*): small droplets of one liquid spread through another liquid that it doesn't mix with, such as oil and water. It's cloudy and heterogeneous, and on standing it separates into two layers unless an emulsifier (*emulgator*) keeps the droplets apart. Examples: milk (fat droplets in water) and mayonnaise (oil droplets in water and vinegar, with egg yolk as the emulsifier); a shaken oil-and-vinegar dressing has no emulsifier and soon separates again. *(CvTE M2.3)*
- An emulsifier's molecules have a hydrophobic part that sits in the oil and a hydrophilic part that sits in the water, at the surface of each droplet; soap works the same way on grease. Hydrophobic and hydrophilic are explained in [B03](../../B-substances-and-materials/B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md).
- Alloy (*legering*): a mixture of metals, sometimes with a little of a non-metal, made by melting the components together and letting the melt solidify. An alloy has metallic properties and is usually harder than the pure metals. Examples: brass (*messing*, copper and zinc), bronze (*brons*, copper and tin), steel (*staal*, iron with a little carbon; stainless steel also contains chromium and nickel). *(CvTE M2.3)* Why alloys are harder is CCVX B4.2 in [B04](../../B-substances-and-materials/B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md). *(CvTE M4.2)*
- CCVX B2.6 uses all these terms, including homogeneous and heterogeneous, in arguments about mixtures; see [B02](../../B-substances-and-materials/B02-properties-and-models/notes.md).
- **Trap:** clear doesn't mean colourless: blue copper(II) sulfate solution is clear. Cloudiness means undissolved particles or droplets, so a suspension or an emulsion.
- **Trap:** a suspension is a solid in a liquid, an emulsion a liquid in a liquid.

## sb5 Separation techniques

- A separation technique (*scheidingsmethode*) splits a mixture into its components, or purifies a substance by removing contaminants (*verontreinigingen*). Each one uses a difference in a property of the substances, and no chemical reaction is needed (except in the precipitation reaction mentioned under settling). *(CvTE R&A2.2)*

| Technique | Dutch | Separates | Based on a difference in |
|---|---|---|---|
| extraction | *extraheren* | a solid mixture or a solution | solubility in the extracting agent |
| adsorption | *adsorberen* | small amounts of substances from a liquid or gas | adhesion to the adsorbent |
| distillation | *destilleren* | a solution or a mixture of liquids | boiling point |
| filtration | *filtreren* | a suspension | particle size |
| centrifuging | *centrifugeren* | a suspension or an emulsion | density |
| precipitation (settling) | *bezinken* | a suspension | density |
| concentration by evaporation | *indampen* | a solution of a solid | boiling point |
| paper chromatography | *papierchromatografie* | small amounts of dissolved substances | solubility in the eluent and adhesion to the paper |

### Extraction

- Extraction (*extraheren*): stir or shake the mixture with a liquid, the extracting agent (*extractiemiddel*), in which the wanted substance dissolves well and the rest doesn't. It's based on a difference in solubility (*oplosbaarheid*) in the extracting agent. *(CvTE R&A2.1, R&A2.2)*
- From a solid mixture: making tea or coffee, where hot water extracts colour and flavour substances and caffeine from the leaves or ground beans. From a solution: shaking a solution of iodine in water with hexane, an apolar solvent that doesn't mix with water, moves most of the iodine into the hexane layer, because iodine dissolves much better in apolar solvents. A separating funnel (*scheitrechter*) then separates the two layers.
- The CvTE pairs extraction with washing (*extraheren/wassen*): the same principle used to remove an unwanted soluble substance, such as rinsing a solid on a filter with water, or bubbling a gas through a liquid that dissolves an impurity. *(CvTE R&A2.1)*
- A good extracting agent dissolves the wanted substance well and the rest poorly, and for an extraction from a liquid it doesn't mix with that liquid. Why a substance dissolves in a given solvent (hydrophilic, hydrophobic) is in [B03](../../B-substances-and-materials/B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md).

### Adsorption

- Adsorption (*adsorberen*): particles of some substances stick to the surface of a solid, the adsorbent (*adsorptiemiddel*), usually activated carbon (*actieve kool*), which has an enormous internal surface. It's based on a difference in how strongly substances stick to that surface (adhesion, *hechting*). *(CvTE R&A2.1, M3.8)*
- It removes small amounts of substances, often coloured or smelly ones, from a liquid or gas: decolourising a solution, purifying drinking water, the filter of a cooker hood. The adsorbent is then removed, for example by filtration. Which bonds form during adsorption is CCVX C1.5 in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- **Trap:** adsorption (sticking to a surface, *adsorberen*) isn't absorption (being taken up throughout a material, *absorberen*, as water is by a sponge).

### Distillation

- Distillation (*destilleren*): heat a liquid mixture until it boils, and cool the vapour in a condenser (*koeler*) so that it condenses. The liquid collected is the distillate (*destillaat*); what stays behind in the flask is the residue (*residu*). It's based on a difference in boiling point (*kookpunt*). *(CvTE R&A2.1, R&A2.2)*
- It separates a solution of a solid in a liquid (from salt water, the distillate is pure water and the residue is the salt) or a mixture of liquids with different boiling points (ethanol from a fermented mixture). Crude oil is split into fractions with different boiling ranges by fractional distillation.
- A thermometer at the side arm measures the temperature of the vapour that distils over; while a pure substance distils, it stays at that substance's boiling point. Which bonds are broken (between the molecules, not inside them) is CCVX C1.5 in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- **Trap:** when the boiling points are close together, one distillation doesn't give a pure distillate, only a mixture richer in the component with the lower boiling point.
- **Trap:** "residue" is what stays in the flask in distillation, but what stays on the filter in filtration.

### Filtration

- Filtration (*filtreren*): pour the mixture through a filter, such as filter paper (*filtreerpapier*) in a funnel. Undissolved solid particles are too big for the pores and stay on the filter as the residue (*residu*); the liquid, with everything dissolved in it, passes through as the filtrate (*filtraat*). It's based on a difference in particle size (*deeltjesgrootte*): dissolved molecules and ions are far smaller than undissolved grains. *(CvTE R&A2.1, R&A2.2)*
- It separates a suspension, or solid particles from a gas (an air filter, a vacuum cleaner). Filtering sand from salt water leaves the sand as the residue and gives a clear salt solution as the filtrate.
- **Trap:** filtration can't separate a solution. Dissolved particles pass through the filter, so a clear filtrate can still contain dissolved substances.

### Centrifuging

- Centrifuging (*centrifugeren*): spin the mixture fast in a centrifuge, which pushes the denser component to the bottom of the tube much faster than settling under gravity would; then pour or pipette off the liquid. It's based on a difference in density (*dichtheid*). *(CvTE R&A2.1, R&A2.2)*
- It separates suspensions whose particles are too fine to settle quickly or would clog a filter, and emulsions: blood into blood cells and plasma, cream from milk.

### Precipitation (settling)

- CCVX's "precipitation" here most likely translates *bezinken*, settling. According to the omnummeringstabel, the old Dutch D1.1 had seven separation methods (*adsorberen, bezinken, centrifugeren, destilleren, extraheren/wassen, filtreren, indampen*); CCVX D1.1 lists seven in English, and "precipitation" is the only one left to match *bezinken*. The same list is in [D01](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D01-chemical-techniques/notes.md).
- Settling (*bezinken*): let a suspension stand, so that the solid, being denser than the liquid, sinks to the bottom as a sediment (*bezinksel*); then carefully pour off the liquid (*afschenken*, decanting). It's based on a difference in density (*dichtheid*). *(CvTE R&A2.1, R&A2.2)*
- It separates a suspension with fairly coarse, heavy particles, such as sand in water, as in the settling tanks of a water treatment plant. Two liquids that don't mix also settle into layers on standing; a separating funnel then lets you run off the bottom layer.
- In English chemistry texts, precipitation usually means a precipitation reaction (*neerslagreactie*): two solutions are mixed and a poorly soluble salt forms as a solid, the precipitate (*neerslag*). It's a related way to remove dissolved ions: add ions that form a poorly soluble salt with the unwanted ion, then filter off the precipitate (or let it settle). Sulfate ions are removed with barium ions: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s). Which ion combinations are poorly soluble is in the Binas table of salt solubilities ([B03](../../B-substances-and-materials/B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md)), and precipitation as a reaction type is in [C07](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C07-classification-of-reactions/notes.md). *(CvTE M3.10)*
- **Trap:** in this list and in CCVX D1.1, "precipitation" is the separation method; in the list of reaction types in C07 it is the precipitation reaction.

### Concentration by evaporation

- Concentration by evaporation (*indampen*): heat a solution so that the solvent evaporates, partly (to make the solution more concentrated) or completely, until the dissolved solid is left, often as crystals. The solvent is lost, not collected. It's based on a difference in boiling point: the solvent is volatile, the dissolved solid isn't. *(CvTE R&A2.1, R&A2.2)*
- It separates a solution of a solid in a liquid. Example: salt from sea water in salt pans, where sun and wind evaporate the water.
- **Trap:** to recover the solvent too, distil instead. And don't confuse *indampen*, the separation technique, with *verdampen*, the phase transition it uses.

### Paper chromatography

- Paper chromatography (*papierchromatografie*): put a small spot of the mixture (ink, food colouring, plant pigments) on a pencil start line near the bottom of a strip of paper, and stand the strip in a little solvent, the eluent or mobile phase (*loopvloeistof, mobiele fase*). The eluent rises through the paper and carries the substances along. A substance that dissolves well in the eluent and sticks weakly to the paper, the stationary phase (*stationaire fase*), moves far; one that sticks strongly stays behind. It's based on differences in solubility in the eluent and in adhesion to the paper. *(CvTE R&A2.1, R&A2.2)*
- It works on small amounts of dissolved, usually coloured substances, and is used to analyse a mixture (which substances it contains) rather than to obtain them. *(CvTE R&A2.3)*
- The R<sub>f</sub> value (*R<sub>f</sub>-waarde*) is the distance moved by a substance divided by the distance moved by the eluent front, both measured from the start line, so it lies between 0 and 1. A spot that moved 3.0 cm while the front moved 6.0 cm has R<sub>f</sub> = 3.0 / 6.0 = 0.50. With the same paper and eluent a substance always has the same R<sub>f</sub>, so comparing with known substances identifies the spots. *(CvTE R&A2.1)* Gas chromatography and the rest of the details are in [D01](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D01-chemical-techniques/notes.md).
- **Trap:** draw the start line in pencil (ink would separate too) and keep it above the eluent level, or the spots dissolve into the eluent. Measure both distances from the start line, not from the bottom of the paper.
- **Trap:** more than one spot proves a mixture, but a single spot doesn't prove a pure substance: two substances can have the same R<sub>f</sub> in that eluent.

## CCVX versus the Dutch syllabus

- **In both:** everything above. In the Dutch syllabus, pure substances and mixtures, melting and boiling ranges and the types of mixture (M2.1–M2.3) and the separation techniques themselves (R&A2.1–R&A2.2) are regular central-exam specifications. Only the phase-transition terms and state symbols (M1.3) and, in R&A2.1, terms such as *destillaat*, *extractiemiddel*, *residu*, *oplosmiddel* and *loopvloeistof* are printed in italics to mark them as assumed known from lower school, the school exam or other subjects. CCVX covers the same topics again in [B02](../../B-substances-and-materials/B02-properties-and-models/notes.md) and [D01](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D01-chemical-techniques/notes.md).
- **Translation trap:** "precipitation" in CCVX sb5 and D1.1 is the separation technique *bezinken* (settling), not the precipitation reaction (see sb5).
- **Numbering trap:** the CCVX PDF drops the label sb3; the README restores it.
