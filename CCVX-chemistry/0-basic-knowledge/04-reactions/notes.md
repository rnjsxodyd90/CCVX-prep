## sb12 Chemical reactions

- In a chemical reaction (*chemische reactie*) substances disappear and new substances with other properties form. The substances you start with are the reactants (*beginstoffen*); the new ones are the reaction products (*reactieproducten*). *(CvTE R1.2)*
- At micro level the atoms are regrouped: bonds between atoms break and new bonds form, but no atom disappears, appears or turns into another kind. In a phase transition or in mixing, by contrast, the particles stay the same, so no new substance forms.
- A reaction equation (*reactievergelijking*) has the formulas of the reactants left of the arrow and those of the products on the right. Coefficients (*coëfficiënten*) in front of the formulas make every atomic species occur equally often on both sides (element balance, *elementbalans*) and, when ions take part, make the total charge equal too (charge balance, *ladingsbalans*). Burning methane: CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l). *(CvTE R1.1, R1.2)*
- Law of conservation of elements (*elementbehoud*): in a chemical reaction the number of atoms of each element stays the same, which is why an equation must balance. *(CvTE R&A3.1)*
- Law of mass conservation (*massabehoud*), also called the law of conservation of mass (*wet van behoud van massa*): the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants, because the atoms and their masses are conserved. Burning 48.62 g magnesium uses 32.00 g oxygen and gives 48.62 g + 32.00 g = 80.62 g magnesium oxide: 2 Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2 MgO(s), and indeed 2 × 40.31 g = 80.62 g. *(CvTE R&A3.1)*
- Because of this, the masses in a reaction are in a fixed ratio, which the calculations in [C02](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C02-chemical-calculations/notes.md) use. The conservation laws for mass, energy and charge are treated further in [C03](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C03-conservation-laws-and-cycles/notes.md). *(CvTE R&A1.1)*
- **Trap:** in an open container the mass seems to change. A burning candle gets lighter because carbon dioxide and water vapour escape; burning magnesium or iron wool gets heavier because it takes up oxygen from the air. In a closed container the total mass stays the same.
- **Trap:** balance with coefficients only; changing an index changes the substance (H₂O₂ isn't H₂O). Atoms and mass are conserved, but the number of molecules need not be: 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O turns three molecules into two.

## sb13 Decomposition reactions

- A decomposition reaction (*ontledingsreactie*) has one reactant, a compound (a decomposable substance), which splits into two or more products. It is named after the form of energy that drives it. *(CvTE R1.3)*
- Thermolysis (*thermolyse*): decomposition by heating. Sodium hydrogen carbonate (baking soda) decomposes on heating: 2 NaHCO₃(s) → Na₂CO₃(s) + H₂O(g) + CO₂(g). Limestone in a lime kiln: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). *(CvTE R1.3)*
- Electrolysis (*elektrolyse*): decomposition by an electric current, a direct current through a liquid or solution that conducts. Water, with a little sodium sulfate or sulfuric acid dissolved in it so that it conducts: 2 H₂O(l) → 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g). Hydrogen forms at the negative electrode and oxygen at the positive electrode, in the volume ratio 2 : 1. Molten sodium chloride: 2 NaCl(l) → 2 Na(l) + Cl₂(g). *(CvTE R1.3)* The electrode reactions are CCVX C1.9 in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- Photolysis (*fotolyse*): decomposition by light. Silver bromide decomposes in light, the basis of black-and-white photography: 2 AgBr → 2 Ag + Br₂; the finely divided silver that forms looks black. *(CvTE R1.3)*
- Thermolysis, electrolysis and photolysis such as these are endothermic: the reaction continues only while heat, current or light is supplied (sb15). *(CvTE R5.1)*
- A substance that can be decomposed is a compound, and complete decomposition ends in non-decomposable substances (sb6 in [02-elements-and-compounds](../02-elements-and-compounds/notes.md)). One decomposition step can still give compounds, such as Na₂CO₃, H₂O and CO₂ above. Classifying reactions by type is [C07](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C07-classification-of-reactions/notes.md).
- **Trap:** heating a substance in air doesn't always decompose it. Copper heated in air turns into black copper(II) oxide because it reacts with oxygen, 2 Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2 CuO(s), and gets heavier; a decomposition has only one reactant.

## sb14 Combustion

- Combustion (*verbranding*) is the reaction of a substance, the fuel (*brandstof*), with oxygen, usually with a flame. It is exothermic, and the products are usually oxides (*oxiden*) of the elements in the fuel. Magnesium burns with a bright white flame to magnesium oxide (sb12); sulfur burns to sulfur dioxide: S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g). *(CvTE R1.3)*
- Complete combustion (*volledige verbranding*): with enough oxygen, a compound of carbon, hydrogen and possibly oxygen gives only CO₂ and H₂O. *(CvTE R1.2, R1.3)*
    - Natural gas (mainly methane): CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l).
    - Ethanol: C₂H₅OH(l) + 3 O₂(g) → 2 CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l).
    - Balance C first, then H, then O. A fraction is allowed, C₂H₆ + 3½ O₂ → 2 CO₂ + 3 H₂O, or double everything: 2 C₂H₆ + 7 O₂ → 4 CO₂ + 6 H₂O. More in CCVX C1.6 in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- Incomplete combustion (*onvolledige verbranding*): with too little oxygen, part of the carbon ends up as carbon monoxide (CO) or as soot (*roet*, carbon) instead of CO₂. *(CvTE R1.3)*
    - 2 CH₄(g) + 3 O₂(g) → 2 CO(g) + 4 H₂O(l)
    - CH₄(g) + O₂(g) → C(s) + 2 H₂O(l)
    - A yellow, sooty flame (a gas burner with its air inlet closed) shows incomplete combustion; with the air inlet open the flame is blue and the combustion (nearly) complete. Carbon monoxide is colourless, odourless and poisonous, which makes a badly ventilated gas heater dangerous.
- Ignition temperature (*ontbrandingstemperatuur*): the lowest temperature to which a substance must be heated before it starts to burn. Below it the fuel doesn't catch fire even with oxygen present; once it burns, the heat released keeps the fuel above its ignition temperature, so the fire keeps going. *(CvTE R5.1)*
- The fire triangle (*branddriehoek*): a fire needs fuel, oxygen, and a temperature at or above the ignition temperature. Taking one away puts it out: cool it with water (below the ignition temperature), smother it with a fire blanket, sand or a CO₂ extinguisher (no oxygen), or remove the fuel (close the gas tap).
- A finely divided fuel (dust, powder, sawdust) burns much faster than a lump of the same fuel, because the surface area of reacting substances (*verdelingsgraad*) is larger: more of the fuel is in contact with the oxygen. See [C04](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C04-reaction-kinetics/notes.md). *(CvTE R3.1)*
- **Trap:** never put water on burning oil or fat. The water sinks, boils explosively and sprays burning oil around; smother the fire with a lid or a fire blanket instead.
- **Trap:** write oxygen as O₂, and for a complete combustion of a compound of C, H and O give only CO₂ and H₂O as products.

## sb15 Exothermic and endothermic reactions

- Chemical reactions have a heat effect: energy is released or taken up, because the products contain a different amount of chemical energy (*chemische energie*) from the reactants. *(CvTE R5.1)*
- Exothermic (*exotherm*): the reaction releases energy to the surroundings (heat, often also light), so the surroundings warm up; the products have less chemical energy than the reactants. Examples: every combustion, and the reaction of an acid with a base. *(CvTE R5.1)*
- Endothermic (*endotherm*): the reaction takes up energy, so the products have more chemical energy than the reactants. Usually the energy has to be supplied the whole time, and the reaction stops when the supply stops. Examples: the decompositions in sb13, and photosynthesis in plants, which runs on light: 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂. *(CvTE R5.1)*
- The heat of reaction ΔE (*reactiewarmte*) is negative for an exothermic and positive for an endothermic reaction. Forming 1 mol of liquid water from hydrogen and oxygen releases 2.86·10⁵ J (Binas: heats of formation), so ΔE = −2.86·10⁵ J mol⁻¹. The reverse reaction, decomposing 1 mol of liquid water, has ΔE = +2.86·10⁵ J mol⁻¹. *(CvTE R5.1, R5.3)* Calculations with heats of formation are in [C06](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C06-energy-calculations/notes.md).
- Many exothermic reactions need energy to get started, such as heating a fuel to its ignition temperature (sb14). This activation energy (*activeringsenergie*) is shown in an energy diagram (*energiediagram*), CCVX C6.1 in [C06](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C06-energy-calculations/notes.md). Once started, an exothermic reaction usually keeps itself going; an endothermic one usually doesn't. *(CvTE R5.1, R5.2)*
- No energy is lost: the energy released by a reaction is taken up by the surroundings, and the energy taken up by an endothermic reaction comes from them (energy conservation, [C03](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C03-conservation-laws-and-cycles/notes.md)). *(CvTE R&A3.1)*
- **Trap:** needing heat to start, as when lighting a gas burner, doesn't make a reaction endothermic. What counts is whether, overall, energy is released or taken up.
- **Trap:** the sign of ΔE: negative for exothermic (energy leaves the reacting substances), positive for endothermic.

## sb16 Detecting substances

- A reagent (*reagens*) is a substance that shows the presence of another substance by a clearly visible change: a change of colour, a cloudiness or precipitate, or a gas. A good reagent gives that change with as few other substances as possible. Showing that a substance is present is called detecting it (*aantonen*). *(CvTE R&A2.1)*

| Substance | Test | Positive result |
|---|---|---|
| oxygen | glowing splint (*gloeiende houtspaan*) | the splint relights |
| hydrogen | burning splint at the mouth of the tube | a squeaky pop |
| water | white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate | it turns blue |
| carbon dioxide | bubble the gas through limewater (*kalkwater*) | the limewater turns cloudy |
| sulfur dioxide | iodine solution, or acidified permanganate solution | the solution loses its colour |

- Oxygen: a glowing splint bursts into flame in oxygen; air has too little oxygen to do this.
- Hydrogen: a mixture of hydrogen and air burns explosively with a squeaky pop when lit: 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 H₂O(g).
- Water: white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate (*watervrij koper(II)sulfaat*) takes up water as crystal water (*kristalwater*) and turns blue: CuSO₄(s) + 5 H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s). *(CvTE M1.11)*
- Carbon dioxide: limewater is a clear solution of calcium hydroxide, Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq). Carbon dioxide makes it cloudy because poorly soluble calcium carbonate forms: Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l).
- Sulfur dioxide is a reducing agent (*reductor*). It decolourises a yellow-brown iodine solution (*joodoplossing*): SO₂(aq) + I₂(aq) + 2 H₂O(l) → SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) + 4 H⁺(aq). It also decolourises a purple acidified potassium permanganate solution: 5 SO₂(aq) + 2 MnO₄⁻(aq) + 2 H₂O(l) → 5 SO₄²⁻(aq) + 2 Mn²⁺(aq) + 4 H⁺(aq). Carbon dioxide does neither. How to build such redox equations is in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md). *(CvTE R1.3)*
- **Trap:** blue copper(II) sulfate shows that water is present, not that a liquid is pure water: any aqueous solution turns it blue.
- **Trap:** sulfur dioxide also turns limewater cloudy (calcium sulfite, CaSO₃, is poorly soluble too), so cloudy limewater proves carbon dioxide only if sulfur dioxide can't be present; the decolourising tests tell them apart.
- **Trap:** if you keep bubbling carbon dioxide through limewater, the cloudiness disappears again, because the calcium carbonate reacts further: CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 HCO₃⁻(aq).

## CCVX versus the Dutch syllabus

- **In both:** reactions and reaction equations, the conservation of elements and mass, decomposition and combustion, ignition temperature, exothermic and endothermic, and the term reagent *(CvTE R1.1–R1.3, R5.1, R&A2.1, R&A3.1)*. The Dutch syllabus prints decomposition reactions, incomplete combustion, ignition temperature, the coefficient and the reagent in italics to mark them as assumed known from lower school, the school exam or other subjects; complete combustion, element and charge balance, exothermic and endothermic and the conservation laws are regular central-exam specifications.
- **CCVX only:** the tests for oxygen, hydrogen, water, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide. The CvTE syllabus names only the term *reagens*.
