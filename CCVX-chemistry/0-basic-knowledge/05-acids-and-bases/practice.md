# 0-basic-knowledge 05 — Acids and bases: practice

Ten original questions on learning outcomes sb17–sb18, in CCVX style. This is the foundation for C01 — chemical processes (strong/weak acids, acid-base equations) and C05 — chemical equilibrium (pH calculations).

### chem-05-q01 | points 2 | outcome sb17 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the name and the particle notation in water of each: (1) hydrogen chloride and the solution it forms, (2) 'carbonic acid' and its solution, (3) ethanoic acid and the everyday dilute solution called vinegar.
#### Answer
1. Hydrogen chloride is HCl(g); in water it forms hydrochloric acid, H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). 2. 'Carbonic acid' is the fictional molecule H₂CO₃; its solution is CO₂ dissolved in water (sparkling water), written CO₂(aq) + H₂O(l). 3. Ethanoic acid (acetic acid) is CH₃COOH; vinegar is a dilute solution CH₃COOH(aq).
#### Solution
1. Pure HCl is a gas (hydrogen chloride); its solution is hydrochloric acid, written as its ions H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), because the strong acid has reacted completely with the water.
2. H₂CO₃ hardly exists as molecules: carbon dioxide dissolves as CO₂ molecules, a small part of which reacts as an acid; hence the quotes.
3. Vinegar is a weak-acid solution: the molecules are written as CH₃COOH(aq), because only a small part has reacted to CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq).

### chem-05-q02 | points 3 | outcome sb17 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the equation for what happens when (1) hydrogen chloride dissolves in water and (2) ethanoic acid dissolves in water. Explain the difference in notation.
#### Answer
1. HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) (complete). 2. CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) (equilibrium; only a small part reacts).
#### Solution
1. Hydrochloric acid is a strong acid: every HCl molecule gives its H⁺ to a water molecule, so the reaction is complete and written with one arrow.
2. Ethanoic acid is a weak acid: only a small fraction of the molecules passes on its H⁺, so both directions keep running: a double arrow (equilibrium), K_z = 1.8·10⁻⁵ (Binas, table of acid and base constants).
3. That is why a solution of a strong acid is written as ions and a solution of a weak acid as molecules.

### chem-05-q03 | points 3 | outcome sb18 | difficulty easy
#### Question
From the following particles, pick out the bases of the sb18 list and state what each base does with H⁺: O²⁻, Na⁺, OH⁻, H₃O⁺, CO₃²⁻, NH₃, SO₄²⁻.
#### Answer
The bases are: O²⁻ (strong), OH⁻ (strong), CO₃²⁻ (weak), NH₃ (weak); each takes up an H⁺ (proton acceptor). Na⁺, H₃O⁺, SO₄²⁻ are not bases of the list.
#### Solution
1. A base is a proton acceptor. Oxide O²⁻ reacts completely with water: O²⁻ + H₂O → 2 OH⁻ (a strong base).
2. Hydroxide OH⁻ takes up H⁺ to H₂O (strong).
3. Carbonate CO₃²⁻ takes up H⁺ to HCO₃⁻ (weak base).
4. Ammonia NH₃ takes up H⁺ to NH₄⁺ (weak base).
5. Na⁺ is an inert ion, H₃O⁺ is a strong acid, and SO₄²⁻ (conjugate base of the strong-ish HSO₄⁻) is a far weaker base than the four above and is not on the sb18 list.

### chem-05-q04 | points 3 | outcome sb17–sb18 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Give the base that belongs to each acid (the conjugate base), and the acid that belongs to each base: (1) HNO₃, (2) NH₃, (3) HCO₃⁻ as acid, (4) HCO₃⁻ as base.
#### Answer
1. NO₃⁻; 2. the conjugate acid NH₄⁺; 3. CO₃²⁻; 4. H₂CO₃ (that is, CO₂(aq) + H₂O).
#### Solution
1. Removing H⁺ from an acid gives its conjugate base: HNO₃ → NO₃⁻.
2. Adding H⁺ to a base gives its conjugate acid: NH₃ → NH₄⁺.
3. HCO₃⁻ acting as an acid donates its H⁺: HCO₃⁻ → CO₃²⁻.
4. HCO₃⁻ acting as a base accepts H⁺: HCO₃⁻ → 'H₂CO₃' (CO₂ + H₂O). A particle that can do both, like HCO₃⁻, is an ampholyte (see C01).

### chem-05-q05 | points 3 | outcome sb18 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Give the equation for what happens when each of the following is mixed with water: (1) ammonia, (2) sodium oxide, (3) sodium carbonate solution (the carbonate ion with water).
#### Answer
1. NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq); 2. Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2 Na⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq); 3. CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HCO₃⁻(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
#### Solution
1. Ammonia is a weak base: a small part takes up H⁺ from water, so the solution (Dutch *ammonia*) contains some OH⁻ — an equilibrium.
2. The oxide ion is a strong base and reacts completely: O²⁻ + H₂O → 2 OH⁻; including the ions of the salt: Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2 Na⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq).
3. The carbonate ion is a weak base: CO₃²⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻, so soda (sodium carbonate solution) is basic.
4. In all three, an O–H bond of water breaks and the OH⁻ that forms makes the solution basic (pH > 7).

### chem-05-q06 | points 4 | outcome sb16–sb17 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A student adds hydrochloric acid to a sodium carbonate solution ('soda solution') and sees fizzing. (1) Give the equation for the reaction with an excess of acid. (2) Name the gas and explain how you test for it. (3) Give the equation if the acid is in short supply instead.
#### Answer
1. 2 H₃O⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l). 2. CO₂; pass it through limewater, which turns milky. 3. H₃O⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₂O(l).
#### Solution
1. With excess acid both removable protons are delivered to the carbonate; the 'carbonic acid' that forms falls apart into CO₂ gas and water (the fizzing).
2. The gas is carbon dioxide; the test is limewater: Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l), a white precipitate.
3. With little acid, carbonate takes up only one H⁺, to hydrogencarbonate: H₃O⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O, and no gas escapes.
4. Which equation applies depends on which reactant is in excess — the same excess/deficiency thinking as in C02.

### chem-05-q07 | points 2 | outcome sb17 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Two solutions both contain 0.1 mol of acid per litre: one of hydrochloric acid, one of ethanoic acid. (1) Which has the lower pH? (2) Both turn universal indicator red; what does that tell you, and what does it not tell you?
#### Answer
1. The hydrochloric acid. 2. It tells you both solutions are acidic (pH < 7); it does not tell you how acidic — it cannot distinguish strong from weak at this concentration.
#### Solution
1. Hydrochloric acid is strong: [H₃O⁺] = 0.1 mol L⁻¹, pH = 1.0. Ethanoic acid is weak: only a small part has ionised, so [H₃O⁺] is much lower and the pH is higher (about 2.9; the calculation is in C05).
2. Universal indicator says "acidic", roughly by colour; a pH meter is needed to say how acidic.
3. Indicators themselves (litmus, universal indicator) are acid-base reagents: they change colour where the pH passes their change-over point (see E01 — chemical research and A08 — scientific instruments).

### chem-05-q08 | points 3 | outcome sb18 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Calcium oxide (quicklime) is spread in tiny amounts on acidic soil. (1) Give the equation for its reaction with water. (2) Explain why one calls the oxide ion a strong base. (3) Why is too much lime bad for plants?
#### Answer
1. CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq). 2. O²⁻ reacts completely with water to OH⁻ (irreversible), which is the definition of a strong base. 3. The OH⁻ it produces would raise the soil pH too far (burn roots/lock up nutrients).
#### Solution
1. CaO dissolves and the oxide ion immediately takes protons from water: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) (limewater when little CaO is used, lime milk when much is).
2. O²⁻ + H₂O → 2 OH⁻ goes to completion: a strong base. (Contrast NH₃, which only partly reacts: a weak base.)
3. Neutral is not the target for every plant, and an excess of OH⁻ makes the soil too basic: roots are damaged and several nutrient ions become insoluble. The point of liming is to neutralise acid (H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2 H₂O), not to overdose the base.

### chem-05-q09 | points 4 | outcome sb17 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Sparkling water has a pH of about 4. (1) Give the equation that explains why the solution of carbon dioxide in water is acidic. (2) Explain why chemists put 'H₂CO₃' in quotes. (3) Compare the way CO₂ and HNO₃ behave when they dissolve in water.
#### Answer
1. CO₂(aq) + 2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq). 2. The molecule H₂CO₃ hardly exists: in the solution most of it stays as dissolved CO₂ and only a small part reacts as an acid, so 'H₂CO₃' is only a name for the CO₂-water system. 3. HNO₃ reacts completely (strong acid, one arrow); CO₂ reacts only partly (weak acid, equilibrium arrow).
#### Solution
1. A small part of the dissolved CO₂ reacts with water as an acid (donates H⁺): CO₂(aq) + 2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq); the H₃O⁺ formed sets pH ≈ 4.
2. You will not find separate H₂CO₃ molecules in the bottle: analytically the acidity behaves as if 'H₂CO₃' existed, but microscopically the particles are CO₂ molecules and a little HCO₃⁻ and H₃O⁺ — hence the quotes.
3. HNO₃(l) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) is complete (strong acid); the equation with 'H₂CO₃' is an equilibrium (weak acid). Notation: H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) versus CO₂(aq).

### chem-05-q10 | points 4 | outcome sb16–sb18 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A student wants to make a small amount of carbon dioxide in the lab by reacting marble chips (CaCO₃) with dilute hydrochloric acid. (1) Give the equation (leave out spectator ions). (2) Why does the student choose marble chips instead of sodium carbonate powder? (3) The gas produced washes through limewater; give the equation for the test reaction.
#### Answer
1. 2 H₃O⁺(aq) + CaCO₃(s) → Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l). 2. Marble is an insoluble salt: the reaction takes place at its surface, so it runs at a controllable rate and gives off CO₂ steadily; with soluble Na₂CO₃ the whole charge of acid would react in one violent fizz. 3. Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l).
#### Solution
1. Particles present: H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ (from the strong acid) and solid CaCO₃ (Binas, table of salt solubilities: CaCO₃ is insoluble). The carbonate takes up two protons: 2 H₃O⁺(aq) + CaCO₃(s) → Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l). Cl⁻ does not react.
2. Two practical reasons: at the surface of chips the reaction runs at a steady, controllable rate (surface area, see C04 — reaction kinetics), while soluble carbonate powder would mix at once and fizz violently; and you can dose the acid on the lumps and stop the reaction by decanting.
3. Limewater is Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq); CO₂ precipitates the calcium: Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) — the liquid turns milky.
