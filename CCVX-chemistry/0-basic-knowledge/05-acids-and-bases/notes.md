# sb17–sb18 — Acids and Bases: notes

These are English explanations of every learning outcome in [README.md](README.md), and every topic is *drafted*: written from the CCVX and CvTE syllabi and standard VWO chemistry, not yet checked against an open source text or exam question (see [PLAN.md](../../PLAN.md) for the statuses).

Dutch terms are in brackets. A code such as *(CvTE M1.4)* points to the matching specification in the [CvTE 2027 syllabus](../../syllabus/cvte-syllabus-scheikunde-vwo-2027.pdf); Binas tables are named by subject, because their numbers differ between editions.

## sb17 Acids and acid solutions

- An acid (*zuur*) is a particle that can donate an H⁺ ion (a proton) to another particle; a base (*base*) is a particle that can accept one. The transfer of an H⁺ ion from an acid to a base is an acid-base reaction (*zuur-base reactie*). *(CvTE R1.3)* Acid-base reactions as donor-acceptor reactions are CCVX C1.7 and C1.11 in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- When an acid dissolves in water, its molecules give H⁺ ions to water molecules, which become oxonium ions (*oxoniumionen*), H₃O⁺. An acid solution (*zure oplossing*) therefore contains H₃O⁺(aq) together with the negative ion left over from the acid; H⁺(aq) is also written for H₃O⁺(aq). *(CvTE R1.3, R&A1.4)*
- The H₃O⁺ ions give all acid solutions their shared properties: a pH below 7 at 298 K, a sour taste (never taste anything in the lab), and a reaction with bases. An indicator (*indicator*) shows this: litmus (*lakmoes*) turns red in an acid solution, and universal indicator paper (*universeel indicatorpapier*) shows the pH by its colour (Binas: acid-base indicators). *(CvTE R&A1.4, R&A2.1)* The pH scale is CCVX C5.5 in [C05](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C05-chemical-equilibrium/notes.md).
- A strong acid (*sterk zuur*) reacts completely with water, so the equation has a single arrow; a weak acid (*zwak zuur*) reacts only partly, giving an equilibrium with a double arrow. Of the acids below, hydrogen chloride, sulfuric acid (its first step) and nitric acid are strong; 'carbonic acid' and ethanoic acid are weak. *(CvTE R1.8)* The difference is CCVX C1.10 in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- The Dutch list of acids to recognise also has phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄ *(CvTE R1.5)*; CCVX lists it with the others in B01 item 8 ([B01](../../B-substances-and-materials/B01-particle-models/notes.md)).

### Hydrogen chloride and hydrochloric acid

- Hydrogen chloride (*waterstofchloride*), HCl, is a colourless gas made of molecules. It dissolves very well in water and reacts completely, as a strong acid: HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). *(CvTE R1.5)*
- The solution is hydrochloric acid (*zoutzuur*), written H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq); it contains no HCl molecules. Stomach acid contains dilute hydrochloric acid. *(CvTE R1.4)*
- **Trap:** HCl is the gas, a molecular substance; hydrochloric acid is its solution. In an equation with hydrochloric acid, write H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), not HCl(aq).

### Sulfuric acid and sulfuric acid solution

- Pure sulfuric acid (*zwavelzuur*), H₂SO₄, is a colourless, oily liquid made of molecules. Each molecule can give two H⁺ ions, in two steps: the first is complete, the second only partial. *(CvTE R1.5)*
    - H₂SO₄(l) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + HSO₄⁻(aq)
    - HSO₄⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq)
- A sulfuric acid solution (*zwavelzuuroplossing*) therefore contains H₃O⁺ ions with hydrogen sulfate ions (HSO₄⁻) and sulfate ions (SO₄²⁻). How to write it in a reaction equation is in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).
- **Trap:** concentrated sulfuric acid gets very hot when it's mixed with water. Always pour the acid slowly into the water, never water into the acid.

### Nitric acid and nitric acid solution

- Nitric acid (*salpeterzuur*), HNO₃, is a liquid made of molecules. It's a strong acid: HNO₃(l) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq). *(CvTE R1.5)*
- A nitric acid solution (*salpeterzuuroplossing*) is H₃O⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq). Its salts are the nitrates (*nitraten*).

### 'Carbonic acid' and 'carbonic acid solution'

- Carbon dioxide dissolves in water, and a small part of it reacts with water as a weak acid: CO₂(aq) + 2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq). Sparkling water is therefore slightly acidic, and so is clean rainwater. *(CvTE R1.5)*
- The quotation marks mean that H₂CO₃ molecules hardly exist as such: a 'carbonic acid solution' is mainly CO₂(aq) in water. The Dutch syllabus writes the acid as H₂O + CO₂ / 'H₂CO₃' *(CvTE R1.5)*, and in an equation you write CO₂ + H₂O.
- The salts of carbonic acid are the carbonates and hydrogen carbonates (sb18, and sb9 in [02-elements-and-compounds](../02-elements-and-compounds/notes.md)).
- **Trap:** in everyday Dutch, *koolzuur* (or *koolzuurgas*) often means the gas CO₂ itself, as in the bubbles of a fizzy drink. Chemically that gas is carbon dioxide (*koolstofdioxide*); the acid is CO₂ + H₂O.

### Ethanoic acid, acetic acid and vinegar

- Ethanoic acid (*ethaanzuur*), also called acetic acid (*azijnzuur*, a trivial name), CH₃COOH, is a carbon acid (*carbonzuur*): an organic acid with a carboxyl group (*carboxylgroep*), –COOH. *(CvTE M1.3, R1.5)*
- It's a weak acid: CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq), with K<sub>z</sub> = 1.8·10⁻⁵ (Binas: acid and base constants). The negative ion is the ethanoate ion (*ethanoaat*), also called acetate (*acetaat*). Calculations with K<sub>z</sub> are CCVX C5.2 in [C05](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C05-chemical-equilibrium/notes.md). *(CvTE R1.8, R&A1.6)*
- Vinegar (*azijn*) is a dilute solution of ethanoic acid in water.
- **Trap:** CH₃COOH has four H atoms, but only the H of the COOH group can be given to water; the three H atoms of the CH₃ group can't. Ethanoic acid gives one H⁺ per molecule.

## sb18 Bases

- A base (*base*) is a particle that can accept an H⁺ ion. A basic solution (*basische oplossing*) contains more OH⁻ ions than H₃O⁺ ions: its pH is above 7 at 298 K, and litmus turns blue in it. *(CvTE R1.3, R&A1.4)*
- Three of the four bases below are negative ions, which occur in salts. The positive ion of such a salt, such as Na⁺, isn't the base and doesn't react; it's a spectator ion (*tribune-ion*) that you leave out of the reaction equation. Ammonia is the only base below that is a molecule. *(CvTE R1.6, R1.7)*
- The Dutch list of bases to recognise also has the hydrogen carbonate ion, HCO₃⁻ *(CvTE R1.6)*; CCVX lists it in B01 item 9 ([B01](../../B-substances-and-materials/B01-particle-models/notes.md)). Writing acid-base equations is CCVX C1.11 in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md).

### Hydroxide ions

- The hydroxide ion (*hydroxide-ion*), OH⁻, is a strong base. With an acid solution it forms water: OH⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → 2 H₂O(l). If equal chemical quantities of H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ are mixed, the solution becomes neutral, pH 7 at 298 K; this is neutralisation (*neutralisatie*), and it is exothermic. *(CvTE R1.6, R1.7)*
- Hydroxide ions come from soluble hydroxides such as sodium hydroxide (*natriumhydroxide*), a white solid salt: NaOH(s) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). Its solution is caustic soda (*natronloog*), written Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). A solution of calcium hydroxide is limewater (*kalkwater*; sb16 in [04-reactions](../04-reactions/notes.md)). *(CvTE R1.4)*
- **Trap:** caustic soda is the solution, NaOH(s) is the solid. In an equation with caustic soda, write Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq); the base is the OH⁻ ion.
- **Trap:** an OH group in a molecule doesn't make a base. Ethanol, C₂H₅OH, is an alcohol and forms no OH⁻ ions in water.

### Oxide ions

- The oxide ion (*oxide-ion*), O²⁻, occurs in metal oxides, which are salts: sodium oxide Na₂O, calcium oxide CaO, copper(II) oxide CuO. It's a strong base and can't exist in water, because it takes an H⁺ ion from a water molecule straight away: O²⁻ + H₂O → 2 OH⁻. *(CvTE R1.6)*
- So a soluble metal oxide gives a basic solution: Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2 Na⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq).
- An insoluble metal oxide still reacts with an acid solution. Black copper(II) oxide dissolves in an acid and gives a blue solution: CuO(s) + 2 H₃O⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 3 H₂O(l). *(CvTE R1.7)*
- **Trap:** only metal oxides contain O²⁻ ions. Non-metal oxides such as CO₂ and SO₂ are molecular substances and give acid solutions, and an O atom in a molecule or in OH⁻ isn't an oxide ion.

### Carbonate ions

- The carbonate ion (*carbonaation*), CO₃²⁻, occurs in carbonates such as sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃, and calcium carbonate, CaCO₃ (limestone, marble and chalk). It can accept two H⁺ ions, one after the other: *(CvTE R1.6)*
    - CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)
    - HCO₃⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l)
    - together, with enough acid: CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2 H₃O⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l)
- With an acid, a carbonate fizzes because carbon dioxide forms, which you can show with limewater (sb16 in [04-reactions](../04-reactions/notes.md)). Limestone in hydrochloric acid: CaCO₃(s) + 2 H₃O⁺(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l). Vinegar removes limescale (*kalkaanslag*) in the same way; ethanoic acid is weak, so it's written as molecules: CaCO₃(s) + 2 CH₃COOH(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 CH₃COO⁻(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l). *(CvTE R1.7)*
- A solution of sodium carbonate is basic, because the carbonate ions take H⁺ from water: CO₃²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HCO₃⁻(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
- **Trap:** the carbonate ion is a base, not an acid, even though carbonates are the salts of 'carbonic acid'. An acid turns a carbonate into CO₂ and water.

### Ammonia molecules

- Ammonia (*ammoniak*), NH₃, is a colourless gas with a sharp smell, made of molecules. It dissolves very well in water and is a weak base: NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq), with K<sub>b</sub> = 1.8·10⁻⁵ (Binas: acid and base constants). *(CvTE R1.6, R&A1.6)*
- With an acid solution, ammonia reacts completely to ammonium ions (*ammoniumionen*): NH₃(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq) + H₂O(l). Ammonium salts such as ammonium nitrate are used as fertiliser. *(CvTE R1.7)*
- The solution of ammonia in water is called *ammonia* in Dutch, written NH₃(aq); the cleaning product sold as ammonia is such a solution. *(CvTE R1.4)*
- **Trap:** English "ammonia" is the substance NH₃, Dutch *ammoniak*; Dutch *ammonia* is its solution in water, NH₃(aq). The terms are listed in CCVX A10 ([A10](../../A-skills/A10-using-chemical-concepts/notes.md)).
- **Trap:** the ammonium ion, NH₄⁺, isn't a base. It has taken up an H⁺ ion and can give it back, so it's a weak acid.
