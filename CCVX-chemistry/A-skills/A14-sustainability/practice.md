# A14 — Sustainability: practice

Five original questions on the A14 learning outcomes (life cycles, alternatives, social role, world problems), in CCVX style.

### chem-a14-q01 | points 3 | outcome A14 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A shopper compares a disposable plastic cup with a washed china mug. Describe the life cycle of each product in three sentence snapshots (resources → use → end of life), and state at which life-cycle stage the maddest difference is found.
#### Answer
Plastic cup: crude oil → polymerised once → membrane cycle, used once → incineration with some energy recovery (or litter: decades of plastic waste). China mug: sand-/clay-based raw materials → once at high temperature made → hundreds of washes (with soap and water) → at end: landfill of inert material lasting centuries. The decisive stage is the use phase: the mug only pays its environmental price back through many reuses, so the number of reuses decides which one is actually greener.
#### Solution
1. Life-cycle thinking names the stages: extraction/production, use, end-of-life.
2. A disposable's low price is bought at the end-of-life stage; a dishwasher + detergent price is the mug's use phase.
3. The break-even point (how many washes equal one cup) is the crux: A14 asks comparative life cycles, not "paper good, plastic bad".

### chem-a14-q02 | points 3 | outcome A14 | difficulty medium
#### Question
In a choice between two solvents for a pharmaceutical synthesis — dichloromethane (DCM, highly useful but toxic and persistent) and ethyl acetate (less toxic, made from fermentation) — justify the choice with three life-cycle comparisons (source path, use phase risks, end-of-life).
#### Answer
Source: DCM is made from chloromethane/chlorine (fossil + chlorine industry chain), ethyl acetate is made by esterification of fermentation ethanol and acetic acid (renewable part of the chain possible). Use: DCM is acutely toxic for workers (and volatile — high emissions at operation), EA has health risks like flammability but far milder toxicity. End-of-life: DCM is persistent and problematic in soil/air and in incineration (halogen acids), EA biodegrades and burns to CO₂ + H₂O — throughout the cycle EA is the demonstrably greener solvent, which justifies the choice unless the DCM step is irreplaceable in the reaction's chemistry itself.
#### Solution
1. Life-cycle = from resource pipeline to fate after use: all three stages scored.
2. "Use phase" includes the workers: process risks belong to the choice too.
3. The last clause is honest: only if the reaction itself needs DCM's special solvation is the alternative worse chemically — A14 is not a purity contest, it's a weighted comparison.

### chem-a14-q03 | points 4 | outcome A14 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A UN-related world problem: give the chemistry in the following lines by sketching one mechanism and one numerical estimate each: (1) why uncontrolled fertilizer in Western farms sustains the algal bloom in rivers; (2) what solar panels on a house do for the fossil part of electricity.
#### Answer
1. Mechanism: nitrate/phosphate run off or leach into water → algae grow explosively (eutrophication) → dead algae decomposed by bacteria, consuming dissolved O₂ → hypoxia kills fish. Estimate: 1 kg of phosphate entering a river can support ≈ 50 kg of algae mass roughly a thousandfolds weight of dead organic matter demanding hundreds of kg O₂ — a small leak, a huge BOD bill.
2. Mechanism: each kWh of solar displaces a marginal kWh of gas/coal production (the marginal electricity in NL today is fossil). Estimate: with 0.4 kg CO₂/kWh avoided (gas mix), a 3,000 kWh/year solar roof avoids ≈ 1.2 tonnes of CO₂ per year — the arithmetic that turns a panel into the global-warming fight.
#### Solution
1. Chemistry: nutrient-P runoff, algal metabolism, bacterial oxidation demanding O₂ (eutrophication as a chemical budget).
2. Chemistry of justice: marginal plant displacement (solar's avoided emissions = what the gas plant would have burned), and the CO₂ count per kWh gives the global-warming meaning of the roof.

### chem-a14-q04 | points 4 | outcome A14 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Social role: argue the two-sided social importance of chemistry for sustainability in six sentences, with (1) chemistry as the cause problem, (2) chemistry as the only possible solution part, (3) why a 'chemistry-free future' is not possible, (4) one example domain.
#### Answer
1. Chemistry caused major sustainability problems by design decision: persistent pesticides, CFCs, plastics that never degrade (examples: DDT accumulation, ozone depletion, microplastics).
2. But chemistry is also the only discipline that knows the materials directly and can fix them: batteries for the energy transition, CO₂-to-fuel catalysts, recovery of phosphorus from sewage — its knowledge is the remedy's motor.
3. A "chemistry-free" life is physically impossible: food, drinking water, health, the air we breathe are chemical (and the technologies we still need — steel, cement, medicine — cannot be done without it).
4. Example: the energy transition — it is at heart a materials/way-station problem (electrochemistry for storage, catalysis for fuels): chemistry provides the required critical knowledge for society.
#### Solution
1. Two-sides argument is honest: properties that got us trouble (persistency, reactivity) are also the properties that help us (recyclable metals, batteries).
2. No-chemistry utopias vanish at the sink: water purification, disinfecting wounds and feeding cities are chemistry.
3. The social role named concretely: not a faith but the required technical knowledge for the sustainability problems of the world.

### chem-a14-q05 | points 4 | outcome A14 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A town must choose whether to allow a battery-fabrik (lithium) factory. Write the A14 decision frame in 5 sentences: (a) life-cycle questions to ask the applicant, (b) social gain if it works, (c) one norm for deciding in doubt (a principle), (d) verdict shape you would use in council, (e) how this ties A14's four bullets together.
#### Answer
(a) Ask: source of lithium and cobalt (mining chain), factory emissions (solvents), energy source of the plant, battery end-of-life handling (recycling loop contract) — the life-cycle table. (b) Social gain: local skilled employment plus a direct stake in the climate transition (batteries). (c) Decision principle: proportionality between demonstrable benefit and bounded harm under the applicant's declared emissions and recycling contracts. (d) Verdict shape: "permit, on conditions X (emission limits, guaranteed recycling path, phase-in audit after year 1, remediation bond for soil)". (e) This is A14: the life cycle lists what to ask, alternatives of the same service (gas-fired vs electric cars) inform the benefit, chemistry's social role supplies the why, world problems (climate, raw materials) supply the weight.
#### Solution
1. Frame first: life-cycle questions are the only complete list.
2. Benefit and a principle as reasoned scale (proportionality).
3. Conditional verdict: permits with evidence+review points; that is how industrial chemistry and democratic control actually talk.
