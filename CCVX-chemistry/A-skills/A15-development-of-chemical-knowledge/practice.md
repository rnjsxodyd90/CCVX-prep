# A15 — Development of chemical knowledge: practice

Five original questions on the A15 learning outcomes (how scientific knowledge is developed, used, and how reliable it is), in CCVX style.

### chem-a15-q01 | points 2 | outcome A15 | difficulty easy
#### Question
What questions can scientific researchers pose that other question-forms cannot, and what distinguishes scientific knowledge from justified opinion? Give the classic answer, with one example.
#### Answer
Scientific questions are those that observations and measurements can in principle answer ("how much phosphate enters this river after a fertilizer lorry tips over?"), not preferences or normative questions ("should farming be this way?"); what distinguishes scientific knowledge is support by reproducible evidence that could in principle falsify it (it survives attempted refutations so far). Example: "at 6:00 PM the river's P was 0.9 mg/L vs the 0.2 baseline" is scientific; "the farmer was careless" is an evaluation of a different kind.
#### Solution
1. A question is scientific when observation can settle it — a question of truth, not of choice.
2. Reliability comes from testability: the result can be checked and could have been false.
3. A15's border crossing: "Is X true?" is research; "Is X acceptable?" is sociology/politics — the exam loves the mix-up.

### chem-a15-q02 | points 3 | outcome A15 | difficulty medium
#### Question
The atomic model was rebuilt several times (indivisible balls → nucleus + electrons → shells → orbitals). Use the historical case to explain how scientific knowledge changes yet stays reliable.
#### Answer
The model changed because new observations (cathode rays, gold foil, spectral lines) didn't fit the old one; each replacement kept the total of verified facts while explaining more (the nucleus model kept Thomson's negative charge; quantum shells kept Rutherford's small nucleus), so reliability is cumulative at the data level while the explanatory frame can be overthrown — scientific knowledge on the model level is "best so far", and its use is appropriate with the domain where it worked.
#### Solution
1. Models succeed when they cover old evidence plus new; they fail in one experiment, not wholesale.
2. Thompson, Rutherford, Bohr left the solid facts intact (charges, small nucleus) while pushing precision — science doesn't restart, it refines.
3. Reliability = compatible use within the model's domain: the shell model is good school physics anyway.

### chem-a15-q03 | points 4 | outcome A15 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Science-tech-society interaction: the Haber-Bosch process. Show the three directions: (1) scientific knowledge enabled a technology, (2) that technology changed society, (3) society then steered further scientific development.
#### Answer
1. Thermodynamics of N₂ + 3 H₂ ⇌ 2 NH₃ plus catalyst research (scientific knowledge) enabled the industrial ammonia plant (technology). 2. That technology made artificial fertilizer cheap, feeding billions and restructuring agriculture and wars — it also tipped the nitrogen cycle (society changed). 3. Society's needs and concerns (food security first, later eutrophication and CO₂ emissions of the process) then steered research: catalysts for lower-temperature synthesis, nitrate-runoff technologies, green ammonia from renewable H₂ — society now bends the trajectory of the science.
#### Solution
1. Direction one: academic finding → technological application (Boltzmann-ish, Ostwald/Haber).
2. Direction two: application → social consequences (food boom; energy bill; nitrogen pollution).
3. Direction three: societal problems → new research programmes (green ammonia revolution): the loop never ends.

### chem-a15-q04 | points 4 | outcome A15 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A mega-analysis reports an association linking soft drink consumption with cancer risk. A fellow student says "science proves it." Assess reliability with the logic of how scientific knowledge is generated: name three qualities of evidence you'd demand before lending it strength, and the warranted claim-form.
#### Answer
1. Dose response: does risk rise with intake (and not flat across amounts)? 2. Confounding: were the other life-style differences split out (sugar also links to smoking, obesity)? 3. Replication: do independent groups find the same association with the same method — and do mechanistic data (e.g. on cells) support it? Warranted claim-form: "in these cohorts, intake associated with higher observed rates of cancer by X%; causality is unproven, the association is consistent with a small risk" — no "proves", no "causes", no moral panic.
#### Solution
1. Evidence is graded by: effect consistency, dose response, confounders addressed, replication, mechanisms.
2. The warranted claim is sized to the evidence: association (evidence) vs proof of causation (needs more).
3. Reliability of scientific knowledge, alive: it is not "science says" vs "fake news" — it is scaling with the CAR(*) (consistency-assured replication) of the work.

### chem-a15-q05 | points 4 | outcome A15 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Botany lesson scenario, 1870s: van Helmont's willow experiment "proved" that plant mass comes from water (he weighed only soil and water). Analyse science-development: (a) what was the scientific mistake, (b) which experiment later corrected the picture, (c) what does this say about trusting results.
#### Answer
(a) Van Helmont measured only soil and water and ignored air: he missed that a 164-lb-grown willow also exchanged gases with the atmosphere — an off-balance-sheet input (oxygen in, CO₂ in), so "weight of soil stable + water in" seemed to explain the mass. (b) Priestly and later Ingenhousz showed by gas-exchange work that plants release O₂ in light and fix carbon from air: the mass is largely from CO₂ — not water. (c) Scientific lesson: a conclusion is only as reliable as its bookkeeping — everything that can cross the boundary must be counted (systems of A13: mass balance); and even measurements taken with great care speak for the model of their era, not for eternity.
#### Solution
1. His error: boundary drawing; air was invisible and so unmeasured — not bad faith, bad system choice.
2. The correction: gas-analysis experiments (Priestley, Ingenhousz) caught the carbon flowing through leaves.
3. Development of knowledge: precision in measurement closes old tracks only as far as the chosen boundary — balance the system, recheck with a different method, and the model gets replaced.
