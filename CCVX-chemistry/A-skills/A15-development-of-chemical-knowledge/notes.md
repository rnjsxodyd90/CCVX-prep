## Knowledge development

- Knowledge development (*kennisvorming*) is how scientific knowledge comes about. Observations raise a question; a hypothesis or model offers an explanation; predictions from it are tested in experiments; the results support or refute it; and the model is kept, adjusted or replaced. Results become accepted knowledge only after other scientists have checked them, through peer review and by repeating the experiments. *(CvTE A15.1)*
- Scientists can only ask questions that observations or measurements can answer: what happens, how much, why, what if. Whether something should be allowed is a question for society, not for science alone (see [A09](../A09-evaluation-and-judgment/notes.md)).
- Reliable answers come from controlled experiments, repeated measurements, methods described so that others can check them, and independent studies that agree ([A05](../A05-research/notes.md)).
- Example, the atomic model ([sb11](../../0-basic-knowledge/03-atomic-structure/notes.md), [B01](../../B-substances-and-materials/B01-particle-models/notes.md)):
  - Dalton (about 1805): matter consists of indivisible atoms, each element has its own kind of atom, and compounds are fixed combinations of atoms. This explained why compounds always have the same mass ratio.
  - Thomson discovered the electron in 1897, so atoms can't be indivisible. His model (1904) had electrons in a sphere of positive charge.
  - Rutherford (1911) explained experiments in which α particles were shot at thin gold foil: most passed straight through, but a few bounced back. So almost all the mass and the positive charge must sit in a tiny nucleus.
  - Bohr (1913) put the electrons in shells with fixed energies, which explained the line spectrum of hydrogen. The shell model is still used to explain valence electrons.
  - Each model was changed because new observations didn't fit the old one, yet older models stay useful within their range of validity ([A07](../A07-modelling/notes.md)).
- **Trap:** a theory that has passed many tests is well supported, not proven; one reproducible result that contradicts it can force a change. And a scientific theory is a tested explanation, not a guess.

## Application of knowledge

- Application of knowledge (*toepassen van kennis*) is about how knowledge is used. Science produces knowledge, technology applies it in products and processes, and society sets the demand, pays for research, makes rules and lives with the results. The interaction (*wisselwerking*) runs both ways: needs in society drive research, new technology such as better instruments makes new research possible, and new applications raise new questions for society. *(CvTE A15.2)*
- Example 1, the ammonia synthesis: N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) ⇌ 2 NH₃(g). Around 1900 natural sources of nitrogen fertiliser were limited. Fritz Haber found how to make ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen with a catalyst at high pressure, and Carl Bosch scaled the process up to industrial size before the First World War. Synthetic fertiliser now feeds a large part of the world's population, but the same ammonia was also used to make explosives during that war, and the process uses a lot of energy, mostly from natural gas ([A13](../A13-systems-change-and-energy/notes.md), [A14](../A14-sustainability/notes.md)).
- Example 2, CFCs and the ozone layer. From the 1930s, CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) such as CCl₂F₂ were used as coolants in refrigerators, and later also as propellants in spray cans, because they are non-toxic and non-flammable. In 1974 Molina and Rowland predicted that CFCs reach the stratosphere, where UV light splits off chlorine atoms: CCl₂F₂ → CClF₂• + Cl•.
  - A chlorine atom then destroys ozone in a chain reaction: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, followed by ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂.
  - Net: O₃ + O → 2 O₂. The Cl• is formed again, so it acts as a catalyst and one chlorine atom can destroy many ozone molecules ([C04](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C04-reaction-kinetics/notes.md)).
  - In 1985 the ozone hole over Antarctica was reported. In 1987 countries agreed in the Montreal Protocol to cut the production of CFCs, and later amendments turned that into a complete phase-out; the ozone layer is now slowly recovering. The replacements (HFCs) contain no chlorine and don't harm the ozone layer, but they are strong greenhouse gases, so their use is now being reduced as well.
- The CFC story shows the whole interaction: a technology that seemed safe had an effect nobody foresaw, science detected and explained it, and society acted through an international treaty.

## The influence of science and technology

- The influence of science and technology (*de invloed van natuurwetenschap en techniek*): draw conclusions about a scientific question and relate them to the reliability (*betrouwbaarheid*) of the knowledge behind them: how much evidence there is, how well it has been tested, how large the uncertainty is, and whether independent studies agree. Science never gives complete certainty, but well-established knowledge such as the law of conservation of mass is far more certain than the result of a single new study. *(CvTE A15.3)*
- Example: for decades noble gases were thought to form no compounds, because of their full outer shell. In 1962 the first xenon compounds were made, such as XeF₄. The conclusion "noble gases never react" had to be revised, and the octet rule turned out to be a useful model with limits.
- Example: the case against CFCs grew stronger over time, from a prediction (1974) to measurements of the ozone hole (1985) and of chlorine compounds in the stratosphere. Independent lines of evidence pointing the same way made the conclusion reliable enough to act on.
- An observed link between two things, such as a substance in the environment and a health effect, doesn't by itself prove that one causes the other; a mechanism and controlled studies are needed as well.
- **Trap:** treating one study as settled fact, or dismissing well-tested knowledge because "science is never certain".
