# A11 — Context and concepts: practice

Five original questions on the A11 learning outcome (use chemical concepts in daily, professional and scientific contexts; predict, estimate and calculate), in CCVX style.

### chem-a11-q01 | points 3 | outcome A11 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Context daily life: a 0.33 L can of an energy drink states "sugar 11 g per 100 mL". Estimate quickly how many grams of sugar you drink with the can, and how many sugar cubes of 4 g that is. Show the estimation, not only the result.
#### Answer
36.3 g ≈ 36 g; about 9 cubes.
#### Solution
1. Sugar = 11 g per 100 mL → 0.33 L = 330 mL → 3.3 × 11 = 36.3 g.
2. Cubes: 36.3 / 4 = 9.1 → about 9 cubes.
3. This is the "predict, estimate, calculate" pattern of A11: convert once, estimate honestly, and keep the channel of the estimation visible.

### chem-a11-q02 | points 3 | outcome A11 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Context professional: swimming-pool staff measure pool water with a photometer using a calibration line (absorbance vs [free chlorine]). What is a calibration line, and why would you measure it again tomorrow even if nothing in the optical machine looks different?
#### Answer
A calibration line is the measured relation between absorbance and known concentrations of standard solutions used to convert a sample's absorbance into a concentration. You measure it fresh because lamp intensity, filter ageing, reagent freshness and temperature drift daily — the calibration is tied to today's machine and reagents, not to last week's.
#### Solution
1. The calibration line converts the machine's signal into a concentration (A10 item in context).
2. It is "of today": instruments drift and reagents age; a new day = a new calibration in professional practice.
3. Same idea as A08's instruments and the A05 blank/standard — in context: daily checks keep today's numbers comparable.

### chem-a11-q03 | points 4 | outcome A11 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Estimate like a chemist. Your house has a solar water heating panel "of 1 m² with 40% efficiency" facing south, irradiated by about 1 kW m⁻² at summer noon. Estimate the rough heat per hour into the water and, with 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹, the mass of water it could heat by 20 K in an hour; do the calculation as the syllabus does it (in context).
#### Answer
0.4 kW = 0.4 kJ s⁻¹ per hour = 0.4 × 3600 = 1440 kJ h⁻¹ = 1.44·10⁶ J; divided by (4.18 × 20) = 83.6 J g⁻¹ gives about 1.7·10⁴ g ≈ 17 kg of water heated by 20 K in one noon hour.
#### Solution
1. Power available: 1 kW m⁻² × 1 m² × 0.40 = 0.4 kW.
2. Energy per hour: 0.4 × 3600 = 1440 kJ = 1.44·10⁶ J.
3. Q = m c ΔT → m = Q / (c ΔT) = 1.44·10⁶ / (4.18 × 20) = 1.44·10⁶ / 83.6 ≈ 1.7·10⁴ g = 17 kg.
4. In-context estimate: honest about the roughness (noon, 1 kW m⁻² only at peak), and conceptually right — the A11 calculation channel.

### chem-a11-q04 | points 4 | outcome A11 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A newspaper headline says a solar plant produces "a continuous 100 MW, enough for 100,000 households". Assess this with numbers (continuous means: assume 5,000 kWh per household per year and a capacity factor of 15% for Dutch solar), and draw the conclusion a scientist would draw.
#### Answer
At capacity factor 15%, 100 MW peak delivers on average 15 MW = 15,000 kW; per year: 15,000 × 8760 = 1.31·10⁸ kWh. At 5,000 kWh per household per year that supplies 1.31·10⁸ / 5,000 = 2.6·10⁴ ≈ 26,000 households — about a quarter of the newspaper's claim. Conclusion: the headline mixes peak power with continuous power; with Dutch sun, 100 MWp (peak) covers ≈ 26,000 household-years of electricity, not 100,000.
#### Solution
1. The recurring trap: a solar nameplate is rated at peak (maximum) output; continuous output costs the capacity factor.
2. With 8760 h per year: 15 MW average × 8760 h = 1.31·10⁸ kWh.
3. Divided by 5,000 kWh household use: ≈ 26,000 households — reality check beats repeating the marketing number (A11 predicts and calculates in social context).

### chem-a11-q05 | points 4 | outcome A11 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Front-page discussion: "A single washing cycle with 90 °C costs a household 25 cents in gas/electricity." Estimate with the A11 style whether this is defensible, using realistic numbers (heating 10 L of water from 15 °C to 90 °C; efficiency of the boiler 80%; Dutch energy price ≈ 0.25 €/kWh-equivalent for both carriers), and score how reliable the 25-cent figure is.
#### Answer
Heat: 10 kg × 4.18 × 75 = 3.14·10³ kJ = 3135 kJ = 3135/3600 = 0.87 kWh(from water); at 80% efficiency: 0.87/0.8 = 1.09 kWh. Cost: 1.09 × €0.25 = €0.27. Assessment: 25 cents is defensible in order of magnitude (± 10 cents with the assumed water volume, boiler efficiency and tariff), but rest on all three assumptions; for 8 L of water at 60 °C it is one third.
#### Solution
1. Heat water: m c ΔT with 75 K difference.
2. Convert and correct for efficiency: ≈ 1.09 kWh.
3. Price: ≈ €0.27.
4. Verdict: headline at the right scale but assumption-heavy; the reliability statement ("± x cents under what variation") is exactly what A11-style honesty in public numbers looks like.
