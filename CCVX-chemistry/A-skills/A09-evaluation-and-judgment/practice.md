# A9 — Evaluation and judgment: practice

Five original questions on the A9 learning outcome (reasoned judgment, distinguishing scientific arguments, normative social considerations and personal views), in CCVX style.

### chem-a09-q01 | points 3 | outcome A9 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Classify each sentence as a scientific argument, a normative social consideration or a personal view:
1. "Chlorine gas is acutely toxic to lung tissue at low ppm."
2. "A city shall not place a chlorine warehouse next to a school."
3. "I would never work in a chlorine plant myself."
#### Answer
1. Scientific argument; 2. normative social consideration; 3. personal view.
#### Solution
1. Sentence 1 is testable (toxicity vs measured concentration): science.
2. Sentence 2 carries "shall": a choice about what is acceptable — normative (not true/false, but a judgment in a set of social rules).
3. Sentence 3 expresses the speaker's own preference: personal.
4. A9 judgement keeps the three apart — the exam expects you to name what kind of support a recommendation leans on.

### chem-a09-q02 | points 3 | outcome A9 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A chemical plant's chief says: "Our chimney emissions are below the legal limit, so the plant is clean." Give one scientific counterquestion and one normative counterquestion, and show the difference.
#### Answer
Scientific: "What is the uncertainty of the emission measurement, and are the pollutants that are measured the worst ones?" (is the claim true and complete). Normative: "Is 'within the limit' a sufficient definition of 'clean' for the neighbouring residents?" (what society should accept as clean).
#### Solution
1. The scientific question tests the empirical claim (accuracy, coverage of the measurement): normative questions cannot touch that, but the "clean" conclusion needs more than accuracy.
2. The normative question asks what should count: legal limits are set by compromise, not by nature; an emission within limits can still be judged unacceptable by the locals.
3. The point: "below the limit" is a fact; "clean" is a valuation — A9 is the habit of not letting one suggest the other automatically.

### chem-a09-q03 | points 4 | outcome A9 | difficulty medium
#### Question
ENV-group claims: "All synthetic substances are unsafe and should be banned; nature's own are safe." Pick this claim apart with two scientific counter-arguments and one normative consideration that could still defend part of the intuition behind it.
#### Answer
Scientific 1: toxicity is a property of the substance's structure and dose, not of its birth certificate: botulinum toxin (fully natural) is one of the most acutely toxic substances known, while many synthetics (e.g. the inert polymer PTFE) are practically harmless at normal exposure. Scientific 2: dose makes the poison (Paracelsus principle): every substance is safe below some exposure and unsafe above — "safe/unsafe" without dose is meaningless. Normative: a stricter principle of precaution for new synthetic substances is a defensible social choice (society may demand more proof before approving novel entities than for long-familiar ones).
#### Solution
1. Counterexamples collapse the universal claim (botulinum toxin; refined natural carcinogens; harmless synthetics).
2. The dose-response stating replaces the safe/unsafe dichotomy.
3. The normative residue: precaution as a social rule (which is a choice about unknown risks, not a scientific finding) — a sound A9 verdict permits it while denying the pseudo-science around it.

### chem-a09-q04 | points 4 | outcome A9 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A board must decide whether a chlorine-based bleach may still be sold in supermarkets. Put forward a two-sided judgment (a reasoned opinion, not a vote): list one scientific pro and con, then the normative weigh-in you'd propose, and form the conclusion you would defend.
#### Answer
Scientific pro: properly diluted chlorine bleach is a fast, broad disinfectant proven in health practice (and its use in low concentrations in drinking water saved large numbers of lives). Scientific con: misuse creates chlorine gas and chlorinated organics (accident profile in households), and stains/damages. Normative weigh-in: a public sale with clear pictograms and caps is proportionate for a substance with thousands of safe daily uses — a ban falls disproportionately on ordinary households that disinfect reliably. Conclusion: keep it on sale under the current labelling/dilution rules, with an information campaign; a ban is not proportionate to the documented scale of harm vs benefit.
#### Solution
1. Pro and con are grounded arguments with evidence, not slogans.
2. The normative layer is spelled out: proportionality between burden (damage by misuse) and benefit (public health / hygiene).
3. The verdict names conditions and why the counter-position loses — the shape of a reasoned judgment.

### chem-a09-q05 | points 4 | outcome A9 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The newspaper headline reads: "Study shows that organic food contains fewer pesticides: eat organic." Respond with a four-step evaluation: (1) what the study can and cannot show, (2) what extra evidence you would demand before acting, (3) one direction a normative consideration adds (not scientific), (4) your final structured judgment.
#### Answer
1. It shows lower measured pesticide residues in the organic sample set; it does not show that the difference is health-relevant (magnitude orders below legal limits?), nor that organic is better on other axes (cost, land use, food safety of natural toxins), nor that the samples are representative. 2. Demand data: actual detected levels versus the health-based limits (do the measured levels even matter?), plus outcome data at human-relevant exposure (is there any measurable health difference at these levels?), from independent replications. 3. Normative: if a consumer prefers to pay for a precaution margin (and for the environmental profile of organic farming) even without a proven health gain, that is a legitimate value-laden choice. 4. Judgment: scientifically the margin at these residue levels is small and the evidence for health benefit is weak; normatively the preference is defensible; "eat organic" as an imperative overstates the science — advertise it as a choice backed by values, not commanded by data.
#### Solution
1. Distinguish the scope: measured residue difference ≠ health difference.
2. Ask for dose vs ADI/MRL and outcome data: the missing link between measurement and claim.
3. Name the normative extra (precaution value, environmental care) as such.
4. Conclusion includes all three layers: what is shown, what is not shown, and what one may still choose — the complete A9 form.
