# A12 — Structure and properties: practice

Five original questions on the A12 learning outcomes (relate macroscopic properties to meso, micro and submicro structure, and vice versa), in CCVX style.

### chem-a12-q01 | points 3 | outcome A12 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Name for each observation the structural level it belongs to (submicro = electron distribution; micro = particles; meso = assemblies; macro = material/sample): (a) the violet colour of potassium permanganate; (b) the dipole-moment of a water molecule; (c) the cloud of microdroplets in mayonnaise; (d) a diamond scratching glass.
#### Answer
(a) Meso–macro: the colour of the KMnO₄ solution (sample level). (b) Micro/submicro: the molecule's shape and electron distribution (one H₂O molecule). (c) Meso level: oil droplets as assemblies in water (invisible to the eye but larger than single molecules). (d) Macro-meso: diamond's wear/hardness as material property (meso for its crystal lattice).
#### Solution
1. Property by level: colour and sample properties are macro; single-molecule charge distribution is micro/submicro; droplet assemblies live at meso.
2. The A12 habit is to speak of one observation at one structural level at a time, and to forbid sloppy scale-jumping.

### chem-a12-q02 | points 3 | outcome A12 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain from each structure the macroscopic property: (1) olive oil is liquid at 20 °C while lard is solid: (2) the lactic acid in sour milk forms hydrogen bridges with water.
#### Answer
1. Olive oil's many cis double bonds kink its chains (micro structure), so molecules can't pack closely (meso crystal structure), giving far lower melting point: liquid oil (macro property). 2. The –OH and –COOH groups form hydrogen bridges with water (submicro bond + micro molecule), so lactic acid mixes with water (macro: no separation in the milk).
#### Solution
1. Chain kink → weak meso-crystallisation → liquid: classic three-level argument for fats (see B04).
2. Hydrophilic groups → bridging with water at the micro level → miscibility at the macro level.
3. Format you should reproduce in the exam: structure (micro/meso/sub) → consequence → macroscopic property, in that order.

### chem-a12-q03 | points 4 | outcome A12 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Choose the structural level responsible for each of these and explain the mechanism: (1) steel is harder than pure iron; (2) conductance of copper higher than brass; (3) vulcanised rubber snapping back to shape.
#### Answer
1. Meso: second atoms in the lattice (lattice errors) hinder plane sliding — steel's mesoscale defect structure makes a harder metal. 2. Submicro/meso: delocalised electrons (submicro) move through an undistorted lattice; the zinc distortions in brass scatter electron waves, so brass conducts less well than copper. 3. Meso: crosslinks (–S–S– bridges) between the rubber chains tie the chains: stretching lets chains slip within bounds but the crosslinks pull them back — macroscopic elasticity.
#### Solution
1. Steel's hardness reason sits at meso level (lattice errors), not at "atoms".
2. Brass's lower conductivity walks down to submicro electron mobility across the mesoscale distortions.
3. Rubber's elastic return is a mesoscale meshwork effect of crosslinks.
4. A12's key practice: name the level first, then reason within it — not "the atom does it" for everything.

### chem-a12-q04 | points 4 | outcome A12 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A detergent removes the grease stain from your shirt, although grease and water don't mix. Use micro/meso structures to explain the whole chain: grease micelle formation, why the grease then moves with the water, and what happens at rinsing.
#### Answer
The detergent molecule has an apolar tail cutting into the grease droplet and a polar head sticking out into the water: the micelles (meso structure: droplets with detergent shell) isolate the grease from the water (macro: "the water carries the grease away") because the polar heads hydrogen-bridge with water. At rinsing the replaced micelles wash away with the detergent — the meso-assembly is stable only in the detergent-water mixture, and fresh water carries them off before they can re-attach.
#### Solution
1. Grease in water → detergent tails into grease, polar heads outward: micelle (meso-level shell structure around each droplet).
2. The shell is hydrophilic: the wrapped droplet dissolves in the water phase (macro: the stain goes).
3. Rinsing: dilution keeps the micelles stable and floating away; washed-off before re-deposition.
4. A12 links amphiphilic micro structure (detergent molecule) → meso micelle assembly → macro cleanliness.

### chem-a12-q05 | points 4 | outcome A12 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Two oppositely shaped material decisions are taken from the same element: (1) window glass is brittle, (2) but a baker also makes spun-sugar decorations that shatter only after days because the sugar "glass" slowly crystallises out of its amorphous state. Explain from the structure / structure classification: what both share and what separates them, in terms of amorphous and crystalline structure, and how the timescale enters.
#### Answer
Common: both are "glasses" = amorphous solids (no crystal lattice; atoms/molecules frozen where they were), which gives both their brittleness (no slip planes: shatter instead of bend). Differently: window glass amorphous SiO₂ network is frozen by temperature (cooler than its crystallisation point, practically stuck at room temperature); sugar glass is amorphous only for a while — room temperature is warm enough for its molecules to reorder into crystals (days), after which it "sugars" and shatters. Time enters as crystallisation kinetics: the amorphous state is metastable and its lifetime depends on molecular mobility (governed by the glass-transition temperature).
#### Solution
1. Amorphous = no crystal order; mechanically rigid because frozen, brittle because there are no slip systems (contrast metals).
2. The two glasses differ in the distance between service temperature and their glass-transition temperature: silica far above usage, sugar close by — so sugar crystallises while you watch, glass waits centuries.
3. A12 reasoning: meso atom/ion network organisation (amorphous vs crystalline) + a kinetic time scale → macroscopic useable properties over time.
