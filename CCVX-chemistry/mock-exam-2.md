### chem-mock2-q01 | points 12 | outcome C5/C2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 1 — Ammonia from a scrubber**

A factory absorbs ammonia from air into water; the resulting aqueous ammonia is reused as a cleaning reagent. T = 298 K.

a (4p). The solution contains 0.0500 mol NH₃ per litre (K_b = 1.8·10⁻⁵). Calculate the pH of this solution.

b (4p). For quality control, 25.00 mL of the solution is titrated with 0.0400 M hydrochloric acid. Calculate the volume (in mL) of acid needed to reach the equivalence point.

c (4p). The solution is diluted ten times. Show, by computing the degree of ionisation (the fraction of NH₃ molecules that reacted with water) before and after dilution, how dilution changes it, and explain the direction with the equilibrium expression.
#### Answer
a. pH = 10.98
b. 31.3 mL
c. At 0.0500 mol L⁻¹: 1.9%; at 0.00500 mol L⁻¹: 6.0%. Dilution raises the degree of ionisation.
#### Solution
1. a: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻; x = [OH⁻] = √(K_b × c) = √(1.8·10⁻⁵ × 0.0500) = 9.5·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ (check: x ≪ c). pOH = 3.02 → pH = 14.00 − 3.02 = 10.98.
2. b: n(NH₃) = 25.00 mL × 0.0500 mol L⁻¹ = 1.25 mmol = n(H₃O⁺); V = 1.25 mmol / 0.0400 mol L⁻¹ = 31.3 mL.
3. c: dilute: c = 0.00500 mol L⁻¹: x = √(1.8·10⁻⁵ × 0.00500) = 3.0·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹. Fractions: 9.5·10⁻⁴/0.0500 = 1.9%; 3.0·10⁻⁴/0.00500 = 6.0%. (The exact quadratic route on the diluted case gives x = 2.9·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ and a fraction of 5.8%.) K_b = x²/(c − x): dividing every concentration by 10 makes the quotient smaller (one power fewer in the denominator than in the numerator), so more NH₃ must react until Q = K again: the degree of ionisation rises.

### chem-mock2-q02 | points 13 | outcome C1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 2 — Refining copper**

Crude copper (98.0% Cu, with Ni, Zn, Ag and Au) is refined electrolytically: the crude copper is the positive electrode, a sheet of pure copper the negative electrode, in a copper(II) sulfate bath. Relevant V₀ values (Binas): Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76 V; Ni²⁺/Ni −0.26 V; Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34 V; Ag⁺/Ag +0.80 V.

a (3p). Give the half-reactions at the two electrodes for the copper itself, and state which way the electrons flow in the external circuit.

b (4p). Use the V₀ values to explain what happens to Ni, Zn, Ag and Au during the electrolysis (where does each end up and why).

c (3p). Explain why the copper deposited on the negative electrode is very pure, although Ni²⁺ and Zn²⁺ accumulate in the bath.

d (3p). An anode of 100 kg crude copper (98.0% Cu) is completely used up. Give the mass balance: mass of deposited pure copper, and the fate of the other 2.0 kg.
#### Answer
a. Positive electrode: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻; negative electrode: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻ → Cu(s); electrons flow from the impure anode to the cathode through the external circuit.
b. Zn and Ni (stronger reductors, lower V₀ than Cu) dissolve with the copper as Zn²⁺ and Ni²⁺ into the bath; Ag and Au (weaker reductors, higher V₀) do not dissolve and fall off as anode sludge.
c. At the negative electrode the strongest oxidator present is reduced: Cu²⁺ (+0.34 V) beats Ni²⁺ (−0.26) and Zn²⁺ (−0.76), so as long as enough Cu²⁺ is present, only copper deposits; Ni and Zn stay in solution.
d. 98 kg of pure copper is deposited; the 2.0 kg of impurities ends up in the bath (Zn²⁺, Ni²⁺ ions) and in the anode sludge (Ag, Au).
#### Solution
1. a: the cells' polarity is driven by the external source: positive electrode = where oxidation is forced. Copper dissolves at +, plates at −.
2. b: ordering by V₀ / reductor strength: metals more reactive than Cu (Zn, Ni) also give up electrons and dissolve; metals more noble (Ag, Au, the "edelere" metals) cannot react as oxidators win there — they drop as sludge.
3. c: selectivity through potential ordering: Cu²⁺ is the strongest oxidator in the bath, so it wins the electrons; others deposit only when Cu²⁺ would run out.
4. d: copper mass is conserved: 98 kg dissolves and deposits (the bath as medium); impurities split by the rule of b into solution or sludge.

### chem-mock2-q03 | points 12 | outcome C1/F3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 3 — A hydrogen delivery van**

A delivery van carries 5.0 kg of hydrogen in compressed tanks feeding an alkaline fuel cell.

a (2p). Give the half-reaction of hydrogen as a reductor in alkaline medium.

b (4p). The combustion energy of hydrogen is 1.42·10⁵ J per gram. The fuel cell converts 55% of this energy into electrical energy. Calculate the electrical energy available from the 5.0 kg of hydrogen.

c (3p). The van uses on average 0.72 MJ of electrical energy per kilometre. Calculate the practical range on one tank.

d (3p). Half-reaction-based comparison: in a fuel cell the electrons of the fuel travel through the external circuit and do work; in a hydrogen combustion engine the same reaction just heats gas. Explain where the fuel cell's efficiency advantage comes from, in the language of energy conversions.
#### Answer
a. H₂ + 2 OH⁻ → 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻
b. 3.9·10² MJ
c. about 540 km
d. A fuel cell converts chemical energy straight into electrical energy (electrons made to flow through the circuit), while combustion first becomes heat and heat→work conversion loses a large fraction to low-temperature heat; fewer conversion steps, less loss.
#### Solution
1. a: alkaline medium: H₂ + 2 OH⁻ → 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻.
2. b: 5.0·10³ g × 1.42·10⁵ J g⁻¹ = 7.1·10⁸ J chemical. Electrical: 0.55 × 7.1·10⁸ = 3.9·10⁸ J = 3.9·10² MJ.
3. c: range = 3.9·10² / 0.72 ≈ 5.4·10² km.
4. d: combustion: chemical → heat → (partly) mechanical/electrical with big loss; fuel cell: chemical → electrical in one redox step across the membrane (ions through the electrolyte, electrons outside), so quality-of-energy losses are much smaller (see C06, C09).

### chem-mock2-q04 | points 10 | outcome C2/C1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 4 — Vitamin C in rosehip drink**

25.00 mL of filtered rosehip drink is mixed with 20.00 mL of 0.0100 M iodine solution (excess iodine). The ascorbic acid is oxidised: C₆H₈O₆ + I₂ → C₆H₆O₆ + 2 H⁺ + 2 I⁻. The iodine left over is titrated with 0.0200 M sodium thiosulfate solution: I₂ + 2 S₂O₃²⁻ → 2 I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻. End point at 12.40 mL of thiosulfate.

a (4p). Show that the equation for the oxidation of ascorbic acid is balanced, and state the mole ratios I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ and I₂ : C₆H₈O₆.
b (4p). Calculate the molarity of ascorbic acid in the rosehip drink.
c (2p). Express this content in mg ascorbic acid per 100 mL (M = 176.12 g mol⁻¹).
#### Answer
a. Charge balance 0 = +2 −2 and atom balance hold (C 6, H 8, O 6, I 2); the ratios are I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2 and I₂ : C₆H₈O₆ = 1 : 1.
b. 3.04·10⁻³ mol L⁻¹
c. 53.5 mg per 100 mL
#### Solution
1. a: charge: 0 = +2 −2 ✓; atoms: C 6, H: 8 = 6 + 2 ✓, O 6 = 6, I 2 = 2 ✓. I₂ : S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 : 2; I₂ : C₆H₈O₆ = 1 : 1.
2. b: added I₂: 20.00·10⁻³ L × 0.0100 mol L⁻¹ = 2.000·10⁻⁴ mol. Left over: S₂O₃²⁻ used = 12.40·10⁻³ × 0.0200 = 2.480·10⁻⁴ mol → I₂ left = 1/2 × 2.480·10⁻⁴ = 1.240·10⁻⁴ mol. Consumed by ascorbic acid: 2.000·10⁻⁴ − 1.240·10⁻⁴ = 7.60·10⁻⁵ mol = n(ascorbic acid) in 25.00 mL. c = 7.60·10⁻⁵ / 25.00·10⁻³ = 3.04·10⁻³ mol L⁻¹.
3. c: per 100 mL: 4 × 7.60·10⁻⁵ = 3.04·10⁻⁴ mol; × 176.12 g mol⁻¹ = 5.35·10⁻² g = 53.5 mg.

### chem-mock2-q05 | points 12 | outcome D3/B1/E1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 5 — PLA from potato waste**

Potato-starch waste is fermented to lactic acid (2-hydroxypropanoic acid, CH₃–CH(OH)–COOH), which is then polymerised to PLA: n CH₃CH(OH)COOH → –[CH(CH₃)–COO]–ₙ + n H₂O.

a (2p). Mark the asymmetric carbon atom in lactic acid and give its four groups.
b (3p). Glucose is converted to lactic acid in the ratio 1 glucose → 2 lactic acid. Starting from 1.00 kg of glucose, show (without weighing anything new) that about 1.00 kg of lactic acid is the maximum.
c (4p). From that 1.00 kg of glucose, calculate the maximum mass of PLA obtainable (M of a lactic acid unit in the chain = 72.06 g mol⁻¹).
d (3p). Calculate the atom economy of the polymerisation step (monomer → polymer + water), and name the by-product.
#### Answer
a. The middle C carries CH₃, H, OH, COOH — asymmetric.
b. 1.00 kg glucose = 5.55 mol → 11.1 mol lactic acid × 90.08 g mol⁻¹ ≈ 1.00 kg.
c. 800 g
d. 80.0%; by-product is water (n × 18.016 g per n monomer units).
#### Solution
1. a: C2 carries CH₃, H, OH, COOH — four different groups, hence the mirror-image forms.
2. b: n(glucose) = 1000/180.16 = 5.55 mol → n(lactic acid) = 11.1 mol; m = 11.1 × 90.08 = 1.00·10³ g (mass is conserved: the fermentation only rearranges the atoms).
3. c: n(units) = 11.1 mol; m(PLA) = 11.1 × 72.06 = 8.00·10² g = 800 g.
4. d: AE = 72.06/90.08 × 100% = 80.0%; H₂O is split off in the condensation. (The full-process economy, glucose to PLA, is also 800/1000 = 80.0%.)

### chem-mock2-q06 | points 13 | outcome C5/C4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
**Question 6 — Ammonia plant monitoring**

An ammonia plant runs N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) ⇌ 2 NH₃(g) (ΔE = −0.92·10⁵ J per mol of reaction as written) over an iron catalyst. The operators analyse a sample from the outlet of a reactor at T = 723 K and find [N₂] = 0.160, [H₂] = 0.600, [NH₃] = 0.0400 mol L⁻¹.

a (4p). Calculate K at 723 K from these equilibrium concentrations, with its unit.

b (3p). A later sample shows [N₂] = 0.100, [H₂] = 0.280, [NH₃] = 0.0500 mol L⁻¹. Determine by calculating Q whether the system is at equilibrium, and if not, which direction it reacts.

c (3p). Explain why K gets smaller when the temperature is raised, and why the plant still runs at 723 K.

d (3p). Explain the double role of the iron catalyst and of removing NH₃ (condensing it out) in achieving a good production rate per hour.
#### Answer
a. K = 4.63·10⁻² L² mol⁻²
b. Q = 1.14 > K: not at equilibrium; the mixture reacts to the left (NH₃ decomposes back to N₂ and H₂).
c. The forward reaction is exothermic, so raising T lowers K; the plant runs hot because without temperature the rate is far too low — even at a worse equilibrium position (compromise of rate vs position).
d. The catalyst lets the equilibrium be reached fast (lower activation energy, both directions) — it does not change K; condensing NH₃ out of the loop keeps Q < K downstream, so the reactor keeps converting (rate per hour, not yield per pass, is maximised).
#### Solution
1. a: K = [NH₃]²/([N₂][H₂]³) = (0.0400)²/(0.160 × (0.600)³) = 1.60·10⁻³/3.456·10⁻² = 4.63·10⁻²; unit: (mol L⁻¹)²/(mol L⁻¹)⁴ = L² mol⁻².
2. b: Q = (0.0500)²/(0.100 × (0.280)³) = 2.50·10⁻³/2.195·10⁻³ = 1.14. Q (1.14) > K (0.0463): reaction to the left.
3. c: for an exothermic reaction the back reaction is endothermic: heating favours it, so K falls. Still 723 K: the activation energy is high; at low temperature the equilibrium position is excellent but the rate is negligible — the equilibrium yield would never be reached in practice. With the catalyst + heat + continuous removal of NH₃ the plant runs at the best total rate.
4. d: catalyst: lowers Ea, equilibrium reached sooner (nothing to do with K). Condensing NH₃: subtracting product from Q makes Q < K, so the reaction keeps driving right — continuous production with recycled N₂/H₂ (see F01 on recirculation).

### chem-mock2-q07 | points 8 | outcome C5/C1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 7 — Shells in a souring ocean**

Sea water absorbs part of the rising CO₂: the water becomes more acidic.

a (3p). Give the equilibrium equation by which dissolved CO₂ makes sea water acidic (one step), and explain with Q and K why more atmospheric CO₂ means a lower pH.

b (3p). Shells and corals consist of CaCO₃: CaCO₃(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq). Explain with these two equilibria why more acidic water works against shell formation.

c (2p). Explain why cold, deep ocean water stores more CO₂ than warm surface water (use solubility of gases).
#### Answer
a. CO₂(aq) + 2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq); more CO₂ raises [CO₂], so Q < K and the equilibrium shifts right: [H₃O⁺] rises → pH falls.
b. Acid consumes carbonate: H₃O⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O, so [CO₃²⁻] falls; with Q = [Ca²⁺][CO₃²⁻] then smaller than K_s, CaCO₃ dissolves (the equilibrium is driven to the right), so shells cannot form or even dissolve.
c. Gases dissolve better in cold water than in warm water (solubility rises as T falls — Binas table of gas solubilities): cold deep water can hold much more CO₂.
#### Solution
1. a: weak-acid equilibrium of 'H₂CO₃' (K_z = 4.5·10⁻⁷); raised [CO₂] means raised products until Q = K: the new equilibrium has more H₃O⁺ and HCO₃⁻ (and a lower pH).
2. b: sketch the coupling: acid-to-carbonate protonation lowers [CO₃²⁻]; with a lower [CO₃²⁻] the solubility product of CaCO₃ is no longer reached (Q < K_s), so net dissolution — the exact line of ocean-acidification arguments in the literature.
3. c: gas solubility goes up as the temperature goes down (see Binas): high-latitude and deep water sinks CO₂ (which is why the poles are also where acidification shows up first).
