# F3 — Energy conversion: practice

Ten original questions on the F3 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Heats of combustion where given come from C06 (methane: 8.90·10⁵ J mol⁻¹).

### chem-f03-q01 | points 3 | outcome F3.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the equation for the alcoholic fermentation of glucose by yeast and write down why this process is the chemical basis of bioethanol as a fuel part.
#### Answer
C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂ (anaerobic, by yeast enzymes); the ethanol that forms is separated (by distillation) and used as bioethanol blended into petrol, so sugar/starch crops become a renewable fuel.
#### Solution
1. Yeast cells decompose sugar without oxygen: one glucose molecule gives two ethanol and two CO₂ molecules.
2. Fermentation is the biological conversion step; distillation separates the ethanol to fuel grade.
3. Because the feedstock grows back (sugar beet, maize, cane), the ethanol is a biofuel — its carbon cycles on a short time scale (see F3.4).

### chem-f03-q02 | points 3 | outcome F3.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A farmer ferments manure and plant waste in a digester and burns the gas produced in a small engine with a generator. (1) What is the gas called and what does it consist of? (2) Give the two chemical/technological steps of this energy conversion. (3) Name one extra advantage beyond the energy.
#### Answer
1. Biogas: mainly methane CH₄ and carbon dioxide CO₂ (with a little H₂S and water). 2. (a) Anaerobic digestion by bacteria produces the gas; (b) its combustion in the engine converts chemical energy into mechanical/electrical energy (CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O). 3. Waste is treated on site (less manure smell and waste transport), and the digestate goes back to the field as fertiliser.
#### Solution
1. Anaerobic digestion by symbiotic bacteria produces biogas: roughly 50–70% CH₄, the rest mostly CO₂.
2. Energy chain: fermentation → combustion → dynamo (just like a fossil gas plant, but local and biological).
3. Double profit: waste streams are cleaned up while giving fuel, and nutrients stay on the farm — the sustainability point biogas is valued for.

### chem-f03-q03 | points 4 | outcome F3.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Describe the full energy conversion chain of a coal-fired power station, from coal in the special combustion unit to the electricity at the dynamo, and name the three main polluting reaction products of the combustion stage and their origin.
#### Answer
Chemical energy → heat in the boiler (combustion of coal in a specially fired furnace) → steam at high pressure (heat absorbed by water) → mechanical energy in the steam turbine (steam expands) → electrical energy at the dynamo (generator). Pollution: CO₂ (from the carbon of the fuel), SO₂ (from sulfur impurities in the coal) and NOₓ (NO/NO₂, formed from N₂ and O₂ of the air at the high flame temperature).
#### Solution
1. Combustion unit: the chemical energy of the coal becomes heat; a boiler makes high-pressure steam of it.
2. The steam expands through a turbine (heat → motion), which drives the dynamo (motion → electricity); exhausted steam is condensed and returned.
3. Atmospheric pollution at the flame: complete combustion yields CO₂; the sulfur in coal burns to SO₂; the heat itself makes N₂ + O₂ → 2 NO (which air further turns to NO₂): the classic trio coal plants must control (scrubbers, low-NOₓ burners; F3.4 takes the comparison from here).

### chem-f03-q04 | points 3 | outcome F3.3 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain why photosynthesis is called the process that converts light into chemical energy, giving the equation and naming the gas it captures from and releases to the air.
#### Answer
6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂: chlorophyll absorbs light, and the energy is stored as chemical energy in glucose — the gas captured is CO₂ from the air, the gas released is O₂.
#### Solution
1. Plant leaves take up CO₂ from the air and water from the soil; light drops the chemical energy into the product glucose, where it stays stored until the plant (or its eater) releases it by respiration.
2. The by-product O₂ is released to the air — the origin of the oxygen in our atmosphere.
3. So photosynthesis is the base of the food chain and of every biofuel (F3.1): sunlight → plant → fuel.

### chem-f03-q05 | points 4 | outcome F3.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Using the C/H ratio argument, explain why a natural gas power station (fuel CH₄) emits less CO₂ per joule than a coal-fired station (coal taken as carbon C with combustion heat 3.9·10⁵ J per mol C). Methane's combustion heat is 8.90·10⁵ J per mol CH₄.
#### Answer
Per joule: CH₄ gives 1/8.90·10⁵ = 1.12·10⁻⁶ mol CO₂ per J; C gives 1/3.9·10⁵ = 2.6·10⁻⁶ mol CO₂ per J — natural gas about 2.3 times less CO₂ per joule.
#### Solution
1. Burning carbon produces only CO₂ as energy source: C + O₂ → CO₂, 1 mol CO₂ per 3.9·10⁵ J.
2. Burning methane CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O delivers most of its energy also from the H part (which ends as water, not as CO₂): 1 mol CO₂ per 8.90·10⁵ J.
3. Per joule, methane therefore emits 1/8.90·10⁵ = 1.12·10⁻⁶ mol CO₂, coal 1/3.9·10⁵ = 2.6·10⁻⁶ mol — a factor 2.3 less: the lower the C/H ratio of a fuel, the lower its CO₂ per joule (the C/H ratio criterion of F3.4).

### chem-f03-q06 | points 3 | outcome F3.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Where do the NOₓ emissions of a power station come from, although the fuel itself contains almost no nitrogen? Give the reaction and one reason why cars are an even bigger NOₓ source, and why NOₓ is fought even though it is not a greenhouse gas like CO₂.
#### Answer
At the high flame temperature nitrogen and oxygen of the combustion air react: N₂ + O₂ → 2 NO (then 2 NO + O₂ → 2 NO₂). Car engines make the same reaction in a very hot, fast cycle — a huge mobile source. NOₓ matters because it forms acid rain (HNO₃) and photochemical smog and is directly harmful to airways.
#### Solution
1. Air is 78% N₂; above roughly 1300 °C the N≡N bond is broken by collisions and NO forms (called thermal NOₓ / the Zeldovich mechanism).
2. Petrol and diesel engines reach such temperatures in every stroke, multiplied over millions of vehicles: traffic is the larger total NOₓ source.
3. NO then oxidises to NO₂, dissolves in rain as HNO₃ (acidifying soil and waters), and together with sunlight makes ozone/smog — so NOₓ is scrubbed and catalysts fitted (see F3.4's emissions CO₂, NOₓ, SO₂).

### chem-f03-q07 | points 4 | outcome F3.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A hydrogen fuel-cell bus is proposed as the clean solution. (1) Describe the energy conversion in the fuel cell in terms of redox and electrodes. (2) Why is the cell's efficiency potentially higher than burning the same hydrogen in an engine? (3) What is the sustainability reservation about the hydrogen itself?
#### Answer
1. H₂ (reductor) is oxidised at the negative electrode and O₂ (oxidator) is reduced at the positive electrode; the electrons travel through the external circuit (the bus's motor), so chemical energy becomes electrical energy directly. 2. Because the conversion skips the heat engine: a fuel cell turns chemical energy straight into electricity and wastes less as heat, while a combustion engine is capped by the temperature difference it can use. 3. Greenness depends on the hydrogen's source: only "green" hydrogen (electrolysis powered by renewables) is climate-friendly; the usual "grey" route makes it from natural gas, emitting CO₂ at the plant.
#### Solution
1. Negative electrode: H₂ → 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ (acid) or H₂ + 2 OH⁻ → 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻ (alkaline); positive electrode: O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ → 2 H₂O. The ions pass through the membrane/electrolyte, the electrons outside.
2. Combustion first makes heat, then motion, then maybe electricity — each conversion loses part to low-temperature heat; the fuel cell keeps it in one electrochemical step (see C06 q09).
3. The chain matters: if H₂ comes from steam reforming (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3 H₂, then CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂), the upstream CO₂ can exceed the gain — judge by the whole life cycle, as F3.4/F3.5 demand.

### chem-f03-q08 | points 3 | outcome F3.1–F3.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A power station co-fires 20% wood chips with coal. Evaluate the claim "this part of the electricity is CO₂-neutral" with the carbon cycle and with two counter-arguments.
#### Answer
Claim: the wood's carbon was captured by the trees recently and is recaptured by regrowth, so within the cycle it is neutral. Counter-arguments: (1) the fuel chain (felling, chipping, drying, transport) burns fossil fuels; (2) the CO₂ from burning is released now while regrowth takes decades, so there is a temporary extra CO₂ burden — "neutral" only after the cycle has closed years later.
#### Solution
1. Biomass combustion returns to the atmosphere CO₂ that was taken out of it recently by the growing trees: fast-cycle carbon, unlike fossil carbon.
2. Counter 1 (fugitive emissions): harvesting and shipping use diesel; drying uses heat — so the delivered fuel is not 100% neutral.
3. Counter 2 (timing): the molecule comes back only when the next trees have grown: several decades of extra atmospheric CO₂, which matters in the critical period. Honest schemes account for these and for the alternative uses of the land (G02/G04 go deeper).

### chem-f03-q09 | points 4 | outcome F3.4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A city replaces a condensing power station by a combined heat and power (CHP) plant that also delivers district heating. The condensing unit converted 40% of the fuel energy into electricity; the CHP unit converts 35% into electricity and 45% into useful heat. (1) Compare their total efficiencies. (2) Explain why CHP is more sustainable. (3) Name one practical condition that limits CHP.
#### Answer
1. Total: condensing 40%, CHP 80%. 2. The condensing station dumps 60% of every joule as low-temperature heat into cooling water (lost), while CHP uses the same joule twice — 35% as electricity and 45% as heat for houses — so less fuel per useful joule and less CO₂ and cooling-water load per service delivered. 3. It needs a nearby heat demand (a district that accepts the hot water): without heat consumers close enough, the heat has nowhere to go (it cannot be transported far without large losses) and the advantage vanishes.
#### Solution
1. CHP (warmte-kracht) turns a larger fraction of the fuel into useful energy: 35% + 45% = 80%.
2. Same efficiency also means fewer emissions per useful output and less spent cooling water at the river — the chain of environmental factors in F3.4 (fuel delivery, storage, cooling water).
3. Heat can only travel a few kilometres efficiently, so CHP economics depend on a city or greenhouse horticulture next door; otherwise the heat is again thrown away — technology never rescues a bad match with demand.

### chem-f03-q10 | points 3 | outcome F3.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
For a city delivery van fleet, compare batteries versus hydrogen fuel cells across the F3.5 sustainability list: production, delivery/storage and conversion. Give a reasoned recommendation for one of them.
#### Answer
Batteries: charged from the grid (electricity production decides the footprint), heavy per kWh stored but very efficient (little loss between socket and wheel). Hydrogen: produced at a plant (only green H₂ is clean), needs compression/storage infrastructure, and its conversion chain (electrolysis → compression → fuel cell) loses more energy than a battery loses in charge/discharge; recommendation for a city fleet: batteries, for the superior total efficiency and existing grid; hydrogen reserved for long-haul/heavy duty.
#### Solution
1. Production: battery electricity from the grid (mix), hydrogen from reforming (CO₂ emission) or electrolysis (power-dependent).
2. Delivery/storage: batteries are integrated but add vehicle mass; H₂ needs new fuelling stations and high-pressure tanks.
3. Conversion: a battery + motor wastes only ~10–20%; H₂ chain wastes ~60–70% end-to-end. Judged by total efficiency, city vans go battery; hydrogen is defended where weight and refuelling time beat efficiency — this trade-off is the F3.5 reasoning target.
