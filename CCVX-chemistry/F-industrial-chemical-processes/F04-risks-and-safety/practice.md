# F4 — Risks and safety: practice

Six original questions on the F4 learning outcomes, in CCVX style.

### chem-f04-q01 | points 3 | outcome F4 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain the difference between risk and probability in industrial chemistry, using the storage of 500 m³ of liquefied ammonia as the example, and name one measure that reduces each of them separately.
#### Answer
Risk = probability × effect: probability is how likely an accident (e.g. a tank leak) is; the effect is how large the damage would be (an ammonia cloud is lethal over hundreds of metres). Reducing probability: pressure-tested integrity inspection every quarter and double-walled tank design. Reducing effect: locate the tank far from housing and plant it in a concrete catch basin (limiting the cloud's travel and the condensed volume that becomes vapour).
#### Solution
1. Probability is about how often; effect about how bad — the same substance can be high-probability/low-effect (kitchen vinegar, splashes) or low-probability/high-effect (500 m³ anhydrous ammonia).
2. Probability measures attack the "how often": inspection cycles, double hulls, procedures.
3. Effect measures attack the "how bad": distance, bunds, venting paths, volumes kept smaller (F4's what-if: separate and manage both).

### chem-f04-q02 | points 3 | outcome F4 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A chemical dispatcher sends a truck with sodium hydroxide pellets. (1) Which pictogram belongs on the packaging and what does it mean? (2) Give an example of an R-phrase/H-phrasing idea for this substance and state what such phrases add beyond the pictogram.
#### Answer
1. The corrosive pictogram (liquid corroding a hand and a surface): this substance attacks skin, eyes and materials. 2. (Modernised as H-phrases: "Causes severe skin burns and eye damage.")
#### Solution
1. The pictogram shows at a glance the nature of the danger (corrosion here); for flammables or acute toxics the signs are the flame and the skull.
2. The H-phrases (in the old CLP wording the R-phrases; with S/P-phrases for the safety measures) make the pictogram concrete and mandatory by law (CLP/GHS): H314 "causes severe skin burns and eye damage"; the corresponding P-phrases prescribe goggles and gloves.
3. The CCVX naming of R and S sentences points to this hazard-communication system; answers in the exam name the meaning, not the exact code.

### chem-f04-q03 | points 4 | outcome F4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A plant runs a process that is routinely safe at the 250 L pilot scale. The director proposes to skip the new safety study and go straight to the 10 m³ scale. Give three reasons from the properties of risk at a large scale why this is a bad idea.
#### Answer
(1) Scale effects: 40× more volume means 40× larger inventories of hazardous substances, so the effect of one failure goes up massively. (2) Heat: a bigger vessel has a smaller surface-to-volume ratio, so runaway reactions (exothermic) are harder to cool — probability of thermal runaway rises. (3) New failure modes: at 10 m³, mixing, sampling and maintenance differ (man-entry, hot work): the accident paths are new, not just larger.
#### Solution
1. The effect scales with content: stored energy and stored hazardous material.
2. The probability scales with physics: cooling capacity grows with surface, contents with volume — cube-square law (see chem-c08-q01's heat of scale).
3. Operations (entry into a tank, welding on lines, cleaning) differ between lab and factory: a risk analysis must be redone at each scale — the reason for the change-of-scale item in F4.

### chem-f04-q04 | points 4 | outcome F4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
The same site holds substance flows of hydrogen, caustic soda solution, and hydrochloric acid. Match each measure with the right substance flow, and justify the match with the substance's properties: (1) bunded storage tank (catch basin); (2) eyewash stations and sealed, acid-resistant pumps at the sampling points; (3) gas detectors with automatic shutdown; (4) named segregation of storages according to their R- and S-sentences.
#### Answer
(3) belongs to hydrogen: it is a colourless and odourless flammable gas, so leaks are invisible and odourless — only detectors catch them in time. (1) belongs to the hydrochloric acid stock: a catch basin captures a leaked corrosive load (the effect, if it happens). (2) belongs to both NaOH and HCl sampling points: both are corrosive to eyes and skin; the eyewash is the first effect-control, the pumps lower the leak probability. (4) belongs to segregation of HCl and NaOH: separate bunds keep leaked acid and base apart — a shared bund would let them neutralise violently with heat and spattering.
#### Solution
1. Hydrogen: risk type fire/explosion; colourless and odourless, lighter than air — detectors and open ventilation (roofs without pockets) see it before the spark does.
2. HCl/NaOH: risk type corrosive; bunds catch the liquid, eyewash and resistant pumps attack effect and probability exactly where people stand.
3. R- and S-phrases (H- and P-phrases in CLP) prescribe these separations by law: a site plan reads them, not just the tonnages.
4. Segregation is itself a measure: never store substances whose accident products react violently with each other.

### chem-f04-q05 | points 4 | outcome F4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Substance cycles in F4: a chlorine plant recycles its hydrogen by-product into its own HCl synthesis (H₂ + Cl₂ → 2 HCl) instead of venting it. Use the instance of substance flow + risk to explain (1) the safety gain of the recycling, (2) the new risk it creates, and (3) one control the site should have for the unwanted scenario.
#### Answer
1. The flammable hydrogen is destroyed in a controlled reaction immediately instead of accumulating or being transported: smaller inventory = smaller effect. 2. New risk: the HCl synthesis (H₂ + Cl₂) is exothermic and can run out of control (explosion H₂/Cl₂ mixture), so the loop creates a new highly-reactive pair at one point. 3. Control: ratio control keeping the mixture outside explosion limits (H₂-driven excess or Cl₂ excess), interlocked so the reactor trips on deviation; flame arrestors; cooling real-time margin.
#### Solution
1. Substance cycles don't just save money (F2) — they cut transport/storage of risky substances (flow management is risk management).
2. Any tightening of the loop concentrates the hazard at the reaction point: H₂ and Cl₂ meet there every second.
3. Controls follow the properties: explosion limits, trip interlocks, no ignition sources — the F4 triple: people, distances, processes.

### chem-f04-q06 | points 4 | outcome F4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A regulator asks a phosgene-using plant for a "risk reduction plan" before renewing its permit. Phosgene (COCl₂, acute toxic gas) is made on-site from CO and Cl₂ and immediately converted further. Outline for the site: two structural reductions of probability, two of effect, and one design philosophy that the industry has chosen for substances this toxic — and name its substance-cycle side point.
#### Answer
Probability: (1) make phosgene only in pipes/reactor, never in a tank (continuous processing: minimum inventory at any moment); (2) closed sampling systems and double mechanical seals everywhere people connect equipment. Effect: (1) the unit is at the far end of the site with a water/alkaline scrubber curtain at the fence line; (2) all adjacent processes are automated: the minimum number of people nearby at any time. Philosophy: "just-in-time" production and immediate conversion (no storage) — and as a substance-cycle side point, the CO by-product of the downstream conversion can be caught and fed back, so the loop closes without emitting either gas.
#### Solution
1. Jan Kletz's principle (intensification): the safest phosgene is the phosgene that does not exist yet; continuous pipes make inventory hours instead of tonnes.
2. Probability also drops by sealing people out of the loop; effect drops by distance, scrubbing and fewer people in the zone.
3. This is the risk design F4 describes: probability (inventory, sealing), effect (location, scrubbing, people) and the substance-cycle idea as neighbour (recycle of CO): risk and process design join.
