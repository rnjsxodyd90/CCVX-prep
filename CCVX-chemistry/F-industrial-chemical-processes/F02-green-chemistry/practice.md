# F2 — Green chemistry: practice

Ten original questions on the F2 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Molar masses as needed: Binas.

### chem-f02-q01 | points 3 | outcome F2.2 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Hydrogen is made industrially by steam reforming: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3 H₂. Calculate the atom economy of this reaction for the target product H₂.
#### Answer
17.8%
#### Solution
1. Atom economy = M(all atoms in the desired product) / M(all atoms in the reactants) × 100%.
2. Desired: 3 H₂ = 3 × 2.016 = 6.048 g mol⁻¹. Reactants: CH₄ + H₂O = 16.04 + 18.016 = 34.06 g mol⁻¹.
3. Atom economy = 6.048 / 34.06 × 100% = 17.8%. The rest ends in the by-product CO — the reason green designs often start from electrolysis instead (using renewable electricity).

### chem-f02-q02 | points 3 | outcome F2.2 | difficulty easy
#### Question
In a batch synthesis, 100 g of product is isolated while 25 g of salts and solvent remain as waste. (1) Calculate the E-factor. (2) Which class of chemicals typically has a far higher E-factor, and why?
#### Answer
1. E = 0.25. 2. Fine chemicals/pharmaceuticals: their multi-step batch syntheses make a great deal of solvent, salts and by-products per kilogram of product.
#### Solution
1. E-factor = mass of waste / mass of product = 25 / 100 = 0.25 kg waste per kg product (ideals of green production aim at single digits for fine chemicals and near 0.1 for bulk).
2. In fine chemicals, protecting groups, solvents and purification generate more waste than product (E-factors of 25–100 are common); bulk processes are so large that waste streams must be reused, or they would bankrupt the plant.

### chem-f02-q03 | points 4 | outcome F2.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Ethyl ethanoate is made by CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O (M(ester) = 88.11, M(acid) = 60.05, M(alcohol) = 46.07 g mol⁻¹). (1) Calculate its atom economy. (2) Explain why if the same recipe is run with an excess of ethanol, the computed atom economy does not change but the E-factor does.
#### Answer
1. 83.0%. 2. The atom economy considers only the atoms in the equation (fixed); the E-factor is about real production: unreacted excess ethanol has to be separated and discarded/or recycled, so the measured waste per kg of product rises with the excess.
#### Solution
1. Atom economy = 88.11 / (60.05 + 46.07) × 100% = 83.0%. All atoms of acid and alcohol go either into the ester or into water; 17% of the mass leaves as water.
2. The atom economy belongs to the equation and ignores amounts of mixing (excess, yield); the E-factor is about reality: excess ethanol beyond the stoichiometry is extra waste (or must be recycled), and anything under 100% yield also ends in the waste number.
3. Conclusion: atom economy judges the route on paper; E-factor judges the run in the plant — both are asked for by green chemistry.

### chem-f02-q04 | points 3 | outcome F2.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A company changes a colouring synthesis from batch operation to a small continuous unit. Give one argument for this change in terms of energy use, one argument in terms of product quality, and one situation where the batch process was still the better choice.
#### Answer
Continuous: energy control is much better at steady state (integrated heating and cooling without repeated heat-ups), and product quality is more constant (same residence time and conditions for every part of the stream); batch remains better when volumes are small and recipes change often (fine chemicals, pilots), because cleaning and redesigning a continuous line per product is prohibitively slow and expensive.
#### Solution
1. In batch production every cycle is heated and cooled again (energy thrown away each cycle); a continuous line runs hot at steady state and can integrate heat (see F01).
2. At steady state, temperature, concentration and residence time are the same for all material, so the product comes out more evenly.
3. For small runs and changing products, multipurpose batch vessels (fill, run, empty, clean, repeat) beat a dedicated line: there is no single green answer, the outcome depends on volume and variation of the product.

### chem-f02-q05 | points 4 | outcome F2.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Biodiesel is made from plant oil (a triglyceride) and methanol: triglyceride + 3 CH₃OH ⇌ 3 fatty-acid methyl ester + glycerol, catalysed by KOH solution. (1) What is the reaction type called (besides "condensation/transesterification")? (2) Name the two useful product streams. (3) Give one argument why this is greener than fossil diesel, and one objection that crops up at large scale.
#### Answer
1. A transesterification: the ester's alcohol part (glycerol) is exchanged for methanol. 2. The biodiesel itself (the fatty-acid methyl esters, FAME) and glycerol (glycerine) as a valuable by-product (feedstock for soap/pharma). 3. Greener because the carbon cycles on a short time scale (plants took the CO₂ from the air); objection: large-scale demand competes with food and drives deforestation/land use change, which can make the total CO₂ balance worse than diesel.
#### Solution
1. In a transesterification one alcohol replaces another at the ester bonds; the triglyceride gives three methyl esters (the biodiesel) plus glycerol.
2. Both products are saleable: FAME as diesel, glycerol for pharma/food/cosmetics.
3. Within the carbon cycle the fuel part is (nearly) CO₂-neutral (see chem-c03-q05); but the footprint includes farming, fertiliser, land conversion and transport — a fair judgment weighs those as well (F2.1 environmental requirements).

### chem-f02-q06 | points 4 | outcome F2.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Match the three refinery operations with their purpose: fractional distillation, cracking, reforming. Purposes: (a) splitting long-chain hydrocarbons into petrol-length molecules and alkenes for the plastics industry, (b) separating crude oil into boiling-range fractions, (c) reshaping straight-chain molecules into branched ones for high-octane petrol.
#### Answer
Fractional distillation → (b); cracking → (a); reforming → (c).
#### Solution
1. Fractional distillation separates the crude by boiling points into fractions (gas, petrol, kerosene, diesel, fuel oil, residue) on the sheer height of a refinery column.
2. Cracking thermally or catalytically breaks long chains down: the overabundant heavy fractions become petrol-range molecules and alkenes (ethene, propene for polymerisation, see D03).
3. Reforming changes the shape of molecules (same atoms): linear chains to branched/aromatic structures with higher octane numbers. Together these three make the oil refinery an order-of-magnitude smarter separation-and-conversion plant than a simple still: F2.3 recognises them in described processes.

### chem-f02-q07 | points 3 | outcome F2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A yoghurt cup made of PLA (from maize starch) is marketed as "renewable", a PET cup as "recyclable". Using the green-chemistry criteria from F2.1, weigh one argument for each cup and finish with one condition on which the better choice depends.
#### Answer
PLA: renewable resources (maize regrows yearly, short carbon cycle) instead of fossil feedstock. PET: the fossil origin is a drawback, but a well-established recycling stream keeps the material in use and avoids new production. The better choice depends on the end-of-life system: if the cup is industrially composted, PLA wins; if it is captured and recycled (and PLA would contaminate the PET stream), PET wins.
#### Solution
1. PLA ("polylactic acid") claims biomass origin: lower fossil carbon and regrowth of the resource (renewable resources aspect).
2. PET claims recycling: the existing deposit-and-recycle chain can keep the material in the economy without new crude (recycling aspect).
3. The judgment hangs on the infrastructure after use: compostable without composters is litter; recyclable without collection is landfill — green decisions compare the whole life cycle, not the word on the cup.

### chem-f02-q08 | points 3 | outcome F2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Producing 1 kg of secondary (recycled) aluminium needs about 5% of the energy of primary aluminium from bauxite. Explain this huge difference, referring to the primary production process.
#### Answer
Primary aluminium production ends with the electrolysis of Al₂O₃, a very energy-intensive step (the energy of the electricity plus the whole mining and refining chain); recycling only remelts the already existing metal, which costs a fraction of that energy with the same metal as product.
#### Solution
1. Primary aluminium: mine bauxite, purify to Al₂O₃, dissolve in molten cryolite and electrolyse (Al³⁺ + 3 e⁻ → Al at the negative electrode) — an electrically very expensive end step (see C01 on electrolysis and F03 on electricity).
2. Recycled aluminium: collect, sort, remelt — no electrolysis, essentially just the melting energy (Al melts at 660 °C).
3. This is why aluminium cans are one of the most valuable recycling streams (metals aspect of F2.3): a real energy and CO₂ gain with the same product quality.

### chem-f02-q09 | points 4 | outcome F2.2 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A route to a drug intermediate has an atom economy of 90% for the target. In the plant the conversion is 75% (a quarter of the feedstock stays unreacted and is discarded). (1) Compute the approximate E-factor of the step from these data (ignore solvents and auxiliary chemicals). (2) A competitor variant has the same atom economy but 90% conversion. How does its E-factor compare? (3) Conclude why green-chemistry engineers work on selectivity/conversion even when the equation itself is already "clean".
#### Answer
1. About 0.4 (per kg product: 0.25 kg unreacted discard + 0.11 kg by-product from the 10% atomic loss, over 0.68 kg product ≈ 0.53; using the simplified counting in the answer: ~0.4–0.5). 2. The 90% conversion variant wastes only 10% unreacted: E ≈ 0.2. 3. Because underperfect conversion multiplies the waste: in the plant not the equation's clean theoretical lines but the yielded kilograms set the E-factor.
#### Solution
1. Feed 100 g: 75 g reacts; of the reacting 75 g, 90% of the atoms go to product → about 68 g product and 7 g by-product; waste = 25 g (unreacted) + 7 g = 32 g. E = 32 / 68 ≈ 0.47 (roughly 0.5).
2. Same with 90% conversion: waste = 10 g (unreacted) + 8 g (atom-loss on 90 g reacted: 9 g by-product) ≈ 19 g; product ≈ 81 g; E ≈ 0.24 — halved.
3. High atom economy does not rescue poor conversion: every unconverted kilo and every side reaction burdens E directly (and see F2.2 energy effect: also separation energy). Improving selectivity/reducing excess is the daily green work of process technology.

### chem-f02-q10 | points 3 | outcome F2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A classical route uses dichloromethane (DCM) as the reaction solvent; a proposed route uses water. The DCM route runs 20 °C lower and gives 85% yield, the water route 70%. Discuss this trade-off with three green-chemistry aspects, and come to a reasoned conclusion.
#### Answer
Safety/health: water wins (DCM is suspected carcinogenic and volatile). Energy: DCM wins a little (lower temperature). Yield/waste: DCM wins on E-factor (less product lost). Conclusion: if the toxicity and volatility of DCM can be contained (closed system, recovery), the DCM route can be defensible; otherwise water, reclaimed yield, is the greener route — green judgment weighs health and environment before small energy gains, and this is exactly the multi-factor trade-off F2.1 asks you to make explicit.
#### Solution
1. Score the aspects systematically: reaction conditions (temperature → energy), safety (toxicity/volatility), E-factor (yield-driven waste), recycling (solvent recovery), environmental requirements (emissions).
2. Water route: safest and cheapest solvent, but 70% yield means more feedstock ends in waste (E-factor rises), and the mild excess energy costs of 20 °C is the price of that.
3. No automatic winner: quantify as far as possible, choose by impact: carcinogenic emissions and worker health usually outrank the small energy penalty — and the honesty of the trade-off is what the examiner wants to read.
