# F5 — Sustainable production processes: practice

Six original questions on the F5 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Given formulas: atom economy, E-factor and the environmental quotient EQ are applied as in the notes.

### chem-f05-q01 | points 3 | outcome F5 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Two designs for the same fine chemical lie on the table. Design 1 uses a solvent recovery loop and a continuous line; design 2 is a batch route with three solvent changes. (1) List for each design one expected value of the E-factor argument. (2) Which design fits which class of production (bulk/fine) better? Give a reasoned opinion.
#### Answer
1. Design 1: small E-factor (recovered solvent, no repeated solvent inventory), less waste, lower raw-material flow = sustainability. Design 2: large E-factor (solvent changes per batch become waste each time), higher flexibility but wasteful. 2. Design 1 is the industrial standard for large (bulk/large-scale) production — high volume pays back the engineering; Design 2, while wasteful, is the pattern made for fast product switches in fine chemistry (multipurpose plants) — but green judgment pushes even fine producers to solvent-recovery and continuous processing where feasible.
#### Solution
1. Every solvent change ends as waste (evaporation losses, residue): E-factor scales with the number of solvents and batches.
2. Bulk economy supports dedicated, integrated lines; fine chemistry tolerates generalist equipment — but the design question always asks: which of the two yields the least waste per kg? Answer: the looped continuous one.
3. The F5 skill: give an opinion with the number-carrying terms of green production in it.

### chem-f05-q02 | points 4 | outcome F5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A company claims "our new synthesis is green: 95% yield." Judge this claim, naming two other constraints of the laws of conservation side (mass, energy) that actually decide how sustainable the process is.
#### Answer
A 95% yield says only how much of the feedstock reacted as desired: it leaves open (1) the atom economy of the equation itself (how much is by-product even at 100% yield) and (2) the E-factor of the real process (solvents, salts, purification waste per kilogram) and its energy bill — both of which can overturn the claim.
#### Solution
1. Mass side: yield ≠ atom economy: a reaction with 30% atom economy at 95% yield still generates more by-product than product.
2. Energy side: five energy-wasting columns (distillation crystallisation etc.) can make a "high-yield green" process emit more CO₂ than a moderate one (F2/F5 total).
3. Practically: demand E-factor / EQ data + energy use alongside the yield — the claim's green title is earned by the numbers, not the marketing.

### chem-f05-q03 | points 4 | outcome F5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Design advice. A plastic additive is currently made in a 4-step batch route at 60 kg waste per kg product. The company wants your opinion on redesigning along green-chemistry lines. Give four concrete design changes, each tagged with the green principle it serves (from: fewer steps/atoms-efficiency, energy, solvents, feedstock choice, waste as resource).
#### Answer
1. Merge steps (fewer unit operations = fewer isolations = less waste): fewer steps/atom efficiency. 2. Replace the solvent by water or by a recycled/recoverable solvent in one loop: solvents. 3. Buy the intermediate with the higher atom economy instead of making it on site (if its production upstream is materially better): feedstock choice. 4. Find a sale for the 60 kg of "waste" (e.g. the salt as brine for the chlor-alkali cell) or redirect it as internal feedstock: waste as resource.
#### Solution
1. Every isolation step adds solvent, filtration cake, washing liquid: cutting steps cuts waste geometrically, not linearly.
2. Solvent is usually the largest component of waste in pharma-type syntheses; water or closed loops are the big lever.
3. Buying upstream atom economy outsources waste (still counts in your supplier's E-factor — check), and waste-kept-in-the-loop is the substance cycle of F4/E3.
4. The advice format: per step name one measurable principle — that is how a "design opinion" earns its marks in CCVX-style questions.

### chem-f05-q04 | points 4 | outcome F5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Sustainable cycles. An acid plant proposes to close its water loop: all process water is purified on site and reused. Name (1) a conservation-law reason this is feasible, (2) one technical catch, (3) the sustainability profit in two dimensions.
#### Answer
1. Conservation: water is matter — none is chemically destroyed in the plant's use (it is a solvent/coolant), so in principle every molecule can be recovered and recycled (mass conservation makes the cycle meaningful). 2. Catch: contaminants accumulate: after several cycles salts and organics concentrate, so the purification train must be designed for the concentrated stream (a small bleed is unavoidable). 3. Profit: lower demand for fresh river water (environmental condition) and far lower discharge (emissions/permits), often with a bonus in heat recovery since the recycled water is already warm.
#### Solution
1. Mass conservation says cycles are possible — matter does not wear out; C03's point in industrial dress.
2. The technical catch is entropy-like: everything accumulates, so loops need their own bleed + purification block (compare the purge of chem-f01-q04).
3. Sustainability loop: less intake (natural resources), less output (emissions toward water quality), and sometimes cheaper energy — three dimensions of a judgement that F5/F2/G02 want to see explicit.

### chem-f05-q05 | points 3 | outcome F5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Compute the atom economy for a redesigned catalyst route of adipic acid: benzene C₆H₆ + 4 H₂O₂ → C₆H₁₀O₄ + 4 H₂O (M(adipic acid) = 146.1; M(benzene) = 78.11; M(H₂O₂) = 34.02), and compare it qualitatively against the industrial standard route (via nitric acid, which produces N₂O — a strong greenhouse gas — as a by-product).
#### Answer
Atom economy = 146.1 / (78.11 + 4 × 34.02) × 100% = 146.1 / 214.2 = 68% (two significant figures). The alternative uses a benign oxidant (H₂O₂) whose only by-product is water, whereas the N₂O route adds a ~300× CO₂-potent greenhouse gas to the air vent: the catalytic-oxidant route is clearly superior on the waste side.
#### Solution
1. AM = M(product) / M(total reactants) = 146.1 / 214.19 ≈ 0.68.
2. In the classic route the unmentionable by-product is N₂O (laughing gas), a potent greenhouse gas — the kind of waste that no EQ correction with Q=1 would ever capture (here Q would be very large).
3. The new route's waste is water and maybe unreacted peroxide (recovered): a structural green gain, not just a procedural one.

### chem-f05-q06 | points 4 | outcome F5 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Given by a redesign group: process P: atom economy 85%, E-factor 0.8, waste NaCl only (Q = 1); process W: atom economy 90%, E-factor 0.5, waste 2% chromium salt (Q = 100) and 98% NaCl (Q = 1). (1) Compute the environmental quotients EQ using waste composition: per 100 kg of product. (2) Give your design judgment.
#### Answer
1. P: waste = 0.8 × 100 = 80 kg of NaCl → EQ = (80 × 1) / 100 = 0.8. W: waste = 0.5 × 100 = 50 kg, consisting of 1 kg chromium salt (2%) and 49 kg NaCl → EQ = (1 × 100 + 49 × 1) / 100 = 1.49 ≈ 1.5. 2. Choose P: despite W's better-looking atom economy and E-factor, W's 1 kg of chromate waste makes it the worse environmental performer; the design judgment takes Q into account.
#### Solution
1. EQ evaluates toxicity weighting per kilogram: P: 80 kg × 1 = 80 toxic-kg → EQ = 0.8. W: 1 kg × 100 + 49 kg × 1 = 149 → EQ = 1.5.
2. The two headline metrics (AM, E) say prefer W; the EQ reverses it. This is exactly the reasoning F5/E03 expect: atom economy, E-factor, coefficient of pollution Q together — never one alone.
3. Real design decision: prefer the process with the slightly worse E-factor over the chromium waste, unless chromium waste can be eliminated.
