## Opgave A — Quality control for straight whiskey (Q1–Q5)

**Subdomains:** C01 (organic equations), C04 (mechanism arrows), D01 (chromatography), B03/G01 (polarity, sugars). **BINAS:** structure tables for sugars (67F1/67A1-type).

### Q1 (2p) — complete the equation sucrose → isosachrosan
**Asked:** fill in the missing structural formula and product of the water-splitting ether formation between the two rings.
**Solution:** the two OH groups on the C atoms directly beside the inter-ring ether bond condense: a second ether (ring) bridge forms and H₂O leaves. Draw the isosachrosan skeleton: both glucose and fructose rings with the new –O– bridge at the indicated positions; beside the arrow the structural formula of H₂O. Balance H, C, O per side.
**Common mistakes:** forming the ether bridge between the wrong carbons (it must be the C atoms neighbouring the original glycoside ether bond; the question literally says it); forgetting the H₂O product; losing stereocentres drawn in the given sugar drawing.
**Points:** 1 point for the correctly placed new ether group, 1 for completing isosachrosan + water.

### Q2 (3p) — equation for forming furfural (FF) from xylan
**Asked:** give the molecular-formula equation: xylan H–(C₅H₈O₄)ₙ–OH → furfural + water as only by-product.
**Solution:** the repeat unit of xylan is C₅H₈O₄ and furfural is C₅H₄O₂. Per unit: C₅H₈O₄ → C₅H₄O₂ + 2 H₂O (O: 4 = 2+2 ✓; H: 8 = 4+4 ✓). For the full polymer: H–(C₅H₈O₄)ₙ–OH → n C₅H₄O₂ + (2n+1) H₂O. Check: H: 8n+2 = 4n+2(2n+1) ✓; find FF's formula from its structure (aromatic aldehyde with the ring C(CHO)–CH=CH–CH=CH–O).
**Common mistakes:** writing C₅H₄O₂ for xylan unit (that's the product); balancing only C and H but forgetting the extra end-group water ((2n+1) counts).
**Points:** 1 for reactant + water product present, 1 for correct FF formula, 1 for the full balance with n coefficients.

### Q3 (2p) — complete the mechanism step with lone pairs and electron arrows
**Asked:** on the answer sheet, finish the last step of the HMF formation mechanism: draw lone pairs before the arrow and electron-pair movements.
**Solution:** a keto–enol/ring-closure style step: the protonated ring O (or –OH) leaves as water while the ring oxygen's lone pair forms the ring double bond. Procedure: draw on the before-arrow molecule the lone pairs for every O (2 per O) and the positive charge on the protonated O; then arrows: one ring lone pair → forms the C=C of the ring (or completes the aromatic ring), the C–O bond pair → onto the leaving water's O. Never move an arrow from a positive centre; pairs move from lone pairs or bonds towards cationic ends.
**Common mistakes:** arrows starting at a positive charge instead of at a lone pair/bond; changing bond positions but ending with the wrong final ring (the target must be the furan ring of HMF).
**Points:** 1 for the lone pairs, 1 for the correct arrows.

### Q4 (3p) — which chromatography set-up (polar/nonpolar) from the retention times
**Asked:** decide between (1) nonpolar stationary + polar mobile and (2) the reverse, using the structures and the chromatogram.
**Solution:** HMF carries an extra –OH group versus FF, so HMF is the more polar (more hydrophilic) one; FF is the less polar. On the chromatograms HMF has the shorter retention time: a substance that elutes fast prefers the mobile phase. So HMF → polar mobile phase → set-up 1 (nonpolar stationary phase, polar mobile phase). The identical logic the other way: FF stays longest because it sticks to the nonpolar stationary phase.
**Common mistakes:** mixing up the two diagrams' peak assignments (compare the calibration to see which peak is which substance — the marker tells you; HMF is the extra-OH molecule); confusing "bonds to the stationary phase" with "dissolves in the mobile phase".
**Points:** 1 for the retention-time observation, 1 for the polarity reasoning, 1 for the consistent conclusion (set-up 1).

### Q5 (3p) — compute molar ratio HMF/FF from peak areas; judge the whiskey
**Asked:** use the standard (1:1 mol) chromatogram to convert the sample's peak areas into the molar ratio, then judge "straight whiskey or not" against the straight-whiskey window 2.0 < HMF/FF < 2.6.
**Solution:** standard: equal moles but unequal areas — the detector factors: area ratio standard = 106 HMF : 137 FF = 0.774 per 1:1 mol. Sample: ratio 100 : 43 = 2.326. True molar ratio = 2.326/0.774 = 3.0. This is outside the 2.0–2.6 window → the sample is not straight whiskey. (Equivalent route: per substance compare with the standard: HMF 100/106 = 0.943×, FF 43/137 = 0.314× against the 1:1 reference: ratio 0.943/0.314 = 3.0.)
**Common mistakes:** taking sample areas at face value (areas don't equal moles when the detector responds differently per substance: you must divide by the standard's area-ratio); using the wrong window direction (window is HMF/FF, not FF/HMF).
**Points:** 1 for the two area ratios, 1 for the molar ratio, 1 for the conclusion.

## Opgave B — Sulfur production: the Claus process (Q6–Q11)

**Subdomains:** C01 (equations), C02 (gas stoichiometry), C06 (formation heats), C05 (temperature and equilibrium), G02 (emission norms), F01 (block diagram). **BINAS:** formation heats (SO₂ −2.968·10⁵, H₂S −0.206·10⁵, H₂O(g) −2.42·10⁵, S₂ +1.28·10⁵ J mol⁻¹; S₈ = 0 per the exam), molar volume, molar masses.

### Q6 (2p) — total equation of the Claus process
**Solution:** reaction 2 × 2: 4 H₂S + 2 SO₂ → (6/x) Sx + 4 H₂O; plus reaction 1: 2 H₂S + 3 O₂ → 2 SO₂ + 2 H₂O; add and cancel SO₂: 6 H₂S + 3 O₂ → (6/x) Sx + 6 H₂O; divide by 3: **2 H₂S + O₂ → (2/x) Sx + 2 H₂O**.
**Common mistakes:** cancelling substances that differ in state (only cancel identical substances, here SO₂); forgetting to reduce to the smallest integers.

### Q7 (4p) — minimum air per day
**Solution:** 20 ton H₂S per day = 20·10⁶ g / 34.1 g mol⁻¹ = 5.87·10⁵ mol. Per Q6, O₂ : H₂S = 1 : 2 → 2.94·10⁵ mol O₂. At 298 K: V = n·V_m = 2.94·10⁵ × 24.5·10⁻³ = 7.2·10³ m³ O₂. Air holds 20.9 vol% O₂: 7.2·10³/0.209 = 3.4·10⁴ m³ air per day (two significant figures).

### Q8 (3p) — heat of reaction 2 per mol H₂S with only S₂ formed
**Solution:** with x = 2: 2 H₂S + SO₂ → 1½ S₂ + 2 H₂O. ΔE per mol reaction = [1.5×(+1.28) + 2×(−2.42) − 2×(−0.206) − (−2.97)]·10⁵ = [1.92 − 4.84 + 0.412 + 2.97]·10⁵ = +0.46·10⁵ J; per mol H₂S: **+0.23·10⁵ J mol⁻¹** (endothermic).
**Common mistakes:** using water's liquid-former (the products here are gases, so use H₂O(g) formation heat); skipping S₂'s +1.28·10⁵ (Binas marks it, S₈ is 0) or dividing by 2 wrongly (per mol reaction = per 2 mol H₂S!).

### Q9 (3p) — endothermic or exothermic for x > 6
**Solution:** in that temperature region, raising the temperature lowers the conversion degree, so the equilibrium moves left on heating. Since heating shifts an equilibrium toward the endothermic side, the forward reaction must be **exothermic**.
**Common mistakes:** reasoning from the computed +0.23·10⁵ J/mol for Q8 only (that concerns S₂; x > 6 is a different product mix — equality is not required); inverting the Le Chatelier direction.

### Q10 (1p) — why the SO₂ limit must be strict
**Solution:** SO₂ is acutely toxic when inhaled (and causes acid rain / corrodes), so even small emissions are restricted.

### Q11 (3p) — complete the block diagram
**Solution:** stream from R2 → S1 (sulfur Sx separated out via cooling/condensing) → remaining gases into the second heat exchanger (reheat) → R3 → gas outlet; the two heat exchangers link their water flows: the hot water out of the R1-side exchanger is the cold side going in to the reheater after cooling (countercurrent layout, warm stream marked at the hot end).

## Opgave C — laminated safety glass, PVA-PVB (Q12–Q21)

**Subdomains:** D03 (copolymers), C04 (rate curves), C02 (titration chain), B04 (plasticisers, adhesion, chain mobility). **BINAS:** butanal structure, molar masses.

### Q12 (3p) — equation PVA + butanal
**Solution:** butanal CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CHO: its C=O reacts with two neighbouring PVA–OH groups (one per neighbouring VA unit) to form the cyclic acetal + one H₂O: 3 VA units? no — two VA units per one butanal: coefficients: 2 (VA units) + 1 butanal → 1 VB unit + 1 H₂O. Draw butanal's structural formula fully; count the units in the figure's indices.
**Common mistakes:** water missing; one VA unit per butanal instead of two (read the question: two OH per butanal).

### Q13 (2p) — why random not block copolymer
**Solution:** blocks would need runs of identical units; but butanal attacks free OH pairs anywhere along the PVA chains, at random — there is no trigger that would bunch the converted units together, so VB units appear at random along the chains: a random copolymer.

### Q14 (3p) — rate drop before t1 (collision model) and sketch after suspension forms
**Solution:** before t1: concentrations of butanal (and of the free OH pairs) fall → fewer effective collisions per second → slower rate. After suspension forms: the draw lies below the dotted curve and ends on the time axis: the polymer precipitates, so the remaining OH pairs chain-locked inside particles have even fewer collision opportunities (smaller reacting surface, and gradually everything converts): rate falls faster than the ideal curve would suggest, ending at zero when conversion is complete.

### Q15 (3p) — % VA units left in PVA-PVB-78
**Solution:** 78 mass% VB units (M = 142) and 22% VA units (M = 44.1) per 100 g: n(VB) = 78/142 = 0.549; n(VA) = 22/44.1 = 0.499. Each VB unit consumed two VA units: original VA = 0.499 + 2×0.549 = 1.597 mol; remaining fraction = 0.499/1.597 = **31%**.

### Q16 (2p) — the two separation methods of the assay
**Solution:** extraction (the soluble ethanoic acid + solvent into water, polymer not) and filtration/settling-and-decanting.

### Q17 (2p) — titration equation in structural formulas
**Solution:** CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O.

### Q18 (4p) — mass% PVB in the sample
**Solution:** n(OH⁻) = 16.00·10⁻³ × 0.650 = 1.040·10⁻² mol = n of leftover VA units (1 OH per unit → 1 acid). Mass of free-OH units: 1.040·10⁻² × 44.1 = 0.459 g. PVB fraction: (2.200 − 0.459)/2.200 = 0.792 → **79.2%** (three significant figures).

### Q19 (2p) — weaker adhesion when lamination happens cooler
**Solution:** at lower temperature the polymer chains can move/flow less, so the sheet cannot conform to the microscopic roughness/unevenness of the glass; less contact surface = fewer hydrogen bridges between the polymer's –OH groups and the glass surface groups = weaker adhesion.

### Q20 (2p) — material II (60% PVB) vs material I (78%): adhesion to glass
**Solution:** material II has more VA units (more free –OH groups) than material I, so its chains can form more hydrogen bridges with the OH-groups/particles at the glass surface: **material II adheres better**. (Glass surface: OH-bearing silicate.)

### Q21 (2p) — material I (25 g plasticiser/100 g) vs material III (17 g): deformability
**Solution:** plasticiser molecules sit between the chains, hold them further apart, and weaken the Van der Waals bonds/hydrogen bridges between the chains → easier chain slip → easier to deform. **Material I with more plasticiser is easier to deform.**

## Opgave D — dystrophin (Q22–Q25)

**Subdomains:** C05 (equilibrium K), G01 (protein structures, DNA/triplets, translation), B01 (Lewis, formal charges, covalence). **BINAS:** amino-acid/codon table (70E/71G).

### Q22 (3p) — equilibrium condition and K for Actine + Dys-A ⇌ Actine–Dys-A
**Solution:** K = [Actine–Dys-A]/([Actine][Dys-A]). Bound actine: 28% × 3.0 µM = 0.84 µM. Free actine: 3.0 − 0.84 = 2.16 µM; free Dys-A: 6.0 − 0.84 = 5.16 µM. K = 0.84·10⁻⁶ /(2.16·10⁻⁶ × 5.16·10⁻⁶) = **7.5·10⁴ L mol⁻¹**.
**Common mistakes:** forgetting to subtract the bound part from both sides; µM cancels in the top×bottom so report units correctly.

### Q23 (2p) — which structural levels change on stretching
**Solution:** primary structure does NOT change (the amino acid sequence/peptide bonds stay; nothing breaks). Secondary structure DOES change (the α-helices are stretched out of their coils). Tertiary DOES change (the S-shaped loops straighten — different orientation of the helices toward each other; hydrogen bridges giving the fold are broken).

### Q24 (3p) — draw the phosphorylated serine unit (covalence of P = 5; in mid-chain peptide)
**Solution:** draw ~NH–CH(CH₂–O–P(=O)(−)-O⁻)–CO~ with: P with five bonds (one double-bonded O, three single O); lone pairs on all O; formal charges: two O⁻ negative, P neutral (5 bonds: formal 0), the carbonyl O with two pairs, and chain continuation marks ~ at both peptide ends. Count P covalence = 5.

### Q25 (4p) — which amino acid the healthy codon codes + mutation effect
**Solution:** the start codon sits at bases 209–211, so base 3396 is translated-base 3396 − 208 = 3188. 3188 = 3×1062 + 2 → the second base of a codon (codon 1063). From the figure that codon in healthy mRNA is UGG → per the Binas codon table UGG = tryptophan (Trp). The mutation changes the middle base G→A: UAG, which is a stop codon → translation of dystrophin stops early → truncated, non-functional protein.
**Common mistakes:** forgetting that to count codons you must first subtract the 208 leading bases; reading UGG wrong direction (codons are read 5'→3' on mRNA); answering "weaker helix" instead of "truncated protein" (a stop codon is shown).
