## Question 1 (official paper): chrome alum (9 points)

**Asked:** protons and electrons in Cr³⁺ (a); the three bond types in the salt crystal and between which particles (b); the Lewis structure of SO₄²⁻ with formal charges and spatial shape (c); the dissolving equation of chrome alum in water (d).

**Subdomains:** B01 (atom/ion structure, Lewis, charges), B03 (bonds), C01 (dissolving equation, hydrate). **BINAS:** periodic table (Z Cr = 24).

**Solution (ours):**

a. Chromium is element 24: 24 protons; Cr³⁺ has lost 3 electrons: 24 − 3 = 21 electrons.

b. The crystal holds K⁺, Cr³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ plus 12 crystal-water molecules per formula unit. Bond types: ion bonds between the positive ions (K⁺, Cr³⁺) and the sulfate ions; atomic bonds (covalent) inside the sulfate ions (S–O) and inside the water molecules (O–H); ion-dipole bonds between the ions and the crystal water molecules (hydration).

c. Lewis structure: central S with four O; draw two O with a double bond (formal charge 0 each) and two O single-bonded carrying −1 each, with a lone pair completed on each O; total charge −2. Four electron domains on S, all bonding: tetrahedral shape, bond angles ≈ 109.5°.

d. KCr(SO₄)₂·12H₂O(s) → K⁺(aq) + Cr³⁺(aq) + 2 SO₄²⁻(aq) + 12 H₂O(l). The composite sulfate ions stay together, the crystal water is released (don't forget it).

**Common mistakes:** counting electrons of the atom instead of the ion; putting the crystal water inside the formula as if it bonded covalently into the salt instead of being released; drawing 4 single O− (charge −4) instead of the resonance-average with formal charges −2 overall.

**Points note (official key):** a 2p, b 2p, c 3p, d 2p.

## Question 2 (official paper): deodorant and the aluminium titration chain (10 points)

**Asked:** mmol of bromine from 7.00 mmol potassium bromate (a); the equation of oxine + bromine (b); the precipitation equation with sodium oxinate (c); the amount of Al³⁺ per mL deodorant from the back-titration chain (d).

**Subdomains:** C02 (stoichiometry, back-titration chain), C01 (precipitation). **BINAS:** none beyond molar work (a hint is given in the question itself).

**Solution (ours):**

a. BrO₃⁻ + 5 Br⁻ + 6 H⁺ → 3 Br₂ + 3 H₂O: 1 : 3, so 7.00 mmol bromate gives 3 × 7.00 = 21.0 mmol Br₂.

b. C₉H₇NO(aq) + 2 Br₂(aq) → C₉H₅Br₂NO(aq) + 2 H⁺(aq) + 2 Br⁻(aq). Two H atoms of the molecule are substituted by Br; the other two H from those spots leave as H⁺ with the Br⁻.

c. Al³⁺(aq) + 3 C₉H₆NO⁻(aq) → Al(C₉H₆NO)₃(s). Aluminium oxinate ties three oxinate ions per Al³⁺ (the poorly soluble 1:3 salt).

d. Chain, in order: 21.0 mmol Br₂ produced; titrated leftover: 0.660 mmol; consumed by oxine: 21.0 − 0.660 = 20.34 mmol Br₂; oxine reacted 1 : 2 → n(oxine) = 20.34/2 = 10.17 mmol; this oxine was released from the oxinate of the aluminium: Al(C₉H₆NO)₃ holds 3 oxinates per Al → n(Al³⁺) = 10.17/3 = 3.39 mmol per 10.0 mL, so **0.339 mmol per 1.00 mL** of deodorant.

**Common mistakes:** in d losing the factor 10 (the titrated excess is not the used-up amount — subtract first); dividing by 2 vs multiplying by 2 for oxine (the ratio is oxine : Br₂ = 1 : 2, so half of the consumed Br₂); in b forgetting that substitution also frees HBr.

**Points note (official key):** a 1p, b 2p, c 2p, d 5p.

## Question 3 (official paper): pectin and D-galacturonic acid (16 points)

**Asked:** what the D in D-galactose stands for (a); the half-reactions and full reaction of propan-1-ol with dichromate (b); why dichromate fails to convert D-galactose into D-galacturonic acid (c); how living cells do this conversion (d); design a lab route (e); write the analogous esterification of propanoic acid (f); how pectin binds cell walls (g); compute the protolysed fraction of pectin's carboxyl groups at pH 4.0 with 40% esterified (h).

**Subdomains:** C01 (redox half-reactions, esterification), E02 (stereochemistry), G01 (biochemistry, enzymes), C05 (weak-acid fractions). **BINAS:** tables of sugars (67F1/67A1 structures), the given K_z.

**Solution (ours):**

a. The capital D marks the configuration series: D-galactose is one of the mirror-image forms (D vs L sugars); the substance is optically active and has an optical antipode (L-galactose).

b. Oxidation half-reaction: C₃H₈O + H₂O → C₃H₆O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ ×3. Reduction: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14 H⁺ + 6 e⁻ → 2 Cr³⁺ + 7 H₂O ×2. Total (electrons 12 both sides): 3 C₃H₈O + 2 Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 16 H⁺ → 3 C₃H₆O₂ + 4 Cr³⁺ + 11 H₂O.

c. Dichromate is not selective: in the sugar it oxidises the aldehyde group at C1 as well as the primary alcohol at C6, so it does not stop at galacturonic acid (it continues toward the dicarboxylic acid = galactaric acid); you cannot complete the wanted mono-oxidation with it.

d. Cells use selective enzymes: the sugar is first activated/protected at C1 (for example as a UDP-sugar derivative at C1) and a specific dehydrogenase then oxidises only the –CH₂OH at C6 (with NAD-type oxidant). Enzyme specificity (active site) replaces the missing selectivity of chemistry.

e. Reproduce that selectivity: protect C1 first (convert to the glycoside, tying up the aldehyde), oxidise C6 with a suitable oxidant, then hydrolyse off the protection to release D-galacturonic acid. (Alternatively a modern selective oxidation of primary alcohols such as a TEMPO-catalysed oxidation.)

f. By analogy with methanol: CH₃–CH₂–COOH + CH₃–OH ⇌ CH₃–CH₂–COO–CH₃ + H₂O; the product is methyl propanoate (with structural formulas drawn).

g. The pectin/polysaccharide chains cling by hydrogen bridges between their many –OH (and C=O) groups of neighbouring cell-wall polysaccharides (and the carboxylate arms also bridge via Ca²⁺ ions between chains — both products of the pectin's chemistry hold the walls together).

h. Free groups: 100 − 40 = 60% of the initial COOH. At pH 4, [H⁺] = 1.0·10⁻⁴; K_z(HZ) = [H⁺][Z⁻]/[HZ] = 5.0·10⁻³ → [Z⁻]/[HZ] = 5.0·10⁻³/1.0·10⁻⁴ = 50. So of the free groups, the protolysed fraction = 50/51 ≈ 98%. Of the initial total: 0.60 × 98% ≈ 59%.

**Common mistakes:** in b balancing by 2e−/4e− instead of 6/4 (largest common multiple 12); in h answering 98% (of the free groups) when the question asks for a share of the *initial* groups — multiply by the 60% free; in c claiming "nothing reacts" — everything reacts, the failure is selectivity.

**Points note (official key):** a 2p, b 3p, c 2p, d 1p, e 1p, f 2p, g 2p, h 3p.

## Question 4 (official paper): oxides and water (10 points)

**Asked:** equation K₂O + water (a); which ions a saturated MgO solution contains (b); show with a calculation that the "poorly soluble" mark for MgO is right (c); milk of lime vs milk of magnesia, higher or lower pH (d1); suspension of zinc oxide vs milk of magnesia (d2); effect of adding extra MgO (e).

**Subdomains:** C05 (Ks, solubility), C01 (oxide + water, strong bases). **BINAS:** tables of salt solubility (45A meaning of "s": dissolves less than about 0.01 mol L⁻¹) and of solubility products (45A/46).

**Solution (ours):**

a. K₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2 K⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) (oxide is a strong base: complete).

b. In saturated Mg(OH)₂-rich water you find Mg²⁺ and OH⁻ (plus the trace H₃O⁺ of water itself); no free O²⁻, because oxide reacts completely with water to hydroxide.

c. K_s(Mg(OH)₂) = [Mg²⁺][OH⁻]² ≈ 5.6·10⁻¹² (Binas). For the saturated solution: s × (2s)² = 4s³ = K_s → s = (5.6·10⁻¹²/4)^(1/3) ≈ 1.1·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹. That is far less than 0.01 mol L⁻¹: magnesium oxide is indeed "poorly soluble" as the solubility table marks — and any dissolved MgO converts to Mg(OH)₂.

d1. Ca(OH)₂ has a much larger K_s than Mg(OH)₂, so saturated milk of lime holds more OH⁻: higher pH than milk of magnesia.

d2. Zn(OH)₂ has a much smaller K_s than Mg(OH)₂ (much less soluble), so a zinc-oxide suspension holds less OH⁻: lower pH than milk of magnesia.

e. Nothing changes: the solution is already saturated, so extra solid MgO just adds to the sediment; [OH⁻] and the pH stay at the saturated values.

**Common mistakes:** writing O²⁻(aq) in the solution (it reacts to OH⁻ completely — a classic, see 05 — acids and bases); forgetting the stoichiometric 2 in K_s = s·(2s)² = 4s³; answering d by "more basic metal" vibes instead of comparing solubility products with numbers.

**Points note (official key):** a 1p, b 2p, c 4p, d 2p, e 1p.

## Question 5 (official paper): 4-methylpent-2-ene (11 points)

**Asked:** its structural formula (a); stereoisomers? (b); name of the bromine adduct (c); draw part of the addition polymer (d); describe the copolymer with ethene (e).

**Subdomains:** B01 (nomenclature, isomerism, structural formulas), E02 (cis/trans), D03 (addition polymerisation). **BINAS:** naming rules as in B01.

**Solution (ours):**

a. Chain of five C with the double bond at C2 and a methyl branch at C4: CH₃–CH=CH–CH₂? no, write it flat: CH₃–CH=CH–CH(CH₃)–CH₃ with the branch on the fourth carbon.

b. At the C2=C3 double bond each side has two different groups (C2: H and CH₃; C3: H and the branched propyl group), so cis/trans isomerism exists: 2 stereoisomers. No asymmetric C anywhere (C4 has two CH₃ groups): no mirror-image isomerism. So there are two stereoisomers, not four.

c. Addition of Br₂ across the double bond: 2,3-dibromo-4-methylpentane.

d. The two double-bond carbons open and chain: –[CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃CH(CH₃)₂)]–ₙ repeated at least three times (write the chain carbons explicitly, branches hanging).

e. In the copolymer, ethene units and the substituted units lie mixed in one chain — roughly alternating or in longer blocks of one kind, depending on feed and catalyst: –CH₂–CH₂– next to –CH(CH₃)–CH(CH(CH₃)–CH₃)–; that mixing disturbs crystallisation and gives a more rubbery material.

**Common mistakes:** miscounting the parent chain (place the double bond at C2 from the end giving the lowest numbers); counting C4 as a stereocentre (two identical methyls kill it); drawing the polymer with the branch inside the backbone (branches hang outside the chain).

**Points note (official key):** a 2p, b 2p, c 2p, d 3p, e 2p.

## Question 6 (official paper): Kolbe electrolysis giving an alkane (20 points)

**Asked:** the two half-reactions of phenylacetic acid with persulfate (a); the two partial equations toward making the sodium pentanoate and their sum, plus why partial reaction 2 is complete (b); explain the styrene polymerisation evidence (c); maximum octane from 0.20 mol pentanoic acid + 0.020 mol sodium pentanoate (d1), and the CO₂ volume produced (d2); two other carboxylic acids giving octane (e); compare mol octane per mol CO₂ versus the single-acid method (f).

**Subdomains:** C01 (redox, electrolysis, acid-base), C04 (radical chain), D03 (addition polymerisation), C02 (stoichiometry with gas volume). **BINAS:** V_m = 24.5 L mol⁻¹.

**Solution (ours):**

a. Anode-type oxidation (the acid loses electrons): 2 C₆H₅CH₂COOH → C₆H₅CH₂CH₂C₆H₅ + 2 CO₂ + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ (two carboxyls each shed CO₂ and the radicals couple). Reduction: S₂O₈²⁻ + 2 e⁻ → 2 SO₄²⁻.

b1. Partial 1: 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 Na → 2 C₂H₅O⁻ + 2 Na⁺ + H₂(g) (sodium reduces the alcohol). Partial 2: C₂H₅O⁻ + C₄H₉COOH → C₂H₅OH + C₄H₉COO⁻ (the strong base ethanolate deprotonates the carboxylic acid; twice). Sum: 2 Na + 2 C₄H₉COOH → H₂(g) + 2 C₄H₉COO⁻ + 2 Na⁺ (the ethanol cancels).
b2. Partial 2 is a complete acid-base reaction: it moves a proton from the stronger acid (pentanoic, carboxylic, K_z way bigger) to the stronger base (ethanolate): ethanol is the much weaker acid, so the equilibrium lies far right — "strong acid + strong base driving irreversibly to weak acid + weak base".

c. The mechanism first makes radicals (the carboxylate is oxidised, the acyl radical splits CO₂ and leaves a butyl radical). Such a radical that meets a styrene molecule opens its double bond (radical adds to the end) and there starts the radical chain of an addition polymerisation: the butyl radical is the initiator — hence some polystyrene forms. Finding polystyrene is itself evidence that radicals were in the flask (a radical trap test).

d1. Bookkeeping: the cathode converts all 0.20 mol pentanoic acid to pentanoate (2 RCOOH + 2 e⁻ → H₂ + 2 RCOO⁻), while pushing 0.20 mol of electrons into the anode; the anode oxidises carboxylate: 2 RCOO⁻ → R–R + 2 CO₂ + 2 e⁻, and keeps going as long as electrons come (the ethanol then takes over at the cathode): all 0.020 + 0.20 = 0.22 mol carboxylate can be anodised. Maximum octane: 0.11 mol.

d2. n(CO₂) = n(carboxylate consumed) = 0.22 mol → V = 0.22 × 24.5 = 5.4 dm³.

e. R–R must total 8 carbons without touching pentanoic acid: butanoic acid (C₃H₇–COOH) with hexanoic acid (C₅H₁₁–COOH), or propanoic acid (C₂H₅–COOH) with heptanoic acid (C₆H₁₃–COOH): both pairs give C₈H₁₈ (as one of the products).

f. Smaller: with two different acids the radicals combine in more ways (R₁–R₁, R₁–R₂, R₂–R₂), so only a fraction of the couplings is octane, and each CO₂ still counts the same: fewer mol octane per mol CO₂ than with one acid.

**Common mistakes:** in d1 forgetting the cathode converts the acid to the very ion the anode burns (so the salt from part b is just the starter); d2 making CO₂ = mol octane instead of 2× (read step 2 of the mechanism: one CO₂ per carboxylate, one octane per two carboxylates); in e, choosing chains whose R-groups cannot combine to C8 (recount the carbons carefully: the acid loses its COOH carbon as CO₂).

**Points note (official key):** a 2p, b1 3p, b2 3p, c 3p, d1 3p, d2 2p, e 2p, f 2p.
