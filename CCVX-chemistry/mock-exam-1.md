### chem-mock1-q01 | points 12 | outcome C1/C5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 1 — Carbonated spring water**

A bottling plant presses carbon dioxide into natural spring water at 4.0 bar. In the closed bottle an equilibrium exists between dissolved CO₂ and the water. Carbon dioxide in water behaves as a weak acid, 'H₂CO₃' (K_z = 4.5·10⁻⁷, Binas).

a (3p). Give the equilibrium equation for the reaction of carbon dioxide with water.

b (4p). The bottled water contains 0.15 mol of dissolved CO₂ per litre. Calculate the pH of the water in the closed bottle.

c (3p). Explain why the water still tastes clearly acidic, although only a small part of the CO₂ has reacted (use the magnitude of K_z in your explanation).

d (2p). The bottle is opened. Explain with the reaction quotient Q what happens next, in terms of the escape of CO₂.
#### Answer
a. CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l) ⇌ H₃O⁺(aq) + HCO₃⁻(aq)
b. pH = 3.59
c. [H₃O⁺] = 2.6·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ is small in absolute terms but a thousand times larger than in neutral water, so it is plainly acidic; K_z being tiny only means a small fraction reacts — not that the effect disappears.
d. CO₂ escapes, [CO₂] drops, so Q becomes larger than K; the equilibrium reacts left (H₃O⁺ + HCO₃⁻ recombine into CO₂ + water) and dissolved CO₂ keeps leaving the open bottle until Q = K again — the water keeps fizzing and slowly goes flatter.
#### Solution
1. a: 'H₂CO₃' ⇌ HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺, written with CO₂ + 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HCO₃⁻; one equilibrium arrow.
2. b: K_z = x²/(0.15 − x) ≈ x²/0.15 → x = [H₃O⁺] = √(4.5·10⁻⁷ × 0.15) = 2.6·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ (check: x ≪ 0.15); pH = −log(2.6·10⁻⁴) = 3.59.
3. c: neutral water holds 1.0·10⁻⁷ mol L⁻¹; 2.6·10⁻⁴ is ~2600 times more; taste and indicators respond to [H₃O⁺].
4. d: opening drops the CO₂ pressure, so dissolved CO₂ leaves the liquid; Q = [H₃O⁺][HCO₃⁻]/[CO₂] then rises above K, so the net reaction goes to the left: H₃O⁺ and HCO₃⁻ recombine into CO₂ + water until Q = K again at the lower [CO₂].

### chem-mock1-q02 | points 13 | outcome B4/C1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 2 — Anodising an aluminium frame**

Bicycle manufacturers anodise aluminium parts: the frame is made the positive electrode in a sulfuric acid bath and a thick, porous oxide layer grows on it.

a (3p). Aluminium has V₀(Al³⁺/Al) = −1.66 V and is therefore a strong reductor. Explain why it can still be used as a frame material in air.

b (4p). At the positive electrode aluminium is oxidised and with water forms aluminium oxide; hydrogen gas forms at the (inert) negative electrode. Give the half-reactions at both electrodes in acidic solution and the overall reaction.

c (3p). In one batch, 5.4 g of aluminium is converted in the oxide layer. Calculate the volume of hydrogen (in L) formed at 298 K and p = p₀.

d (3p). The fresh oxide layer is porous and is coloured by dipping in a dye solution. Explain, at particle level, why the dye remains in the layer (name the process and the bonding).
#### Answer
a. Al forms a dense, adherent Al₂O₃ skin (a protective layer) that seals the metal from oxygen and water; the strong reductor cannot reach the air.
b. + electrode: 2 Al + 3 H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 6 H⁺ + 6 e⁻; − electrode: 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ → H₂; total: 2 Al + 3 H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3 H₂(g)
c. 7.4 L
d. Adsorption: the oxide's large porous surface holds the dye molecules by Van der Waals bonds and hydrogen bridges; the dye is mechanically adsorbed into the pores before the layer is sealed (boiling in hot water closes the pores).
#### Solution
1. a: V₀ says aluminium wants to give electrons; the oxide skin blocks contact — passivation. Rust on iron is porous and flakes off; alumina is adherent.
2. b: Al: 2 Al + 3 H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 6 H⁺ + 6 e⁻ (O from H₂O, H balance, charge balanced with e⁻). Cathode: 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ → H₂. Add ×3: 6 H⁺ + 6 e⁻ → 3 H₂. Sum: 2 Al + 3 H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3 H₂(g).
3. c: n(Al) = 5.4/26.98 = 0.200 mol; ratio Al : H₂ = 2 : 3 → n(H₂) = 0.300 mol; V = 0.300 × 24.5 = 7.4 L.
4. d: porous oxide = huge surface; dye molecules adsorb (hydrogen bridges, Van der Waals) — same principle as activated carbon; sealing in boiling water swells/closes the pores and locks the dye in.

### chem-mock1-q03 | points 12 | outcome C1/F2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 3 — De-icing without chloride**

Calcium magnesium acetate (CMA), Ca(CH₃COO)₂, is used as a de-icer that does not attack concrete reinforcement. It can be made from limestone and acetic acid.

a (2p). Give the equation for the reaction of calcium carbonate with acetic acid (acetic acid written as a molecule).

b (4p). A works produces 500 kg of limestone (pure CaCO₃). Calculate the mass of CMA obtained if the yield is 85% (M(CMA) = 158.2 g mol⁻¹).

c (3p). Explain why CMA is friendlier to reinforced concrete than NaCl or CaCl₂ (think of the chloride ion and of the steel inside).

d (3p). Calculate the atom economy of the CMA production reaction.
#### Answer
a. CaCO₃(s) + 2 CH₃COOH(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 CH₃COO⁻(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)
b. 6.7·10² kg
c. Chloride ions penetrate concrete and destroy the steel's protective oxide layer (pitting corrosion of rebar); acetate does not attack the passive layer, and CMA supplies no chloride at all, so the reinforcement stays protected.
d. 71.8%
#### Solution
1. a: weak acid as molecule; carbonate insoluble salt as (s): CaCO₃(s) + 2 CH₃COOH(aq) → Ca²⁺ + 2 CH₃COO⁻ + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l); charge check: left 0, right +2 − 2 = 0 ✓; elements balance ✓.
2. b: n(CaCO₃) = 500,000/100.09 = 4.996·10³ mol; ratio 1:1 → n(CMA) = 4.996·10³ mol; theoretical 4.996·10³ × 158.17 = 790 kg; with 85%: 0.85 × 790 = 672 kg. Answer: 6.7·10² kg.
3. c: Cl⁻ destroys the passive Fe₂O₃-rich layer on steel (pitting) — the reason for concrete spalling; acetate ion is a far weaker corrosive agent and there is no Cl⁻ source.
4. d: AE = 158.17 / (100.09 + 2×60.05) = 158.17/220.19 = 0.718 = 71.8%.

### chem-mock1-q04 | points 10 | outcome D1/C2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 4 — Nitrate in baby spinach**

Growers fertilise spinach with nitrate. In a lab check, 50 g of spinach is blended with 250 mL of water and filtered. The extract's nitrate is reduced to nitrite; the nitrite is then coupled to a purple dye, whose absorbance is measured. The calibration line reads A = 0.52 × c (c in μg mL⁻¹ nitrite). The spinach extract, diluted 10 times, reads A = 0.31.

a (3p). Calculate the nitrite concentration of the undiluted extract.

b (4p). Calculate the nitrate content of the spinach in mg nitrate per 100 g (nitrite and nitrate contain one N atom each).

c (3p). Babies fed large amounts of high-nitrate spinach can get 'blue baby syndrome'. Explain in one sentence the chemistry chain that frightens doctors (name the key oxidation).
#### Answer
a. 6.0 μg mL⁻¹
b. 4.0 mg per 100 g (≈ 40 mg kg⁻¹)
c. In the baby's less acidic stomach, nitrate is reduced to nitrite, which oxidises Fe²⁺ in haemoglobin to Fe³⁺ (methaemoglobin), and that form cannot bind oxygen — the baby literally turns blue.
#### Solution
1. a: diluted A = 0.31 → c_diluted = 0.31/0.52 = 0.596 μg mL⁻¹; undiluted: × 10 = 6.0 μg mL⁻¹.
2. b: total nitrite in 250 mL: 6.0 × 250 = 1.5·10³ μg. One nitrate gives one nitrite: n = 1.5·10³ / 46.01 = 32.6 μmol; m(nitrate in 50 g) = 32.6 × 62.01 = 2.0·10³ μg = 2.0 mg per 50 g, hence 4.0 mg per 100 g (40 mg kg⁻¹) — far below the vegetable limits (which run to thousands of mg kg⁻¹ for adult consumption).
3. c: the dangerous step is the oxidation of haemoglobin's iron: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺, killing its oxygen-carrying.

### chem-mock1-q05 | points 12 | outcome C1/C5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
**Question 5 — Ester oil for a gearbox**

A lubricant maker synthesises 1-octyl ethanoate from 1-octanol (C₈H₁₇OH, M = 130.2 g mol⁻¹) and acetic acid.

a (3p). Give the esterification equation in structural formulas (equilibrium arrow).

b (3p). During the reaction, the water formed is removed continuously by a water separator. Explain with Q and K why this raises the obtainable yield beyond the equilibrium yield of the closed flask.

c (4p). The chemist starts with 65.1 g of 1-octanol and an excess of acetic acid and obtains 70.5 g of 1-octyl ethanoate (M = 172.3 g mol⁻¹). Calculate the percentage yield.

d (2p). Give the equation, in structural formulas, for the saponification of this ester with caustic soda.
#### Answer
a. CH₃COOH + CH₃(CH₂)₇OH ⇌ CH₃COO(CH₂)₇CH₃ + H₂O
b. Removing water lowers [H₂O], so Q drops below K; the equilibrium restores Q = K by producing more ester, so with water removal the reaction can be driven essentially to completion.
c. 81.9%
d. CH₃COO(CH₂)₇CH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + CH₃(CH₂)₇OH: octanoate (acetate) ion and 1-octanol.
#### Solution
1. a: acid − OH and alcohol − H split off as H₂O: CH₃COOH + HO(CH₂)₇CH₃ ⇌ CH₃COO(CH₂)₇CH₃ + H₂O.
2. b: Q = [ester][H₂O]/([acid][alcohol]); extracting H₂O shrinks the numerator, Q < K → net forward. The equilibrium K is unchanged; the position at any moment is driven right.
3. c: n(octanol) = 65.1/130.2 = 0.500 mol; ratio 1:1 → theoretical ester: 0.500 × 172.3 = 86.1 g; yield = 70.5/86.1 × 100% = 81.9%.
4. d: OH⁻ hydrolyses the ester bond: CH₃COO(CH₂)₇CH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + HO(CH₂)₇CH₃ (saponification).

### chem-mock1-q06 | points 13 | outcome F1/C2 | difficulty hard
#### Question
**Question 6 — SCR in a container ship**

A ship's engine flue gas carries 800 mg of nitrogen monoxide (NO) per m³. An SCR (selective catalytic reduction) unit injects ammonia upstream of a catalyst: 4 NH₃ + 6 NO → 5 N₂ + 6 H₂O. The ship moves 1000 m³ of flue gas per hour.

a (3p). Show that the equation as given is balanced (element balance), and name the oxidator and the reductor.

b (4p). Calculate the mass of ammonia (in kg) needed per hour at stoichiometric dosing.

c (3p). The SCR unit runs at 350 °C, where the reaction is slow without a catalyst. Explain what the catalyst does here in terms of activation energy and equilibrium position.

d (3p). Operators avoid over-dosing ammonia. Name the problem that "ammonia slip" creates downstream, both chemically and environmentally.
#### Answer
a. Balance: N: 4 + 6 = 10 = 5 × 2 ✓; H: 4 × 3 = 12 = 6 × 2 ✓; O: 6 = 6 ✓. Oxidator: NO (it is reduced to N₂; it takes up electrons/the reductor's hydrogen); reductor: NH₃ (N goes from −3 to 0).
b. 0.30 kg NH₃ per hour
c. The catalyst lowers the activation energy (rates rise strongly at this temperature) without changing K or the position of equilibrium; it lets an otherwise too-slow conversion run at engine-exhaust temperature.
d. Unreacted NH₃ slips through: it is a toxic, pungent gas (air pollution, odour) and it reacts with acid components of the exhaust (e.g. sulfuric acid) to ammonium salts that foul cooler parts and add fine particulate matter.
#### Solution
1. a: count N, H, O on both sides (above); oxidise/reduce by oxidation number: N in NO is +2 → 0 (reduced: NO oxidator); N in NH₃ is −3 → 0 (oxidised: NH₃ reductor).
2. b: NO per hour: 1000 × 800 mg = 8.0·10² g; n(NO) = 800/30.01 = 26.7 mol h⁻¹; NH₃ : NO = 4 : 6 → n(NH₃) = 17.8 mol h⁻¹; m = 17.8 × 17.03 = 303 g = 0.30 kg h⁻¹.
3. c: catalyst: lower Ea → much faster at 350 °C; K unchanged (temperature sets K; catalyst does not). The equilibrium lay far right anyway; it was kinetics, not position, that limited.
4. d: NH₃ slip: pungent and toxic air pollutant near the ship; also reacts with acid gases to ammonium sulfate/nitrate particles (fouling and secondary fine dust).

### chem-mock1-q07 | points 8 | outcome C4/0-05 | difficulty easy
#### Question
**Question 7 — Fizzy bath bomb**

A bath bomb contains solid citric acid (H₃C₆H₅O₇, M = 192.1 g mol⁻¹) and solid sodium hydrogencarbonate. In water they fizz.

a (3p). Give the equation for the acid-base reaction (citric acid is triprotic; write it as a molecule).

b (3p). A bomb contains 2.1 g of citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate in excess. Calculate the volume of CO₂ released at 298 K and p = p₀.

c (2p). A dissolving tablet releases its gas much faster in warm water, and the bath-bomb maker also grinds the powders fine. Explain both effects with the collision model.
#### Answer
a. 3 NaHCO₃(s) + H₃C₆H₅O₇(s) → 3 Na⁺(aq) + C₆H₅O₇³⁻(aq) + 3 CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l)
b. 0.80 L
c. Warmer water: more and harder collisions, both more frequent and more often effective (above the activation energy), so the reaction runs faster; finer powder: more surface area of reacting substance in contact with the solution, so more collisions per second.
#### Solution
1. a: the triprotic citric acid donates 3 H⁺, each neutralising one HCO₃⁻ to CO₂ + H₂O: 3 NaHCO₃ + H₃C₆H₅O₇ → 3 Na⁺ + C₆H₅O₇³⁻ + 3 CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l).
2. b: n(citric acid) = 2.1/192.1 = 1.1·10⁻² mol; ratio 1:3 → n(CO₂) = 3.3·10⁻² mol; V = 3.3·10⁻² × 24.5 = 0.80 L (two significant figures).
3. c: temperature effect: energy distribution — more collisions pass Ea (large effect); finer powder: surface area (sites where acid and carbonate can meet and react) — more collisions per time.
