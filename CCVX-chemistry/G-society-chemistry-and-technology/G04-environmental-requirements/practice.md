# G4 — Environmental requirements: practice

Eight original questions on the G4 learning outcomes, in CCVX style.

### chem-g04-q01 | points 2 | outcome G4 | difficulty easy
#### Question
How is drinking-water quality "guaranteed" in the Netherlands from source to tap? Name the two chemical steps of treatment that remove dissolved iron and pathogens, and the monitoring layer behind them.
#### Answer
Aeration (air blown through: dissolved Fe²⁺ oxidises to Fe³⁺, which precipitates as Fe(OH)₃ and is filtered out on sand beds) and disinfection/barrier steps (UV, ozone or membrane filtration — UV/ozone inactivate microbes) close the gas/biological risk; the guarantee comes from the statutory limits on water quality (drinking-water directive values) with continuous laboratory sampling by the water companies.
#### Solution
1. Iron: 4 Fe²⁺ + O₂ + 10 H₂O → 4 Fe(OH)₃(s) + 8 H⁺: oxidation + precipitation + filtration.
2. Microorganisms: UV or ozone inactivation (and multi-barrier design), meeting the legal limits for every parameter.
3. Quality guarantee = measured compliance, not trust: a water company's public lab reports are the proof.

### chem-g04-q02 | points 3 | outcome G4 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Air quality from a process stack is protected in several layers. Name the pollutant classes that are monitored, and describe one cleaning unit per class.
#### Answer
Particles (dust/fly ash): electrostatic precipitators or bag filters (charged particles collected on plates). Acid gases (SO₂, HCl): wet scrubbers with lime/alkali (2 OH⁻ + SO₂ → SO₃²⁻ + H₂O), with gypsum as product. NOₓ: selective catalytic reduction (SCR), 4 NO + 4 NH₃ + O₂ → 4 N₂ + 6 H₂O over a catalyst.
#### Solution
1. Dust: charged in a field and collected (ESP works for fly ash at coal plants); filters for fine particles.
2. SO₂ and HCl are acidic gases: absorbed and neutralised in alkaline scrubbers.
3. NOₓ is converted catalytically: ammonia reduces NO/NO₂ to N₂ — the flue gases leave mostly as N₂, H₂O and CO₂.

### chem-g04-q03 | points 3 | outcome G4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A former petrol station in a city is to be rebuilt as a playground. The soil survey finds petrol (benzene/petrol vapours) and lead. Explain (1) why the soil quality rule demands action before building, (2) describe one remediation approach for each contaminant and its chemical background, and (3) one reuse principle the cleaned soil or space should meet.
#### Answer
1. Children playing contact soil directly (ingestion, vapours indoors): human exposure is maximal, so under soil-quality rules the values exceed the intervention levels — the land must be remediated before the new use. 2. Petrol: in-situ air sparging/soil vapour extraction (the volatile hydrocarbons are pulled out and destroyed above ground or biologically degraded — volatility as property); lead: excavation and removal as hazardous waste (lead does not decompose) or immobilisation with phosphates (precipitation of poorly soluble lead phosphates, keeping it in place — solubility as property). 3. Reuse as playground requires after-care monitoring of vapour and groundwater so the playground standard is not just achieved once but guaranteed.
#### Solution
1. Land use decides the standard: playground > office (exposure routes of soil ingestion and indoor vapour).
2. Two contaminants, two properties, two approaches (volatility for petrol-hydrocarbons; immobilisation/removal for Pb).
3. The guarantee is re-measured after the works: soil quality is an ongoing state, not a one-time event.

### chem-g04-q04 | points 3 | outcome G4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
How is the quality of food with respect to chemical contaminants guaranteed in the EU? Describe the chain: setting of limits (ADI/MRL), measurement, and enforcement, with one example.
#### Answer
Acceptable daily intake (ADI) values are set from toxicology (the maximum daily dose a person can take lifelong without harm); maximum residue limits (MRLs) in foods follow from the ADI and the diet; enforcement laboratories check samples with chromatography and mass spectrometry (see D01) against those limits, and products above them are recalled. Example: a pesticide's MRL on grapes — residues are measured; batches over the limit are withdrawn.
#### Solution
1. Science first: the ADI is toxicological; the MRL converts it into an enforceable number per product.
2. Measurement: GC/HPLC + MS identify and quantify contaminants at µg/kg level (the D01 techniques, used exactly as the G2/G4 guarantee machinery).
3. Enforcement: surveillance programmes and recalls; food quality is a system, not a sticker.

### chem-g04-q05 | points 4 | outcome G4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Sewage sludge ash is increasingly the source of recovered phosphorus (e.g. as struvite, MgNH₄PO₄·6H₂O, or phosphoric acid). (1) Which "quality" problem with sludge does this address? (2) Describe the cycle thinking behind it. (3) Name one condition for the recycled product to be actually usable on fields.
#### Answer
1. The phosphorus content itself: dumping sludge loses a vital raw material and can overload water/water-quality systems with P (eutrophication pressure); recovering it addresses both the reuse of natural resources and the quality of water and soil. 2. Wastewater collects P from food; the P ends in sludge; recovering struvite or acid closes the P-cycle back to fertiliser instead of harming water quality (a cradle-to-cradle approach for a scarce global resource). 3. The recycled product must meet fertiliser-quality standards (heavy-metal and organic-contaminant limits, e.g. limits set by fertiliser regulation), or it cannot be used on food land.
#### Solution
1. Phosphorus is finite (mining) and a pollutant at the same time: recovery turns a burden into a resource.
2. This is the textbook "reuse of natural resources": product cycles replace one-way flows (cradle-to-cradle thinking applied to an element).
3. A recovered fertiliser must pass the same safety bar as mined fertiliser (else farms could not use it) — chemistry and quality standards travel together.

### chem-g04-q06 | points 4 | outcome G4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A carpet company changes from selling carpet to leasing it, and writes into the design that tiles can be returned, depolymerised and remade. (1) Which framework names this approach? (2) Give two reasons this beats conventional recycling economically and environmentally. (3) What is redesigned in the "business model"?
#### Answer
1. Cradle-to-cradle (technical cycle design). 2. Environmental: carpet tiles come back at end-of-life and become new carpet (closed loop: no landfill, no virgin crude for new yarn); economic: the manufacturer keeps the value of the expensive polymer instead of losing it to waste, and the customer is locked into a service relation. 3. The business model shifts from selling a product (ownership of waste moves to the customer) to providing a service (floor covering as a service; the producer keeps ownership of the material), so responsibility for end-of-life belongs where design decisions live.
#### Solution
1. C2C decomposes products into a biological cycle (compostables) and a technical cycle (polymers/metals kept at quality): carpet is designed to be disassembled.
2. Leasing aligns incentives: the owner of the material has the motive to reprocess it (design for disassembly, deposit, take-back logistics).
3. G4 asks: judge a measure; the measure here is the model, not just the chemistry.

### chem-g04-q07 | points 4 | outcome G4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The Lansink hierarchy sets the order: (1) prevention, (2) re-use, (3) recycling (material), (4) incineration with energy recovery, (5) landfill. Apply it to three waste flows: glass bottles, old phones, apple cores. Justify your top choice per flow with the materials involved.
#### Answer
Glass bottles: prevention → re-use (wash/refill: the material is one closed circle and the bottle does not have to be remelted, so big energy gain) → recycling as C2. Old phones: prevention (longer use by design) → re-use (refurbished phone: the expensive assembly and rare metals stay intact) → recycling (material recovery of Au, Cu, cobalt — the elements are conserved, the precious assembly is not). Apple cores: organics: prevention (food planning) → re-use is limited; the right top is digestion/composting (the carbon cycle and nutrients return to soil: "biological cycle" in C2C terms), which sits between re-use and recycling in the spirit of the ladder.
#### Solution
1. The ladder prefers options that keep value closest to the product: repairing > remaking > breaking into raw material.
2. Glass: reuse beats melting every time (compare F02, f02-q08; melting is energy).
3. Phones: the assembly contains the real value (rare metals in highly processed form): keep the product whole as long as possible; recover materials only at the end.
4. Biodegradable waste: its best loop is the biological one (digestion to biogas and compost), not incineration.

### chem-g04-q08 | points 4 | outcome G4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A city councillor proposes: "Every new building in the city must be 50% built from secondary (recycled) materials." Analyse this proposal per the G4 qualities table: name one quality it promotes, one trade-off or risk it hides, and judge whether the measure is better specified by weight or by function, with your recommendation.
#### Answer
Promotes: reuse of natural resources (less quarrying/cement: natural resources quality) and less landfill (soil quality; and space). Hidden trade-off: some "secondary" streams bury hazardous legacy into new structures (old concrete with attached pollutants, gypsum with additives: soil and health qualities) — and structural safety: 50% by weight of what? Judged by weight is a blunt instrument (it mixes pebbles with plastics): judge by function (e.g. elements and materials streams certified for reuse, with declared composition and life-cycle data), because safety (building physics) and health cannot be traded against tonnage targets. Recommendation: set a functional target with certified streams + composition data rather than a flat weight percentage.
#### Solution
1. The measure's good sides: conserving natural resources, diverting waste from landfill (guaranteeing the same qualities with less extraction).
2. Risks: recycled content can carry contaminants (soil quality): composition control needed; and a weight target invites heaviness games (adding glass ballast counts).
3. G4 reasoning: quality of water/air/soil/food is guaranteed by standards and monitoring — construction materials need the same logic: certified streams, not target tonnages.
