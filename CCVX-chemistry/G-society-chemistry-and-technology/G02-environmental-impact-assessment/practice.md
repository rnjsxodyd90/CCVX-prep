# G2 — Environmental impact assessment: practice

Eight original questions on the G2 learning outcome, in CCVX style.

### chem-g02-q01 | points 3 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain what an emission limit is, and name two reasons why society sets such limits for a chemical production process.
#### Answer
An emission limit is a legally fixed maximum for how much of a pollutant (e.g. mg of SO₂ per m³ of flue gas, or kg per year) an installation may release. It is set to protect health (direct toxicity in the surroundings) and the environment (acidification, eutrophication), while allowing the socially useful production to continue.
#### Solution
1. Limits translate toxicology and environmental science into numbers a plant can be measured and fined against.
2. Grounds: preventing harm to people (e.g. airway irritation) and to ecosystems (e.g. nutrient overload), given that a zero-emission plant is not technically or economically possible; the limit is the negotiated balance point.
3. The G2 context always weighs production (work, materials) against those conditions — the "social conditions" of the outcome.

### chem-g02-q02 | points 3 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A chemical plant on an estuary also receives raw materials and ships products and waste by water and rail. Name three transport-related conditions from the G2.1 list and give one concrete risk per condition.
#### Answer
1. Transport of raw materials: a tanker or train accident can spill a large load at once (e.g. chlorine or oil into the estuary). 2. Transport of waste: hazardous waste shipments can leak or be dumped illegally on the road. 3. Use of (cooling) water: the plant's intake and discharge change temperature and oxygen content of the estuary; large flow changes stress fish.
#### Solution
1. Accidents with bulk transports are rare but high-impact — that is why routing, double hulls and emergency plans exist.
2. Waste on the road is a crime vector: without manifests and checks, material ends up where nobody monitors it.
3. The waterway is also the plant's coolant and source: intake structures and thermal discharge have their own permits.

### chem-g02-q03 | points 4 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A paper factory wants to double its use of river cooling water. Two neighbours object: a rowing club and a water company extracting drinking water downstream. Evaluate this case for the factory's management with the concepts from G2.1, listing the environmental consequences it must quantify before it can even ask for a permit.
#### Answer
Cooling water warms the river: at a higher temperature the solubility of O₂ falls (Binas, gas solubility) while fish and micro-organisms respire faster — a double oxygen squeeze. The intake and outfall also trap fish and change flow. Before applying, the factory must model emissions (ΔT, heat load per m³), the mixing with the river, the O₂ budget against downstream uses (rowing, fishing, the drinking-water intake), and whether limits for heat discharge would be violated — then weigh that against the production gain and alternatives (cooling towers).
#### Solution
1. Cooling-water use is an explicitly named G2 environmental condition: the "damage" is not a toxic chemical, but heat.
2. Solubility of gases in water drops with temperature (Binas): less oxygen per litre, plus faster biological oxygen demand → hypoxic zones possible.
3. Alternatives enter the assessment: closed cooling towers shift the heat to air at a price in electricity and noise, so the permit decision is a quantified trade-off, not a veto or a free pass.

### chem-g02-q04 | points 3 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A chemical site stores 40 tonnes of chlorine. Distinguish hazard from risk for this site, explain why an emergency is not ruled out by long accident-free operation, and name two technical barriers against the emergency.
#### Answer
Hazard is the intrinsic ability to do harm (chlorine is acutely toxic to lungs at low ppm); risk is its interplay with probability and exposure (probability × effect). A long quiet period tells about the past, not about rare events (seals, corrosion, transport collisions), each with a small probability and a large effect. Barriers: double-walled or strongly overdesigned pressure tanks with remote shutoff valves, and gas detectors linked to automatic water curtains/isolation; plus organisational barriers like emergency plans and drills with the fire brigade.
#### Solution
1. Hazard is fixed by the substance; risk is what you can manage: reduce probability (maintenance, procedures) and exposure (distance, ventilation, detection).
2. Low frequency is the only thing a long accident-free period measures; the large effect of one chlorine release is why regulation keeps the emergency case mandatory in the assessment.
3. Layers ("what if one fails?") are the standard answer in any risk section: technical design + detection + response.

### chem-g02-q05 | points 4 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A greenhouse horticulture company installs a gas engine with a generator: it sells the electricity, uses the engine's cooling water and exhaust CO₂ in the greenhouses. (1) What is this arrangement called? (2) Explain the sustainability gain with an energy balance of 100 J of natural gas in a normal power station (40 J electricity, 60 J waste heat) versus this arrangement (35 J electricity, 45 J heat to the greenhouse, plus the CO₂ replacing a gas-fired CO₂ supplier in the greenhouses). (3) Name one condition needed for this to be truly sustainable.
#### Answer
1. Combined heat and power (CHP, warmte-kracht) with CO₂ reuse. 2. The CHP arrangement converts 80% of the fuel energy into electricity plus useful heat (instead of 40% alone), and the scrubbed exhaust CO₂ substitutes for CO₂ the grower would otherwise have made by burning more gas — so one joule serves two services and total gas burned for the same services drops. 3. The heat and CO₂ must actually be needed year-round by the greenhouses (otherwise the heat has to be dumped and the profit evaporates).
#### Solution
1. CHP: heat and power together; with CO₂ feeding it is a greenhouse triple.
2. Balance comparison: for 100 J of fuel, standalone electricity gives 40 J useful; CHP plus CO₂ reuse gives 35 J electricity + 45 J heat + avoided CO₂-production fuel — markedly better.
3. Demand matching is the catch (see chem-f03-q09): in warm weeks the heat demand is low, so the plant runs for electricity only and the profit is lost — honest sustainability accounting counts the seasonal reality, not just the design figure.

### chem-g02-q06 | points 3 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Trace the full chain from a coal plant's stack to marble statues dissolving in a German city, and name the reactions on the way.
#### Answer
The stack's SO₂ and NOₓ travel with the wind, are oxidised and dissolved in cloud water: SO₂ → H₂SO₃ → H₂SO₄ (2 SO₂ + O₂ → 2 SO₃, SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄) and NO₂ → HNO₃; acid rain then attacks carbonate stone: 2 H₃O⁺ + CaCO₃ → Ca²⁺ + CO₂ + 3 H₂O, so statues and limestone facades slowly dissolve.
#### Solution
1. Acid deposition chain: emission (coal with sulfur) → oxidation in the atmosphere (SO₃, NO₂ intermediates) → dissolution in rain as sulfuric and nitric acids → deposition far away (transboundary transport).
2. Carbonates react with the acid: marble and limestone fizz themselves to soluble salts and CO₂ — the very equation of C01 (acid + insoluble carbonate).
3. The chain explains why the Netherlands/Sweden fought British coal emissions in the 1980s — and why scrubbers and low-NOₓ burners are in the F3.4/G2.1 emission story.

### chem-g02-q07 | points 3 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Dioxins and dioxin-like PCBs are regulated at extremely low concentrations, far below most other chemicals. Explain this using their chemical fate: persistence, solubility and position in the food chain, and give one consequence for human exposure routes.
#### Answer
Dioxins are very persistent (they resist breakdown in the environment) and apolar: they dissolve in fats, not in water, so they concentrate in the fatty tissue of organisms and biomagnify up the food chain (plankton → fish → fish-eater → human); the route to humans is mainly food (fatty fish, dairy, meat), so even tiny environmental concentrations matter at the top, which is why the allowable limits are so low.
#### Solution
1. Chemically inert + apolar = long-lived and fat-seeking: the two structural facts behind persistence and bioaccumulation.
2. Each trophic level concentrates the substance roughly an order of magnitude: top predators end up with the highest body burden.
3. Human intake is dominated by diet, not by air: milk, fatty fish, meat. Therefore the permitted concentrations in emissions are a small fraction of those for non-accumulating pollutants — exposure route decides the limit.

### chem-g02-q08 | points 4 | outcome G2.1 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A municipality must choose between waste incineration with energy recovery and landfilling with methane capture. List for each option two environmental conditions from G2.1 it best satisfies and two it violates, and close with your reasoned recommendation for a city with district heating.
#### Answer
Incineration with energy recovery: satisfies (a) energy recovery as CHP (replaces fossil fuel for the city network) and (b) small residues for landfill; violates (a) flue-gas emissions needing costly cleaning (NOₓ, HCl, dioxins) and (b) fly ash as hazardous waste. Landfill with capture: satisfies (a) lower immediate emissions at the site (gas is collected as biogas) and (b) lower operating cost and no demand for district heating; violates (a) soil/groundwater and methane leakage risks for decades after closure and (b) permanent loss of the energy and of the space (land use). Recommendation: incineration with energy recovery, because the district heating network takes the CHP heat, so the waste's energy is truly used — but flue-gas cleaning and ash management must be in the contract.
#### Solution
1. Assessment = compare named conditions per option: emissions, waste, energy recovery, land use, long-term risk.
2. Incineration scores on energy (for a heating city) and volume, pays with flue-gas cleaning and ash; landfill scores on local emissions, pays with persistent risk and lost energy.
3. The deciding local condition is the heat sink (see chem-f03-q09): with district heating the incinerator's joules find a customer; without it, the comparison changes — G2 judgment is local, not ideological.
