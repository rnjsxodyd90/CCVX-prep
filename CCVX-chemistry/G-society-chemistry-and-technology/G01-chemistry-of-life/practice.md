# G1 — Chemistry of life: practice

Fifteen original questions on the G1 learning outcomes, in CCVX style.

### chem-g01-q01 | points 3 | outcome G1.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A person eats bread (starch). Describe the path from that nutrient to the person's own substance, naming the breakdown step and one "self substance" that is built from the breakdown products.
#### Answer
Starch is first hydrolysed to glucose in the gut; the glucose is absorbed and then used as building block — for example re-linked to glycogen (the body's own energy-storage carbohydrate in liver and muscles) or used to build other molecules.
#### Solution
1. Nutrients are broken down in the digestive system: the polysaccharide starch is hydrolysed by enzymes (amylases) to the monosaccharide glucose.
2. The breakdown products enter the blood and the cells, where they can be used as raw material: glucose is polymerised to glycogen (storage), or converted and built into the body's own substances.
3. Principle of G1.1: breakdown → building blocks → own substance.

### chem-g01-q02 | points 3 | outcome G1.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Name and describe the first three structural levels of a protein, and state which bonds hold each of the second and third levels together.
#### Answer
Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids in the chain (peptide bonds). Secondary structure: regular folding such as the α-helix or the β-sheet (hydrogen bridges between backbone C=O and N–H). Tertiary structure: the total 3D fold of the chain into its working shape (ionic bonds, hydrogen bridges, Van der Waals bonds, and disulfide bridges between cysteine units).
#### Solution
1. Primary: the order of the amino acids, set at the ribosome; this alone determines all the rest.
2. Secondary: hydrogen bridges between the backbone's O of C=O and H of N–H hold the chain in an α-helix or β-sheet locally.
3. Tertiary: the whole chain folds further; links between side chains (ionic +/−, hydrogen bridges, Van der Waals between apolar groups, and –S–S– disulfide bridges between two cysteine residues) fix the final 3D shape that the enzyme or antibody needs.

### chem-g01-q03 | points 3 | outcome G1.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Classify glucose, lactose and starch as monosaccharide, disaccharide or polysaccharide, and explain the disaccharide's formation.
#### Answer
Glucose: monosaccharide; lactose: disaccharide (one glucose + one galactose unit, linked by a condensation that splits off water); starch: polysaccharide (a chain of many glucose units).
#### Solution
1. Monosaccharides are single sugar units: glucose, fructose, galactose (all C₆H₁₂O₆, isomers of each other).
2. Two monosaccharides condense (a glycosidic link, with loss of H₂O) to a disaccharide: lactose = glucose + galactose; (maltose = glucose + glucose; sucrose = glucose + fructose).
3. Many units in a chain: polysaccharides such as starch and glycogen (α-links) and cellulose (β-links — indigestible for us).

### chem-g01-q04 | points 4 | outcome G1.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
(1) Write the equation, in words or structural shorthand, for the formation of a triglyceride from glycerol and three stearic acid molecules. (2) Explain the difference between a saturated and an unsaturated fatty acid chain, and what that means for the substance at room temperature.
#### Answer
1. Glycerol + 3 fatty acid ⇌ triglyceride + 3 H₂O (three ester links). 2. A saturated chain has only single C–C bonds (it is "full of hydrogen"); an unsaturated chain holds one or more C=C double bonds, whose cis kinks prevent tight packing — saturated → solid fat at room temperature, unsaturated → liquid oil.
#### Solution
1. Glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol, HO–CH₂–CH(OH)–CH₂–OH) esterifies with three fatty acid molecules: the three –OH groups of glycerol each condense with a COOH group, releasing 3 H₂O: a triglyceride.
2. Saturated: straight chains pack closely with strong Van der Waals bonding → higher melting point → fat (see B04, b04-q08). Unsaturated: kinked chains stack badly → liquid oil.
3. The same structure argument explains butter (mostly saturated) vs sunflower oil (mostly unsaturated), and the hardening of oil to margarine (partial hydrogenation of the kinks).

### chem-g01-q05 | points 3 | outcome G1.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Name the building blocks of DNA and of RNA, stating in each case the sugar, the phosphate part and the bases, and point out the two differences between DNA and RNA.
#### Answer
DNA: sugar deoxyribose, phosphate groups, bases A, C, T, G. RNA: sugar ribose, phosphate groups, bases A, C, U, G. Differences: deoxyribose vs ribose, and T (thymine, DNA) vs U (uracil, RNA).
#### Solution
1. Nucleic acids are chains of nucleotides; a nucleotide = phosphate + sugar + base.
2. DNA: 2-deoxyribose, phosphate, and the bases adenine (A), cytosine (C), thymine (T), guanine (G); RNA: ribose, phosphate, and A, C, uracil (U), G.
3. In the double helix the bases pair complementary: A with T (in RNA: A with U) and G with C, by hydrogen bridges — which is how the code is copied.

### chem-g01-q06 | points 3 | outcome G1.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Describe the digestion of a protein from the plate to the blood, naming the bond that is broken, the type of reaction, and in which form the protein reaches the cells.
#### Answer
The protein's peptide bonds are hydrolysed by enzymes (proteases) in the stomach and small intestine into the separate amino acids; the amino acids are absorbed into the blood and used in the cells to build the body's own proteins.
#### Solution
1. Nutritional protein is broken down, not absorbed as a whole: enzymes (pepsin in the stomach, trypsin etc. in the intestine) hydrolyse the peptide bonds: –CO–NH– + H₂O → –COOH + H₂N–.
2. The breakdown products are the amino acids, absorbed through the gut wall into the blood.
3. Cells then re-link these building blocks into the body's own proteins (protein synthesis, G1.4): digestion is reversed condensation (see C01/C05 hydrolysis).

### chem-g01-q07 | points 4 | outcome G1.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Amino acids eaten in excess cannot be stored. (1) Describe what happens to surplus amino acids in the body. (2) Give the formula and the name of the small molecule that carries the nitrogen out of the body. (3) Explain why direct excretion of ammonia would be a bad solution for people.
#### Answer
1. The surplus amino acids are deaminated (their NH₂/nitrogen part is removed); the nitrogen part is converted in the liver to urea, which the kidneys excrete, and the carbon skeletons are burned for energy or stored as glycogen/fat. 2. Urea: CO(NH₂)₂. 3. Ammonia is highly toxic (a strong base, damages tissues and disturbs pH); urea is far less harmful and much less basic — it can be accumulated and excreted at low cost of water and biology.
#### Solution
1. Excess amino acids are not stored: the body takes the nitrogen off (deamination), uses the carbon part in the energy economy (respiration, or building to glycogen/fat).
2. The nitrogen ends up in urea, CO(NH₂)₂, made in the liver (urea cycle) and removed by the kidneys in urine — one of the body's chemical "waste" products named in the CCVX list.
3. Ammonia (NH₃) is dangerous in the blood; many fish can excrete it by dilution with lots of water, but land animals cannot afford that water loss — hence urea (mammals) or uric acid (birds/reptiles).

### chem-g01-q08 | points 4 | outcome G1.3 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The complete combustion of glucose in the body (respiration) is C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O, ΔE = −2.81·10⁶ J mol⁻¹. (1) Calculate the energy released per gram of glucose (M = 180.16 g mol⁻¹). (2) Fat gives about 38 kJ per gram. Which of the two nutrients is the better energy store per gram and why is carbohydrate still used for short-term storage?
#### Answer
1. 15.6 kJ g⁻¹. 2. Per gram, fat stores more than twice as much energy — that is why the body's long-term reserve is fat; carbohydrate is still used short-term because its breakdown (glucose from glycogen) releases energy quickly and anaerobically-accessibly, and glucose is the fuel the brain depends on.
#### Solution
1. Energy per gram: 2.81·10⁶ J mol⁻¹ / 180.16 g mol⁻¹ = 1.56·10⁴ J g⁻¹ = 15.6 kJ g⁻¹ (three significant figures). (Nutrition labels give ≈ 17 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrates: the small difference comes from the exact definition of water's state and from digestibility.)
2. Fat: about 38 kJ g⁻¹ — more than twice the carbohydrate value. Per mass, fats beat carbohydrates as a depot: the body's long-time store is fatty tissue.
3. But glucose/glycogen releases energy quickly without long transport (glycogen sits in liver and muscle), so it covers short-term demand; the system stores carbohydrate first for readily-available energy and shifts the surplus to fat.

### chem-g01-q09 | points 3 | outcome G1.4 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Name the main tasks of proteins, carbohydrates and fats in the body (one each from G1.4), with one example of each.
#### Answer
Proteins: building material (enzymes as catalysts, muscle fibre) and catalysts (enzymes); carbohydrates: energy supply/storage (glucose, glycogen); fats: energy storage (fatty tissue) and building material of membranes (phospholipids).
#### Solution
1. Proteins: the working and building substance of the body — from hair and muscle (building) to enzymes (biocatalysts; see E02).
2. Carbohydrates: fast energy (glucose) and storage carbohydrate (glycogen in liver and muscle).
3. Fats: compact energy reserve (fatty tissue) and membrane fabric (phospholipids, the bilayers of q11).

### chem-g01-q10 | points 3 | outcome G1.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Glycogen is a storage carbohydrate. (1) Where is it stored and what is its building block? (2) Explain its function when a person has not eaten for some hours. (3) Explain why its highly branched structure suits a storage molecule.
#### Answer
1. In the liver and in the muscles; it is a chain of glucose units (α-1,4 with α-1,6 branch links). 2. When blood glucose falls, glycogen is hydrolysed back to glucose (liver releases it to the blood, muscles use it on the spot): fast energy supply between meals. 3. The many branches place many chain ends within reach of the enzymes, so glucose can be clipped off at many points simultaneously — storage that can be emptied fast.
#### Solution
1. Glycogen is a polysaccharide of glucose, stored in the liver and the muscles.
2. Falling blood sugar leads to glycogen hydrolysis (glycogenolysis): the body's buffer of fast carbohydrate.
3. Branches multiply the number of non-reducing ends; enzymes work at all of them in parallel — the molecule delivers many glucose units per unit of time. Fitness of structure for function, as the syllabus asks.

### chem-g01-q11 | points 4 | outcome G1.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Cell membranes consist largely of phospholipids in a double layer. (1) Describe the structure of a phospholipid. (2) Draw (in words) the double layer and the orientation of the molecules. (3) Explain what this means for which substances can pass the membrane.
#### Answer
1. A phospholipid: glycerol esterified with two fatty acid chains (apolar tails) and a phosphate-containing (polar/charged) group as the head. 2. In the double layer the heads face the water phases (inside and outside of the cell) and the tails face each other, hiding in the middle — hydrophobic core between two hydrophilic surfaces. 3. Apolar/uncharged molecules (O₂, CO₂, small fatty molecules) cross this hydrophobic core; ions and strongly polar substances cannot and need channels or transports (see E2.4).
#### Solution
1. Compare a triglyceride, but with one of the fatty acids replaced by a phosphate-linked group: two hydrophobic tails + one hydrophilic head.
2. In water the molecules self-organise so the H-bonding heads are in the water and the tails avoid it: bilayer with the tails inward.
3. Consequence for transport: the core is a wall to anything that cannot live without its hydrogen bridges — ions, sugars — so the cell controls passage with proteins (channels/transporters); apolar small molecules diffuse through freely.

### chem-g01-q12 | points 4 | outcome G1.4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Describe the road from a gene on the DNA to a finished protein: the production of mRNA, the role of ribosomes, and the role of tRNA in the peptide construction.
#### Answer
The gene is copied to a messenger-RNA (mRNA) in the nucleus; the mRNA leaves to a ribosome, where the sequence is read in triplets (codons); each codon matches a tRNA with its complementary anticodon, and the tRNA carries the matching amino acid; the ribosome links the brought amino acids with peptide bonds into the protein chain.
#### Solution
1. Transcription: the DNA code of one gene is transcribed into mRNA (the "messenger" carrying the code out of the nucleus).
2. At the ribosomes (in the cytoplasm or on the endoplasmic reticulum) the mRNA is read in triplets.
3. Translation: each tRNA molecule presents one amino acid; its anticodon pairs complementarily with a codon on the mRNA; the ribosome catalyses the peptide bond between successive amino acids and lets the chain grow.
4. Result: the sequence of bases in the DNA fixes the sequence of amino acids in the protein — the genetic code (G1.4).

### chem-g01-q13 | points 3 | outcome G1.5 | difficulty easy
#### Question
What does "essential" mean in "essential amino acids"? Name two examples, and the class of the diet they must come from. What is the analogous statement for fatty acids?
#### Answer
Essential means the body cannot make them itself, so they must come from the diet; examples: valine, leucine (also lysine, isoleucine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan). They come from protein-rich food (meat, egg, soy). Analogously, essential fatty acids (e.g. linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid) are fatty acids the body cannot synthesise, so they must come from the diet (oils, nuts, fatty fish).
#### Solution
1. An essential nutrient is one the body needs daily but cannot produce from other nutrients: "essential" = must be eaten.
2. For proteins: nine essential amino acids for humans (valine, leucine, isoleucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan, histidine), supplied by protein-rich foods.
3. For fats: the polyunsaturated fatty acids linoleic acid (n-6) and α-linolenic acid (n-3) are essential; deficiency disturbs membranes and signalling.

### chem-g01-q14 | points 3 | outcome G1.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Why does a diet of rice and corn oil alone, sufficient in calories and protein, still risk deficiency diseases? Name two categories of essential substances from the G1 list that such a poor diet can lack, with one example each.
#### Answer
The diet lacks certain vitamins and essential amino acids/fatty acids; examples from the G1.5 list: essential amino acids such as lysine (lacking in maize protein) and essential fatty acids such as α-linolenic acid (n-3), plus vitamins and minerals the body cannot make either.
#### Solution
1. Calories and total protein are not enough: nutrition must supply the essential amino acids, essential fatty acids, vitamins and minerals that the body cannot synthesise.
2. Cereal proteins are poor in lysine (and maize also in tryptophan), so only rice/maize protein starves protein synthesis of those amino acids.
3. Corn oil supplies linoleic acid (n-6) but hardly α-linolenic acid (n-3), and the diet lacks vitamin C (scurvy), vitamin A (blindness) and several minerals: each is "essential" by the same logic.

### chem-g01-q15 | points 5 | outcome G1.2–G1.3 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A dipeptide is Ala–Gly: CH₃–CH(NH₂)–CO–NH–CH₂–COOH. (1) Give the equation for its formation from alanine and glycine. (2) Give the equation for its hydrolysis in digestion. (3) Explain how thousands of repetitions of step 1 form a protein at the ribosome, and what the ribosome reads to choose the next amino acid.
#### Answer
1. CH₃–CH(NH₂)–COOH + H₂N–CH₂–COOH → CH₃–CH(NH₂)–CO–NH–CH₂–COOH + H₂O. 2. Ala–Gly + H₂O → alanine + glycine (the reverse). 3. The ribosome repeats the condensation thousands of times, choosing each next amino acid by the mRNA codon triplet it reads, brought in by the matching tRNA.
#### Solution
1. The amine of glycine and the COOH of alanine condense: –COOH + H₂N– → –CO–NH– + H₂O − a peptide bond is born (condensation, see C01).
2. Digestion: the bond is hydrolysed back: –CO–NH– + H₂O → –COOH + H₂N–.
3. Protein synthesis: successive condensations extend the chain one residue at a time; the order of amino acids — the primary structure, see q02 — comes from the triplet sequence on the mRNA, which was transcribed from the DNA gene; tRNA delivers the right amino acid to each codon.
