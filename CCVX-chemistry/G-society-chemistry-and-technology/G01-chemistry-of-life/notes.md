## G1.1 Nutrients as the basis for self substances

- Digestion (*spijsvertering*) breaks the large molecules in food into their building blocks by hydrolysis, with enzymes as catalysts: proteins into amino acids, polysaccharides and disaccharides into monosaccharides, fats into glycerol and fatty acids. The building blocks pass into the blood, which transports them to the cells. *(CvTE L1.4, L1.1)*
- The cells use the building blocks in two ways: as raw material for self substances (*lichaamseigen stoffen*), the body's own substances, and as fuel that is burned for energy (G1.3). *(CvTE L1.4)*
- Examples: amino acids from food protein are joined into the body's own proteins, such as enzymes, keratin in hair and haemoglobin in blood, in the order coded by the body's own DNA (G1.4); glucose is stored as glycogen; glycerol and fatty acids are joined again into the body's own fats and phospholipids. *(CvTE L1.4, L1.6)*
- The body can also convert some building blocks into others, for example make the non-essential amino acids itself, but not the essential ones (G1.5). *(CvTE L1.4)*
- **Trap:** the body doesn't take up a food protein and use it as it is. It takes up the amino acids and builds new proteins with its own sequence.

## G1.2 Structure of proteins, carbohydrates, fats and nucleic acids

### Proteins

- An amino acid (*aminozuur*) has an amino group and a carboxyl group bonded to the same carbon atom, H₂N–CHR–COOH, with a side chain R that differs per amino acid: R = H in glycine, H₂N–CH₂–COOH, and R = CH₃ in alanine, H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH. Proteins are built from 20 kinds of amino acid; the Binas table of amino acids gives their structures and abbreviations. *(CvTE L1.3, M1.13)*
- Amino acids join by condensation: the COOH group of one and the NH₂ group of the next form a peptide bond (*peptidebinding*), –CO–NH–, and release H₂O. From glycine and alanine: H₂N–CH₂–COOH + H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH → H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–COOH + H₂O, or in molecular formulas C₂H₅NO₂ + C₃H₇NO₂ → C₅H₁₀N₂O₃ + H₂O. Many amino acids joined this way form a polypeptide; a protein consists of one or more long polypeptide chains. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.16, R2.1)*
- **Trap:** glycine and alanine can form two different dipeptides: Gly-Ala (above, with the free NH₂ group on the glycine unit) and Ala-Gly. A peptide is written from the end with the free NH₂ group to the end with the free COOH group.
- Primary structure (*primaire structuur*): the sequence of the amino acids in the chain. It's held together by the peptide bonds, which are atomic bonds (covalent bonds). *(CvTE L1.3, M3.1)*
- Secondary structure (*secundaire structuur*): parts of the chain coil into an α-helix (*alfahelix*) or lie side by side in a β-sheet (*bètaplaat*). Both are held by hydrogen bridges (hydrogen bonds, *waterstofbruggen*) between the C=O of one peptide group and the N–H of another peptide group in the backbone: in a helix between successive turns, in a sheet between neighbouring strands. A hydrogen bridge is drawn as a dotted line between the O and the H. See also [B04](../../B-substances-and-materials/B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md). *(CvTE L1.3, M3.1)*
- Tertiary structure (*tertiaire structuur*): the folding of the whole chain into its 3D shape, held by bonds between side chains: *(CvTE L1.3, M3.1, M3.6)*
    - hydrogen bridges, for example between OH groups;
    - ion bonds between –COO⁻ and –NH₃⁺ groups of acidic and basic side chains;
    - S–S bridges (*zwavelbruggen*) between two cysteine units, which are atomic bonds;
    - hydrophobic contacts: apolar side chains gather inside the protein, away from the water, held together by Van der Waals bonds (molecular bonds).
- Heating, or a pH change that alters the charges on the side chains, breaks hydrogen bridges and ion bonds. The protein loses its secondary and tertiary structure (denaturation, *denaturatie*), but the peptide bonds stay intact. That's why enzymes have a temperature optimum and a pH optimum; see [E02](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md). *(CvTE L2.2)*
- **Trap:** the hydrogen bridges of the secondary structure are between C=O and N–H groups of the backbone; bonds between side chains belong to the tertiary structure. An S–S bridge is an atomic bond, not a hydrogen bridge.

### Carbohydrates

- Monosaccharides (*monosachariden*): glucose, fructose and galactose are isomers with the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆. In the open-chain form they have one C=O group and several OH groups; in water they're mostly rings (glucose: a ring of five C atoms and one O atom). Ribose, C₅H₁₀O₅, and deoxyribose, C₅H₁₀O₄, are monosaccharides with five C atoms (see nucleic acids below). *(CvTE L1.3)*
- Disaccharides (*disachariden*): two monosaccharide units joined by condensation through an O bridge (C–O–C). Sucrose (*sacharose*: glucose and fructose), maltose (two glucose units) and lactose (glucose and galactose) all have the formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁: 2 C₆H₁₂O₆ → C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- Polysaccharides (*polysachariden*): long chains of monosaccharide units, usually glucose. Starch (*zetmeel*), a mixture of unbranched amylose and branched amylopectin, and glycogen (*glycogeen*), which is even more branched, are built from α-glucose. Cellulose is built from β-glucose; its unbranched chains lie side by side, held by many hydrogen bridges, in strong fibres. Each unit that joins the chain releases one H₂O, so a polysaccharide of glucose has the formula (C₆H₁₀O₅)<sub>n</sub>, ignoring the chain ends. *(CvTE L1.3, R2.1)*
- **Trap:** sucrose, maltose and lactose have the same molecular formula, so name the monosaccharides formed when you describe their hydrolysis. Humans can't digest cellulose although it's made of glucose: their enzymes don't fit the β-links.

### Fats

- Fats and oils consist mainly of triglycerides (*triglyceriden*): esters (CCVX: alkoxy alkanoates) of glycerol (*glycerol*, propane-1,2,3-triol, CH₂OH–CHOH–CH₂OH) with three fatty acids (*vetzuren*). A fatty acid is a carbon acid with a long unbranched carbon chain, usually with an even number of C atoms, often 16 or 18. *(CvTE L1.3, M1.13)*
- Glycerol and stearic acid (octadecanoic acid) form glyceryl tristearate by condensation, with three ester groups and three H₂O: C₃H₈O₃ + 3 C₁₇H₃₅COOH → C₅₇H₁₁₀O₆ + 3 H₂O. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.16)*
- Saturated fatty acids (*verzadigde vetzuren*) have only C–C single bonds in the chain, C<sub>n</sub>H<sub>2n+1</sub>COOH: palmitic acid C₁₅H₃₁COOH and stearic acid C₁₇H₃₅COOH. Unsaturated fatty acids (*onverzadigde vetzuren*) have one or more C=C double bonds, each costing two H atoms: oleic acid C₁₇H₃₃COOH (one C=C), linoleic acid C₁₇H₃₁COOH (two) and α-linolenic acid C₁₇H₂₉COOH (three). *(CvTE L1.3)*
- The C=C bonds in natural fatty acids are mostly cis, which puts a bend in the chain. Such molecules pack less closely, so the Van der Waals bonds between them are weaker and the melting point is lower: oils, liquid at room temperature, contain more unsaturated fatty acids than fats, which are solid. See [B04](../../B-substances-and-materials/B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md). *(CvTE L1.3, M3.3)*
- **Trap:** "unsaturated" refers to C=C bonds in the fatty-acid chains, not to the C=O of the ester groups. A triglyceride isn't an acid: its COOH groups have become ester groups.

### Nucleic acids

- DNA and RNA are long chains of nucleotides (*nucleotiden*). A nucleotide consists of a sugar, a phosphate group (derived from phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄) and a base. Sugar and phosphate alternate in the backbone of the chain, and a base is attached to each sugar. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- DNA: the sugar is deoxyribose (*desoxyribose*), the bases are adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) and thymine (T). Two strands run in opposite directions and wind around each other in a double helix. The bases pair A–T and C–G, held by hydrogen bridges (two between A and T, three between C and G), so the strands are complementary: opposite ATTGC lies TAACG. *(CvTE L1.3, M3.1)*
- RNA: the sugar is ribose, which has an OH group where deoxyribose has only an H atom (on the second C atom), so one O atom more. The bases are A, C, G and uracil (U) instead of T, and RNA is a single strand. Protein synthesis uses mRNA, tRNA and the RNA in ribosomes (G1.4). *(CvTE L1.3)*
- At the pH in a cell the phosphate groups have given off H⁺, so the backbone is negatively charged. This acidity gives nucleic acids (*nucleïnezuren*) their name.
- **Trap:** U replaces T only in RNA. When RNA forms on DNA, an A in the DNA pairs with a U in the RNA.

## G1.3 Breakdown in the body: hydrolysis, urea and combustion

- Hydrolysis (*hydrolyse*) is the reverse of condensation: water splits a peptide bond, an ester group or the O bridge between sugar units. In the body enzymes catalyse it; outside the body an acid or a base can do it. Writing these reactions in structural formulas is practised in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md). *(CvTE R1.3, R1.16)*

### Proteins

- Proteases such as pepsin in the stomach and trypsin in the small intestine hydrolyse proteins to amino acids, one H₂O per peptide bond: H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH(CH₃)–COOH + H₂O → H₂N–CH₂–COOH + H₂N–CH(CH₃)–COOH. *(CvTE R1.16, L1.4)*
- Urea (*ureum*): amino acids that aren't used for building are broken down further. The liver removes their amino groups; the ammonia formed is toxic, so the liver turns it into urea, CO(NH₂)₂, which the kidneys excrete in urine. The real route is a cycle of enzyme reactions; its net summary is 2 NH₃ + CO₂ → CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O. Urea is 46.65% nitrogen by mass.
- Combustion (*verbranding*): the rest of the amino acid is burned to CO₂ and H₂O for energy, or converted into glucose or fat. As a net summary for alanine, with the nitrogen ending up in urea: 2 C₃H₇NO₂ + 6 O₂ → CO(NH₂)₂ + 5 CO₂ + 5 H₂O. *(CvTE R1.2)*
- **Trap:** in the body the nitrogen of amino acids ends up in urea, not as N₂ or NOₓ as it would in a flame.

### Carbohydrates

- Amylase in saliva and from the pancreas hydrolyses starch to maltose, and other enzymes hydrolyse the disaccharides to monosaccharides. For sucrose: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O → 2 C₆H₁₂O₆ (one glucose and one fructose). *(CvTE R1.3, L1.4)*
- Glucose is burned in the cells (cell respiration, *verbranding*): C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O. In the body this happens in many enzyme-catalysed steps at 37 °C, but the net equation is the same as for burning glucose, and so is the heat of reaction ΔE, because ΔE depends only on the starting substances and the end products. Breathing and gas exchange (*ademhaling, gaswisseling*) bring in the O₂ and remove the CO₂. *(CvTE R1.2, L1.1)*

### Fats

- Bile from the liver acts as an emulsifier (*emulgator*): it breaks fat into small droplets, which increases the surface area of reacting substances (*verdelingsgraad*). The enzyme lipase then hydrolyses the triglycerides to glycerol and fatty acids: C₅₇H₁₁₀O₆ + 3 H₂O → C₃H₈O₃ + 3 C₁₇H₃₅COOH. *(CvTE R1.16, M2.3, R3.1)*
- Glycerol and fatty acids are burned for energy, stearic acid for example: C₁₇H₃₅COOH + 26 O₂ → 18 CO₂ + 18 H₂O. *(CvTE R1.2)*
- Fat gives about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate or protein: about 37 kJ per gram for fat against about 17 kJ per gram for carbohydrate and protein, the values used on EU food labels. Fats contain much less oxygen (glucose is 53.29% O by mass, glyceryl tristearate 10.77%): they're less oxidised already, so more O₂ reacts per gram, 2.7 times as much for stearic acid as for glucose.
- **Trap:** hydrolysis only splits large molecules into building blocks; nearly all the energy comes from the combustion that follows.

## G1.4 Functions in the body and protein synthesis

### Proteins

- Building material (*bouwstof*): structural proteins such as keratin in hair and nails, collagen in skin, tendons and bone, and the proteins of muscles. *(CvTE L1.5)*
- Enzymes (*enzymen*): proteins that act as catalysts (biocatalysts). The substrate binds at the active position (= active site, *actieve plaats*), whose shape and functional groups make each enzyme specific; see [E02](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md). Other proteins transport substances (haemoglobin carries O₂ in the blood) or act as hormones (insulin). *(CvTE L1.5, L2.1, L2.2)*

### Carbohydrates

- Glucose in the blood is a direct fuel for the cells. Energy is stored (*energieopslag*) as glycogen in the liver and muscles of humans and animals, and as starch in plants. *(CvTE L1.5)*
- Glycogen is highly branched, so it has many chain ends where enzymes can add or remove glucose units quickly. The liver uses its glycogen to keep the glucose level of the blood up; muscles use their own glycogen themselves.
- Cellulose isn't a store but building material, in the cell walls of plants.
- **Trap:** glycogen is the storage form in animals, starch in plants; both are polymers of α-glucose.

### Fats

- Energy storage: fat tissue holds a lot of energy per gram (G1.3), and fat is stored without water, unlike glycogen. *(CvTE L1.5)*
- Building material in membranes (*membranen*): phospholipids (*fosfolipiden*) resemble triglycerides, but the glycerol carries two fatty acids and a phosphate group, usually with a small polar group attached to it. The phosphate end is a charged, hydrophilic head; the two fatty-acid chains are hydrophobic tails. *(CvTE L1.5, L2.3, M3.6)*
- In water, phospholipids form a bilayer (*dubbellaag*): the heads face the water on both sides and the tails point inwards, towards each other. This bilayer is the basis of the cell membrane (*celmembraan*); transport of substances through membranes is in [E02](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md). *(CvTE L2.3, L1.1)*
- **Trap:** a phospholipid has two fatty-acid tails, not three; the third position of glycerol carries the phosphate group.

### Nucleic acids

- DNA is the information carrier (*informatiedrager*): its base sequence holds the genetic information, which is read with the genetic code (*genetische code*, below). A gene (*gen*) is a section of DNA that codes for one protein. *(CvTE L1.5, L1.6, L1.1)*
- Protein synthesis (*eiwitsynthese*) takes two steps: *(CvTE L1.6)*
    - Transcription (*transcriptie*): in the nucleus, one DNA strand of the gene is the template (*matrijs*) on which mRNA (messenger RNA) is built. RNA nucleotides pair with the DNA bases: A in DNA with U in RNA, T with A, C with G and G with C. The mRNA then moves to the ribosomes (*ribosomen*) in the cytoplasm.
    - Translation (*translatie*): a ribosome reads the mRNA in codons (*codons*) of three bases. Each tRNA molecule carries one particular amino acid and has an anticodon of three bases that pairs with a codon. The ribosome joins the amino acids brought by successive tRNAs with peptide bonds, in the order of the codons.
- Four bases give 4³ = 64 codons for 20 amino acids and stop signals, so most amino acids have more than one codon. AUG codes for methionine and is the start codon; UAA, UAG and UGA are stop codons. The Binas table of the genetic code gives the amino acid for each mRNA codon. *(CvTE L1.6)*
- Example: the DNA template strand TAC CCG CGA ATT gives the mRNA AUG GGC GCU UAA, which codes for Met–Gly–Ala and then stops. The tRNA that brings glycine to the codon GGC has the anticodon CCG. *(CvTE L1.6)*
- **Trap:** the genetic-code table lists mRNA codons, so it contains U, not T: convert a DNA sequence into mRNA first. The codon is on the mRNA, the anticodon on the tRNA.

## G1.5 Essential amino acids and fatty acids

- Essential amino acids (*essentiële aminozuren*) are amino acids that the body can't make, or not enough of, so the diet must supply them. For adults there are nine: histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine. Older books count eight, leaving out histidine. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- Essential fatty acids (*essentiële vetzuren*) are linoleic acid, C₁₇H₃₁COOH (octadeca-9,12-dienoic acid, an omega-6 fatty acid), and α-linolenic acid, C₁₇H₂₉COOH (octadeca-9,12,15-trienoic acid, an omega-3 fatty acid). The body can't make a C=C bond beyond the ninth C atom, counting from the carbon of the COOH group, so these must come from food such as vegetable oils, nuts and seeds. Saturated fatty acids and oleic acid the body can make itself.
- **Trap:** "essential" means that the body can't make the substance, not that it's more important than other nutrients.
