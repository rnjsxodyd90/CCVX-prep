# G3 — Energy and industry: practice

Eight original questions on the G3 learning outcomes, in CCVX style.

### chem-g03-q01 | points 3 | outcome G3.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Explain what happens in a coal gasification plant, and why it can be cleaner and more efficient than a traditional coal-fired power station. Give the key reaction.
#### Answer
The coal is not burned in air but partially oxidised with steam/limited oxygen to syngas: C(s) + H₂O(g) → CO(g) + H₂(g) (plus C + ½ O₂ → CO). Syngas can be cleaned (desulfurised) before combustion and then drives a gas turbine plus a steam cycle (IGCC, combined cycle), so it reaches higher efficiency and lower sulfur/particulate emissions than a classic boiler.
#### Solution
1. Gasification converts a dirty solid fuel into a gaseous fuel: the carbon becomes CO and H₂ (also CH₄ and CO₂ in practice).
2. A gas can be scrubbed cleanly (sulfur → H₂S removed and sold as sulfur), while flue gas of a coal boiler holds all the ash and sulfur of the coal.
3. IGCC uses the syngas in a combined cycle (gas turbine + steam turbine): more electricity per tonne of coal than a single steam cycle, at lower emissions per kWh — hence its place in G3.1 comparisons.

### chem-g03-q02 | points 4 | outcome G3.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Compare a natural-gas-fired power station with a coal-fired one on two axes: (1) CO₂ per joule of fuel, (2) fuel handling and impurities. Assume methane vs carbon from chem-f03-q05.
#### Answer
1. Methane emits per joule about 1.12·10⁻⁶ mol CO₂, coal about 2.6·10⁻⁶ mol — gas ≈ 2.3 times cleaner per joule. 2. Natural gas comes by pipeline, desulfurised and dust-free; coal must be mined, shipped, ground and deashed, and it carries sulfur (SO₂) and minerals (fine particles, mercury traces): more emissions per service and far more logistics.
#### Solution
1. C/H ratio decides the CO₂ per joule (worked in chem-f03-q05): methane wins because half of its energy comes from the H part → water.
2. Coal's impurities (S, minerals, metals) turn into SO₂, particulates and stack emissions that demand scrubbers; gas is already clean at the plant gate.
3. Conclusion of the comparison: per service (kWh), natural gas is the cleaner fossil — with the caveat that methane leakages upstream (on drilling sites and in pipes) bite into this advantage, since CH₄ is itself a strong greenhouse gas.

### chem-g03-q03 | points 3 | outcome G3.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A delivery company doubts between hydrogen trucks and battery trucks for 800 km daily routes. Score the two energy sources on (1) energy per kg of storage, (2) refuelling, (3) the chain from production to wheel, and conclude for this route type.
#### Answer
1. H₂: about 1.42·10⁵ J per gram of hydrogen — far better per kg of storage than batteries (which carry their own cell mass), so range weight stays low. 2. Refuelling: hydrogen in minutes vs battery charging in hours for this size — H₂ keeps trucks rolling. 3. Chain: hydrogen needs production from gas or electrolysis, compression and a fuel cell (more conversions, more loss); the battery drives the motor almost straight from the grid (higher end-to-end efficiency). Conclusion: for long daily routes hydrogen is defensible (range + refuelling time beat efficiency losses); for city vans, batteries (see chem-f03-q10).
#### Solution
1. From chem-c06-q10: H₂ holds 1.42·10⁵ J g⁻¹, so kg-per-kWh stays low even with high-pressure tanks; batteries are mass-intensive per kWh.
2. Time is money in logistics: a 15-minute stop is the equivalent of diesel behaviour; charging big battery packs needs idle time.
3. Honest account: per km, H₂ uses more primary energy than batteries through electrolysis → compression → fuel cell; the choice rests on duty cycle — that is the kind of judgment G3.2 trains.

### chem-g03-q04 | points 3 | outcome G3.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Dutch petrol is blended with bioethanol made from sugar/starch crops ("E10"). Give one argument for this blending in sustainability terms, one counter-argument on the farming side, and one of its consequences for car engines.
#### Answer
For: bioethanol's carbon is fast-cycle: the CO₂ it returns on burning was circumstantially captured from the air by the crops, so blending lowers the fossil fraction per kilometre. Counter: first-generation ethanol uses food-grade land and fertiliser, competing with food and nature (indirect land-use change can undo the gain). Consequence: ethanol attracts water and is mildly corrosive, so engines must be compatible (older cars need additives/checks).
#### Solution
1. Short-cycle carbon argument (see chem-c03-q05): the carbon in C₂H₅OH was CO₂ a year ago.
2. Farming: fertiliser (energy-intensive), irrigation and land competition feed the E-factor of the fuel and can cancel the climate gain when forests/grassland are converted.
3. Chemical/engine: ethanol is polar and hygroscopic, attacking some rubbers and metals of older fuel systems (the reason blends are capped at E10) — greet the engineering side as well as the climate side.

### chem-g03-q05 | points 3 | outcome G3.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A dairy farm chooses between a manure digester (biogas feeding a small CHP setup) and simple manure spreading on the field. Score both on (1) energy, (2) emissions, (3) nutrients, and give the better green choice.
#### Answer
Digester: wins on energy (the biogas becomes electricity and heat for the farm) and on emissions (captured methane is burned instead of escaping; the remaining digestate smells less), while holding onto most nutrients (the digestate still fertilises); spreading loses the energy and leaks more methane at spreading time. Green choice: the digester, provided it is airtight and the gas is used, not vented.
#### Solution
1. Biogas = CH₄ + CO₂ (chem-f03-q02): the energy that would otherwise warm the atmosphere as methane goes instead into the barn's kettle and the diesel pump's replacement.
2. Methane is a strong greenhouse gas: even leaked shares matter — the digester is only a win if the plant is tight (a fugitive CH₄ leak undoes it).
3. The nutrients (N, P, K) survive digestion and return to the field as digestate — nothing is lost agronomically. The comparison of energy sources and processes is the core of G3.2.

### chem-g03-q06 | points 3 | outcome G3.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A shipping company evaluates biodiesel from palm oil. Give one technical argument for it, one environmental counter-argument, and one condition under which the environmental balance could still be favourable.
#### Answer
Technical: biodiesel (FAME) is close to diesel chemically, so ships can use existing engines/fuel systems with little adaptation. Environmental counter: palm plantations often replace tropical forest, so the land-use change releases more CO₂ than the fuel saves, and biodiversity shrinks — the numbered worst case of biofuels. Condition: certified oil from existing (not newly cleared) plantations or from waste streams such as used cooking oil (used cooking oil), where no extra land is converted — then the balance is genuinely better.
#### Solution
1. FAME biodiesel retrofits engines: a practical argument for transport fleets (see chem-f02-q05).
2. The climate judgment includes the land: clearing peat forest or rainforest to plant palm can emit decades' worth of fuel's CO₂ at once.
3. Certified waste-derived or genuinely additional crops avoid indirect land-use change — the difference between paper-green and real-green biofuel.

### chem-g03-q07 | points 4 | outcome G3.1 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A regional planner compares two routes for wind-powered heat for houses: (A) wind electricity → resistance heating (direct), and (B) wind electricity → electrolysis → store H₂ → combined heat and power (fuel cell) in winter. (1) Score both on total efficiency (fewer conversions) and on storage. (2) Which route would you defend for a windy island with dark calm weeks, and why? Use losses: resistance 95%, electrolysis + storage + CHP together 35%.
#### Answer
1. Route A: almost every joule of wind power ends as heat in the room (95%); but the windless dark week leaves the house cold, because electricity stores poorly in a house. Route B: only ~35% of the joule returns as useful heat and power — the rest is lost — but the H₂ stock can bridge calm weeks. 2. Defence of B for the island: a seasonal store is the missing service of A; for the calm weeks the lower efficiency is the price of heat security (B can be supplemented by A whenever wind blows, so the losses hit only the stored part).
#### Solution
1. Every conversion pays tax: resistance heat ≈ direct, electrolysis alone costs ~30%, compression/storage more, fuel cell again — the product of efficiencies is about 35%.
2. Against that stands the value of stored winter energy: batteries and heat buffers are days-deep at best; H₂ in salt caverns is months-deep.
3. Judgment: use wind directly as heat/electricity (A) as first resort and electrolyse only the surplus into storage (B): you keep 95% when you can and 35% when you must — the architecture many real plans choose.

### chem-g03-q08 | points 3 | outcome G3.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A small industrial estate needs process heat and chooses between a natural-gas boiler, a biomass boiler (wood pellets) and solar collectors with a gas backup. Justify a choice and show the criteria you weigh (come with three).
#### Answer
Criteria: emissions per kWh of heat (CO₂, particulates), security of supply (winter sun fails), fuel logistics (transport/storage/delivery). Justified choice example: natural-gas boiler with a solar pre-heat and a biomass pilot — gas for reliability and low particulate emissions, solar for uncostly kWhs in summer, biomass pilot to prepare for a real phase-out; a pure pellet boiler wins on paper CO₂ but loses on particulates and deliveries in a dense estate.
#### Solution
1. Explicit criteria first: that is what G3 asks ("justify the choice").
2. Weigh them per option:
    - gas: low SO₂/particulates, pipelines, but fossil;
    - pellets: fast-cycle CO₂, but particulate matter around the estate and many truck deliveries (fuel delivery and storage factors);
    - solar: free joules but intermittent — a backup is required anyway.
3. A defensible hybrid answers each criterion at its best option; the justification of the chosen mix matters more than the winning technology.
