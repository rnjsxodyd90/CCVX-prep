# G5 — Business processes: practice

Six original questions on the G5 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. G5 asks you to analyse an example from the Dutch chemical industry and to describe its contribution to local and global quality of life.

### chem-g05-q01 | points 3 | outcome G5 | difficulty easy
#### Question
DSM (Dutch State Mines) now produces the vitamin B2 (riboflavin) by fermentation instead of by a five-step chemical synthesis. Analyse the contribution of that change to the quality of life on two levels: globally (market level) and locally (production site level).
#### Answer
Globally: fermentation from renewable sugar with a natural catalyst (yeast/fungi) means less hazardous reagent flow and lower CO₂ for the world's vitamins supply; affordable micronutrient at scale (health effect, global quality of life). Locally: at the site there is less solvent emissions and less risky storage (chlorinated intermediates from synthesis replaced by a fermentor): safer, cleaner environment for the city/nature nearby (local quality of life).
#### Solution
1. Global quality of life = what the product achieves: vitamins in food for everyone, affordable supply, less fossil dependence.
2. Local quality of life = what the site means for its neighbours: fewer hazardous substances in the fence (air/soil/water effects), less nuisance (smell, traffic), and modern green jobs.
3. G5 structure: separate the levels clearly; a good answer names both.

### chem-g05-q02 | points 3 | outcome G5 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Dow Benelux in Terneuzen exports its cracking by-product hydrogen to the neighbouring Yara ammonia plant by pipeline. Describe this business process in terms of sustainability and living environment.
#### Answer
The hydrogen Mizushima-like by-product of Dow's cracking would otherwise be flared or delivered at high logistics cost; the pipeline transfers it into Yara's ammonia feedstock — a substance cycle across companies (industrial symbiosis): fewer deliveries (energy and traffic), less waste (less flaring = less CO₂), and the dependency of neighbours on each other keeps the region's industry economically strong.
#### Solution
1. Industrial symbiosis: one plant's by-product is the other's feedstock — a substance-cycle at park level (F4/F5 idea in regional business terms).
2. Sustainability: less flaring, less transport = lower emissions.
3. Living environment: shared infrastructure means jobs, but also a common-risk responsibility (the region's quality of life depends on both plants' safety and emissions standards).
4. Business processes: judge the whole chain buyers/suppliers in the region, not a single plant.

### chem-g05-q03 | points 4 | outcome G5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Analyse lamb-weston/Aviko or AVEBE (potato processing, starch producer) as a Dutch example: contribution to quality of life locally and globally, and one negative environmental point and its mitigation.
#### Answer
Contribution: (G) potato starch goes to world food/papers industries (food additives, adhesives and biodegradable materials); (L) employment in the rural provinces and crop contracts that give thousands of farmers a stable buyer; environmental point: process water with starch/protein residues and odour (and soil transport by tractor traffic), mitigated by wastewater purification (aerated lagoons/anaerobic treatment taking the COD) and by on-site biogas from those washwaters — closing part of the loop.
#### Solution
1. Global: the utilisation of a local crop as a world-class resource, and the value addition of food that would otherwise rot (protein isolation as a pig-feed or human-food ingredient).
2. Local: jobs in the region and the stability of the farm contracts; a plant this size shapes the regional economy.
3. Environmental reality: the odour/effluent question is managed by purification trains — the G5 answer must keep the balance and may not become advertising text.

### chem-g05-q04 | points 4 | outcome G5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Judge this claim, with the structure of G5: "The Dutch chemical industry is a burden on the quality of life in the Netherlands." Argue for nuance using two points for and two against, with the verb structure: claim → argument → counter-argument → conclusion.
#### Answer
For (burden): industry's very existence brings emissions, accident risk (Geleen, Moerdijk, Delfzijl-Botlek zones), heavy truck/train flows and soil history (groundwater pollution from older land) — measurable pressure on the local quality of life. Against (contribution): it is a large direct and indirect employer; it makes the materials that the society's quality of life rests on (packaging, clean water chemicals, insulation, medicines); its current sustainability investments (green hydrogen in the port, CCUS projects, plastics recycling at Moerdijk) make it the place where many Dutch climate goals are actually achievable. Conclusion: the industry is at the same time a major pressure (locally, on air/soil/risk) and an indispensable contribution (economically and for the materials of life): "burden" alone doesn't fit — the G5 evaluation weighs both by facts at the site level, not by sentiment.
#### Solution
1. For: enumerate the pressure points: emissions, accidents, transport, soil.
2. Against: enumerate contribution: employment, materials, solutions infrastructure for the climate transition.
3. Nuance demands evidence density per side, locality (one is not "the Dutch industry" as one — every site weighs differently) and a weighing line at the end: this is the format G5 answers take.

### chem-g05-q05 | points 4 | outcome G5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Regional case: the port of Rotterdam's Botlek-vondelingenplaat cluster runs refineries, a chlorine chain, and fast-growing biobased and recycling plants. Describe this business system along two dictions: substance flows and quality-of-life issues, and name the best and the worst part of its legacy.
#### Answer
Substance flows: crude oil and gas enter by ship/pipeline, fuel/refinery products, chlorine and derivatives (PVC precursors), increasingly biobased oils and recycled plastics leave or continue along the chain (a loop between refining and recycling). Quality of life: employment, port access and infrastructure for green exports vs historic soil and groundwater contamination (named polluted industrial zones), the heavy transport load and air quality. Best: existing infrastructure can host new green processes (pipelines used by CCUS and green H₂ plans; methanol and hydrogen bunkering). Worst: the soil legacy and the sheer scale of emissions make the site a decades-long obligation.
#### Solution
1. Substance flows: petroleum refining → gas/liquid fuels; chlorine–VCM/PVC-like chain; newer biobased and circular flows — the same ground.
2. Quality of life: regional employment & economic anchor vs congestion, air quality and the sobering cost of legacy soil.
3. The legacy of industry is both technical (can carry green tech) and environmental (historic pollution) — a G5 analysis names the two without suppression.

### chem-g05-q06 | points 4 | outcome G5 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A start-up next to a potato-starch plant plans to produce PHA (a biodegradable plastic) from the plant's wastewater sugars. Judge this plan with a full G5 analysis: contribution to quality of life, business process, effects on the living environment, and the two main risks the entrepreneurs should manage.
#### Answer
Contribution: a drop-in biodegradable material made from a waste stream (displaces fossil plastics at the end of their life more gently; keeps material value in the region). Business process: fits industrial symbiosis — the starch plant's effluent becomes feedstock (substance cycle at site level), sharing tanks, labs and permits. Living environment: fewer wastewater pollutants leave the site, and the new plant adds green-tech jobs. Risks: (1) the wastewater stream's composition varies seasonally (feedstock security), and (2) PHA has to compete technically and in price with old polymers (market risk); the company should manage both with a trial plant against its target stream and a volume/quality specification before full investment.
#### Solution
1. Contribution: environmental (less waste, biodegradable product) plus social (jobs, innovation in the region).
2. Business logic: symbiosis gives cheap feedstock with shared permits — but the same coupling is a vulnerability.
3. Risks: variability of feedstock and market acceptance. Every G5 judgment ends with what should be done about the weakest link — that concluding step is exam skills.
