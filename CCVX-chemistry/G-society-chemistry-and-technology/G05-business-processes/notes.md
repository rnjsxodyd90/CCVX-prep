## Sustainability and quality of life

- Sustainable development (*duurzame ontwikkeling*) lets people today provide for what they need without making it harder for later generations to provide for themselves; this idea was made widely known by the Brundtland report, 1987. It's often split into people, planet and profit: social, environmental and economic effects. More on sustainability in [E03](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E03-sustainability/notes.md) and [F05](../../F-industrial-chemical-processes/F05-sustainable-production-processes/notes.md). *(SLO G5; CvTE T6.3)*
- Quality of life (*leefkwaliteit*): health, safety, a clean living environment and prosperity (jobs, income, useful products). A business process adds to it through its products and jobs, and can harm it through emissions, risks and the use of resources. *(SLO G5; CvTE T6.3, T6.4)*
- Local effects are felt near the plant: jobs, traffic, noise, smell, emissions and risk. Global effects are felt everywhere: CO₂ and climate change, the depletion of raw materials, and products that are used worldwide.
- **Trap:** a process can score well locally and badly globally, or the other way round: moving production abroad removes the local emissions but not the global ones.

## Processes: analysing a business process

- Go through: *(SLO G5; CvTE T1.2, T5.1)*
    1. the raw materials and where they come from (finite or renewable, local or imported);
    2. the process steps and reactions, preferably in a block diagram ([F01](../../F-industrial-chemical-processes/F01-industrial-processes/notes.md));
    3. the energy: how much, in which form (heat, electricity) and from which source;
    4. emissions, waste and by-products, and what happens to them;
    5. the products and what they're used for, which is their benefit to society;
    6. the local and global effects on the living environment and on quality of life.
- Much of the Dutch chemical industry is in clusters such as the Rotterdam port area and Chemelot in Geleen. The plants there exchange raw materials, products, steam and heat, often by pipeline, which saves transport and energy.

## Processes: chlor-alkali electrolysis as an example

- Raw materials: salt (NaCl), which in the Netherlands comes from deep underground salt layers: water is pumped down to dissolve it and the brine (*pekel*) is pumped up. Also water, and a lot of electricity. *(SLO G5)*
- Process: electrolysis of purified brine in membrane cells. At the positive electrode chloride is oxidised: 2 Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2 e⁻. At the negative electrode water is reduced: 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻ → H₂ + 2 OH⁻. A membrane lets Na⁺ ions through but keeps Cl₂ and OH⁻ apart, so caustic soda (*natronloog*) forms on the negative side. Overall: 2 NaCl + 2 H₂O → Cl₂ + H₂ + 2 NaOH. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.13)*
- With the Binas table of standard electrode potentials, the minimum voltage is 1.36 − (−0.83) = 2.19 V; in practice it's higher. *(CvTE R1.10)*
- **Trap:** the table predicts O₂ at the positive electrode (1.23 V is lower than 1.36 V), but in concentrated chloride solution Cl₂ forms, because in practice forming O₂ takes a much higher voltage than the table suggests.
- The products come in a fixed mole ratio, Cl₂ : H₂ : NaOH = 1 : 1 : 2: per tonne of Cl₂ the process gives 1.128 t NaOH and 28.43 kg H₂, and uses 1.649 t NaCl. The company has to sell chlorine and caustic soda in that ratio. *(CvTE R&A1.1)*
- Products and their uses: chlorine for PVC, many other chemicals and disinfection; caustic soda for soap, paper, aluminium and many neutralisations; hydrogen as a fuel or raw material.
- Living environment: chlorine is very toxic, so it's best made close to where it's used and stored in small amounts ([G02](../G02-environmental-impact-assessment/notes.md) covers transport and emergencies). The older mercury-cell process caused mercury emissions and has been phased out in the EU in favour of membrane cells. Because of the high electricity use, the CO₂ footprint depends on how the electricity is generated.
- Global contribution: PVC pipes, disinfected water and medicines made with chlorine chemistry benefit people everywhere.

## Effects on the living environment

- Effects on the living environment (*invloed op leefmilieu*): air, water and soil quality around the plant, noise, smell, light, traffic, the risk of accidents for the neighbourhood, and the landscape. How these qualities are guaranteed with limits, permits and monitoring is in [G04](../G04-environmental-requirements/notes.md). *(SLO G5; CvTE T4.1)*
- Weigh them against the benefits: jobs, useful products, and contributions such as residual heat supplied to homes.
- **Trap:** benefits and burdens often fall on different people: the products are used far away, while the noise, emissions and risk stay with the neighbours.

## Biofuels of three generations

- First generation: from food crops: sugar and starch for bioethanol, vegetable oil for biodiesel. Using food crops for fuel competes with food, which can push up food prices, and can lead to land being cleared for crops. *(SLO G5)*
- Second generation: from non-food biomass such as straw, wood, residues and waste fat. There's no direct competition with food, but breaking down cellulose is harder and more expensive.
- Third generation: from algae, which grow fast, can contain a lot of oil and need no farmland; production is still expensive and uses a lot of energy.
- The processes themselves are in [G03](../G03-energy-and-industry/notes.md) and [F02](../../F-industrial-chemical-processes/F02-green-chemistry/notes.md).
