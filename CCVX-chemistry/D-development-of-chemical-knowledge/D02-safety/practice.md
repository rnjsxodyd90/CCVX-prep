# D2 — Safety: practice

Six original questions on the real D2 outcome (analysing and purifying substances in experiments while handling substances, materials and equipment safely), in CCVX style. Note: the CCVX PDF mistakenly prints C10's text under D2; the outcome used here is the one from the Dutch exam programme (CvTE eindterm 31).

### chem-d02-q01 | points 2 | outcome D2 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A student must heat a test tube with a dilute acid mixture. Give three precautions of safe working in a school laboratory taken directly from the properties of the substances and equipment involved.
#### Answer
Wear safety glasses and closed shoes (prop. substances: corrosive/irritating); heat with the tube's opening pointing away from people (risk of splashing when heated); and use a tube holder/clamp and heat-glass apparatus only of appropriate size (equipment: test tube on the flame is hot glass).
#### Solution
1. General protection: safety glasses (and lab coat/gloves where needed) shield eyes and skin against corrosive substances.
2. Hot mixtures can spatter or boil over: the tube points away from the holder and from neighbours.
3. Equipment reasoning: the right glassware (heat-proof glass), properly clamped: glass breaks where stress and heat meet — prevention from the properties of the material.

### chem-d02-q02 | points 3 | outcome D2 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Reading a safety pictogram: a reagent bottle carries the flame pictogram and the exclamation mark. (1) What do these symbols tell you? (2) Give two measures you take before using this reagent in a group experiment.
#### Answer
1. The liquid or vapour is flammable (flame) and harmful or irritant (exclamation mark: health hazard to skin/eyes/breath). 2. Put out all open flames/hot plates nearby (flammability), and work with gloves, goggles and when indicated a fume hood or at least a well-ventilated bench, with the smallest workable amount (toxicity).
#### Solution
1. Pictograms compress the properties of the substance that matter for handling: flame = keep ignition sources away; exclamation = harmful/irritant (not the extreme danger symbols, but still protection-worthy).
2. Measures follow the pictograms, not the mood: no flames, no heat sources, gloves/glasses, ventilation, limited amount, and wash hands.
3. Before any experiment you consult the pictograms and the safety data sheet — the D2 habit is this sequence: read, then act.

### chem-d02-q03 | points 3 | outcome D2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
In a purification experiment a hot saturated solution must crystallise and the crystals be collected. One classmate puts the waste filtrate down the sink; another leaves the hot flask on the edge of the bench. Evaluate both actions on safety and environment.
#### Answer
Sink: only allowed if the filtrate holds no hazardous or metal-containing substances; metal (e.g. copper) or organic-solvent filtrates go to the chemical-waste bottle (chemical waste rules protect the environment — and metal ions offend water quality). Flask at the edge: an accident waiting for a sleeve: hot glass belongs centred and marked, on a heat-resistant tile, cool-down workspace distinct from work area.
#### Solution
1. Waste rules: the sink is fine for dilute, harmless salt solutions; anything with heavy metals, halogenated/organic solvents goes to chemical waste. "Diluting into the sewer" is not a policy.
2. Hot glass is an invisible risk (it looks like cold glass): a hot flask parked where elbows pass is a burn risk — move it to the designated cooling spot.
3. D2 trains exactly this reasoning from properties to precautions: the action is judged against what could happen, not by intention.

### chem-d02-q04 | points 3 | outcome D2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Your experiment must detect iron(II) ions and then purify the product. Explain how you use (1) a property to analyse and (2) a property to purify, and state one safety measure for each step.
#### Answer
1. Analyse: add sodium hydroxide solution — a green precipitate of Fe(OH)₂ shows the iron(II) ion, a property: precipitation of specific hydroxides at the ion level. 2. Purify: use recrystallisation or washing the filtered precipitate with a little ice-cold water — a property: different solubility (impurities stay in solution/washing water). Safety: add drops with a pipette and gloves at (1) (NaOH is corrosive); cold hands/cold water and careful flask handling at (2) (hot solution can crack).
#### Solution
1. Analysing uses a characteristic, visible change (a reagent, see 04 — reactions): here a precipitation that identifies Fe²⁺.
2. Purifying exploits a different solubility between product and impurities (recrystallisation/washing; see D01).
3. Each step has its own hazards: alkali and hot glass — D2 asks you to name them together with the technique, not as an afterthought.

### chem-d02-q05 | points 3 | outcome D2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Design a small analysis-and-purification: a white powder that should be pure potassium chloride came from a stockroom and is doubted. (1) Which simple tests decide between KCl and NaCl or unknown substance? (2) If it needs purification, how? (3) Which safety point applies to the tests themselves?
#### Answer
1. Flame test: lilac-purple for potassium, strong yellow for sodium — fast and characteristic; solution tests (e.g. with Ag⁺: white AgCl precipitate) show the chloride. 2. Recrystallise from water: KCl is soluble, many impurities are not or remain dissolved in the mother liquor; dry the crystals. 3. Only small amounts (spatula flame test over the fume/bunsen burner, no open bottles), and safety glasses during the precipitation (NaOH/Ag-salts and handling the acidified solutions).
#### Solution
1. Analyse by property: flame colours are element-specific, a classic inorganic test; the Ag⁺ precipitation confirms the anion (Binas solubility decides the precipitate).
2. Purify by a different property: solubility — dissolve hot, cool, filter, dry (recrystallisation).
3. Small-scale with flame: danger comes from the gas/bunsen and from neat reagents — small amounts and glasses are the safety answer.

### chem-d02-q06 | points 4 | outcome D2 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The school wants a written safety protocol for an electrolysis experiment that produces hydrogen and oxygen at two electrodes. The producer advises a test with a glowing splint at both sides. Write the four most important protocol rules and justify each by a property of the substances involved.
#### Answer
1. Separate the two gas streams (cover one side) and mark the bottles, because a mixed H₂/O₂ stream explodes on ignition (flammability of H₂ — the pop). 2. No open flame near the hydrogen side; keep the splint away from the cell for the H₂ gas (the gas is flammable and with air explodes). 3. Ventilate: only small volumes, no gas storage (accumulated H₂ is a hazard). 4. Eye protection + insulated cables: the electrolyte (acid/base) is corrosive and the voltage source carries electrical risk — protect for both.
#### Solution
1. H₂ and O₂ that collect together are the stoichiometric explosion mixture: separate bottles, named and counted (property: H₂ flammability).
2. Testing with a glowing splint: O₂ flares it up again; H₂ goes "pop" near the mouth of the hydrogen bottle — keep flames far from the cell itself.
3. No storage: H₂ diffuses through and out of everything; collect only what you test (property: small H₂ molecules, leakage).
4. Electrical and chemical risks together: goggles for the electrolyte, dry hands and intact cables for the source — D2 evaluates risks from the properties of what is present.
