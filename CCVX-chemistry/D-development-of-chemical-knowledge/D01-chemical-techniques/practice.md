# D1 — Chemical techniques: practice

Eight original questions on the D1 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Mind the CCVX wording: "precipitation" in D1.1 names the separation technique bezinken (settling), and "extraction/washing" is one item.

### chem-d01-q01 | points 3 | outcome D1.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
For each of the following tasks, name a suitable separation technique and the difference in substance properties it is based on: (1) ground coffee from coffee, (2) clean water from sea water, (3) perfume molecules from the rest of a shampoo when it is poured into a bowl of two non-mixing liquids.
#### Answer
1. Filtration — difference in particle size. 2. Distillation — difference in boiling point. 3. Extraction — difference in solubility in the two liquids.
#### Solution
1. The coffee grounds are particles that a filter holds back; the drink passes: filtration (filtrate and residue).
2. Water has a much lower boiling point than dissolved salts: evaporate and condense it (distillation), leaving a residue of salt.
3. The perfume molecules dissolve better in the more apolar liquid: they move across (extraction), as with a separatory funnel; the property is solubility.

### chem-d01-q02 | points 3 | outcome D1.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A crystal-clear copper(II) sulfate solution has to yield solid salt, not a dilute solution. (1) Why is filtering useless? (2) Describe a two-step route to blue crystals and name the techniques. (3) What is the remaining liquid called after the crystals are removed?
#### Answer
1. The salt is dissolved at ion level: far smaller than filter pores, so it passes any filter. 2. First concentrate by evaporation (boil off part of the water), then let the concentrated solution cool so the salt crystallises; collect the crystals by filtration. 3. The mother liquor (still a saturated solution).
#### Solution
1. Dissolved Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions are nanometres across; no filter paper stops them.
2. Evaporation raises the concentration until the solubility is exceeded — on cooling, the solubility drops further and CuSO₄·5H₂O crystallises (indampen, then crystallisation).
3. The liquid above the collected crystals is the mother liquor: a saturated solution still holding some salt, which is why yield is not 100%.

### chem-d01-q03 | points 3 | outcome D1.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A group distils a red wine and claims the collected liquid is "pure ethanol". Describe two ways to check whether the separation was successful, and say what each check would show if it was not.
#### Answer
Measure the boiling point (constant at 78 °C only while a pure substance distils) and make a chromatogram or analyse by gas chromatography; a rising boiling range or several spots/peaks shows the distillate is still a mixture.
#### Solution
1. A pure substance has a sharp boiling point: a thermometer above the vapour that reads 78 °C steadily is consistent with ethanol; a rising temperature shows an azeotrope/water coming over too — the mixture is not fully separated.
2. Paper or gas chromatography: one spot (one peak) for a pure substance; several for a mixture. GC also estimates proportions (D1.6).
3. Analytical techniques are used exactly like this: to verify a separation and, if needed, to say what fraction of which substance remains (D1.2).

### chem-d01-q04 | points 3 | outcome D1.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A mixture of plant pigments is separated on paper chromatography. Explain at the level of molecular interactions why the green pigment travels further with the eluent than the yellow one, although both started at the same spot.
#### Answer
Chromatography separates by the balance between adsorption to the stationary phase and dissolving in the mobile phase: the green pigment adsorbs less strongly to the paper and/or dissolves better in the eluent, so it spends more time moving and travels further.
#### Solution
1. In the column (or on the paper) a substance is in a constant two-sided exchange: adsorbed (sticking) on the stationary phase, dissolved in the running medium (mobile phase).
2. Strong adsorption or poor solubility in the eluent = long time parked = short travel; weak adsorption/good solubility = moves along almost with the front.
3. So different substances travel different distances in the same time and separate; the R_f value measures exactly this travel (see 01 — pure substances and mixtures).

### chem-d01-q05 | points 3 | outcome D1.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A mixture of methanol, ethanol and propan-1-ol is injected into a gas chromatograph. The trace shows peaks at 2.1, 3.4 and 5.9 min. (1) Explain which physical process separates the alcohols inside the column. (2) Which substance most likely causes the 5.9 min peak and why? (3) What is the meaning of the retention time?
#### Answer
1. The compounds are carried by the carrier gas (mobile) over a stationary phase they adsorb/dissolve into to different extents, so they travel at different speeds. 2. Propan-1-ol: the largest molecule, with the most Van der Waals attraction to the (apolar) column coating, so it travels slowest. 3. The time a substance needs from injection to detection; each substance has its own retention time under fixed conditions, which identifies it.
#### Solution
1. In gas chromatography the vaporised mixture is carried through a column; substances are retarded according to their adsorption to the column's stationary phase.
2. Propan-1-ol (largest molecule, more Van der Waals contact) interacts most strongly with an apolar coating, so it is retarded most: the last peak (5.9 min). Methanol: 2.1; ethanol: 3.4 (in the middle by size).
3. The retention time is the measured travel time of a substance from injection to the detector — under the same column, gas flow and temperature it is a fingerprint of the substance (D1.4).

### chem-d01-q06 | points 4 | outcome D1.5 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The mass spectrum of a small organic molecule shows a molecular ion at m/z = 58 and strong fragments at m/z = 43 and m/z = 15. (1) Which molecule fits a molecular mass of 58 u among simple alkanones, and how does that fit an alkanone of 58 u? (2) Interpret the fragments at 43 and 15. (3) What does "characteristic pattern" mean in a mass spectrum?
#### Answer
1. Propanone (CH₃COCH₃, M = 58.08 u): the molecular ion [CH₃COCH₃]⁺ at m/z 58. 2. m/z 43 is CH₃CO⁺ (the acyl fragment, from loss of ·CH₃); m/z 15 is CH₃⁺. 3. Every substance fragments in its own reproducible way, so the set of fragment peaks and their heights — the whole pattern — identifies the substance, like a fingerprint.
#### Solution
1. A ketone of M = 58: C₃H₆O → 3 × 12.01 + 6 × 1.008 + 16.00 = 58.08 u: propanone; the molecular ion carries the whole molecule charge (peak at 58).
2. The molecular ion breaks at the C–C bond next to the carbonyl: [CH₃COCH₃]⁺ → CH₃CO⁺ + ·CH₃, giving a strong fragment at 43 (12.01 + 3 × 1.008 + 12.01 + 16.00 = 43.04 u) and CH₃⁺ at 15.
3. The "characteristic pattern" is the complete set of fragments with their relative heights; by comparing it with known patterns (or with logic about typical fragments) you identify substances — that is how mass spectrometry is used for analysis (D1.5).

### chem-d01-q07 | points 3 | outcome D1.6 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Two anaesthetics A and B in a blood sample give chromatogram peak areas 1200 and 600 (arbitrary units) with equal response factors. (1) What does the ratio of the areas say about the ratio of the amounts? (2) The sample is known to contain 0.30 μg mL⁻¹ of A; estimate the concentration of B.
#### Answer
1. The peak area is proportional to the amount of substance, so the sample holds twice as much A as B. 2. 0.15 μg mL⁻¹.
#### Solution
1. With equal response, twice the area means twice the amount: n(A) : n(B) = 1200 : 600 = 2 : 1.
2. If A is 0.30 μg mL⁻¹, B equals 0.30 × (600/1200) = 0.15 μg mL⁻¹ (only because the detector responds equally to both; otherwise a calibration curve per substance is needed).
3. This is the quantitative use of chromatography (D1.6): area = measure of the amount, read off with a standard for exact numbers.

### chem-d01-q08 | points 4 | outcome D1.6 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The mass spectrometer peak height of a standard solution of a flavour compound (0.20 mmol L⁻¹) is 400 mV. A diluted sample (×10 dilution) of fruit extract gives a peak of 1500 mV for the same compound, and the peak height is proportional to the concentration. (1) Calculate the concentration in the diluted sample. (2) Calculate the concentration in the original extract. (3) Name one reason why a lab uses a calibration series (several standards) instead of a single standard.
#### Answer
1. 0.75 mmol L⁻¹; 2. 7.5 mmol L⁻¹; 3. To verify the proportionality over the concentration range (and to estimate the error of the measurement), instead of assuming it from one point.
#### Solution
1. c(sample) = c(standard) × h(sample) / h(standard) = 0.20 × 1500 / 400 = 0.75 mmol L⁻¹.
2. The extract had been diluted by a factor 10, so the original extract held 10 × 0.75 = 7.5 mmol L⁻¹.
3. With one standard you bet everything on linearity; with a series you check linearity, find the range where the proportionality fails, and a best-fit line smooths out the random error of each single point.
