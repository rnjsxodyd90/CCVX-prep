# C10 — Activation energy: practice

Six original questions on the C10 learning outcomes, in CCVX style.

### chem-c10-q01 | points 2 | outcome C10 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain with the particle collision model what the activation energy of a reaction is, and why a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gas can wait for years at room temperature without reacting measurably.
#### Answer
The activation energy is the minimum energy that a collision between reacting particles must have for the collision to lead to reaction (to pass the transition state). At room temperature almost no H₂–O₂ collision has that energy, so the reaction is, for all practical purposes, frozen: the mixture is stable for years.
#### Solution
1. Reactions run only on effective collisions: collisions with at least the activation energy (and a workable orientation).
2. H₂ + O₂ is thermodynamically very favourable (strongly exothermic water formation), but the barrier is high.
3. A spark (local high temperature) heats a part of the mixture above the barrier; the reaction there releases so much heat that the whole mixture ignites — a classic activation-energy story.

### chem-c10-q02 | points 3 | outcome C10 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Two sugar cubes are identical. Student A drops one into lukewarm water and stirs, student B drops the other into boiling water without stirring. B's cube is gone sooner. (1) Which of the two faster-rate explanations is surely true here? (2) What would a catalyst change, and is there one available?
#### Answer
1. The temperature difference (B's water is much hotter, so far more collisions have enough energy — the activation-energy fraction is far larger). 2. A catalyst would lower the activation energy for both experiments; there is no practical catalyst for sugar dissolving (dissolving is a physical process, so "reaction rate" here is really about how fast particles can migrate and be carried off — an activation-energy picture of a different kind).
#### Solution
1. Not all questions that mention temperature are about chemistry; this one is about dissolving, but the faster run at higher T is still explained by more energetic collisions/movement.
2. There is no catalyst for a physical process as such; catalysts exist for chemical reactions (and dissolving sugar is not a reaction).
3. Note the discipline: activation-energy language fits reactions; for dissolving one speaks of diffusion and convection — the concept of rate with a barrier applies by analogy.

### chem-c10-q03 | points 3 | outcome C10 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Sketch the energy diagram of the reaction 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂, once without and once with a catalyst (MnO₂ or catalase). Label the activation energy for both curves, the transition state and the heat of reaction.
#### Answer
Exothermic curve with products below reactants; the curve with catalyst has a lower hump (lower Ea) and sometimes two humps (by steps), while the heat of reaction (start to end) is the same for both curves.
#### Solution
1. Draw reactants (2 H₂O₂) at one level and products (2 H₂O + O₂) lower: exothermic.
2. Uncatalysed: one high hump between them: the activation energy to the transition state.
3. Catalysed (MnO₂ or catalase): the hump is lower (the catalyst opens a pathway with a lower barrier, often in steps, with the catalyst regenerated).
4. Vertical distance reactants → products is identical in both diagrams: the catalyst never changes ΔE.

### chem-c10-q04 | points 3 | outcome C10 | difficulty medium
#### Question
In a biology class, an enzyme speeds up starch hydrolysis by a factor of 10⁷. A student says "the enzyme adds energy, so the reaction goes faster." Correct this with the activation-energy concept and the catalyst's role.
#### Answer
A catalyst/enzyme adds no energy at all: it opens a reaction path with a lower activation energy, so that at the same temperature a far larger fraction of collisions is effective (factor 10⁷); the reaction enthalpy is unchanged and the enzyme emerges unconsumed.
#### Solution
1. What limits the rate is the barrier, not the available chemical energy of the reactants.
2. The enzyme binds the substrate and forces it into a geometry and environment where the step is much easier: a lower Ea, and at ordinary temperature the effective-collision count jumps by the observed factor.
3. The enzyme molecule leaves the reaction unchanged and repeats the trick — that is why you need only a trace: catalysis, not fuel.

### chem-c10-q05 | points 3 | outcome C10 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Element conservation in a mechanism: a reaction runs in two steps: (1) A + Cat → AC, (2) AC + B → AB + Cat, with Cat a catalyst. (1) Give the overall equation. (2) Show that element conservation holds for the steps and for the total. (3) Why does Cat not appear in the total equation?
#### Answer
1. A + B → AB. 2. Every atom and every catalyst particle present before is present after in each step, so it holds per step and per total. 3. Cat is consumed in step 1 and regenerated in step 2 — over the whole cycle its net change is zero, so it leaves the mass balance of the reaction.
#### Solution
1. Adding step 1 and step 2: A + Cat + AC + B → AC + AB + Cat; cancel AC and Cat on both sides: A + B → AB.
2. Per step the element balance closes by construction; adding balanced steps keeps it balanced.
3. The catalyst is a helper that comes back; only reactants and products stand in the net equation — the CCVX concept "element conservation" is checked on the net equation as well as on each step.

### chem-c10-q06 | points 4 | outcome C10 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A factory runs an exothermic equilibrium reaction with a catalyst. An operator proposes to raise the temperature "because everything goes faster, and then the catalyst is no longer needed." List three errors or risks in this reasoning using activation energy, equilibrium and rate.
#### Answer
1. Higher temperature raises the rate, but the reaction is exothermic: heating lowers K (moves the equilibrium to the left), so the yield per pass falls — you get a faster reaction towards a worse mixture. 2. The catalyst's job is not to make the reaction possible but to keep the rate high, and without it the same temperature may still be far too slow for the plant's flow rate. 3. A higher temperature brings its own costs: more fuel, more heat management, more side reactions and possibly catalyst sintering or degradation — so "skip the catalyst" is false economy.
#### Solution
1. Rate and position are different things: temperature changes K (see C05 — chemical equilibrium, notes C5.3), the catalyst changes only the time to reach K.
2. Removing the catalyst raises the activation energy of the route: the unguarded reaction is slower, perhaps so slow that even at the higher temperature the throughput is unattainable.
3. Energy price + selectivity: hotter operation is more expensive and often dirtier. The decision is the joint optimisation of temperature and catalyst, never "catalyst or temperature".
