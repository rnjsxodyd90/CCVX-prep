# C1 — Chemical processes: practice

Fifteen original questions on the C1 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Use Binas where data are needed: the table of salt solubilities, the table of acid and base constants, the table of standard electrode potentials. T = 298 K unless stated.

### chem-c01-q01 | points 3 | outcome C1.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the equation for dissolving ammonium sulfate in water, and state which bonds are broken and which are formed in the process.
#### Answer
(NH₄)₂SO₄(s) → 2 NH₄⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq); the ion bonds in the lattice break and ion-dipole bonds between the ions and water molecules form.
#### Solution
1. A salt splits into its hydrated ions; the composite ions stay together and the charges fix the coefficients: (NH₄)₂SO₄(s) → 2 NH₄⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq).
2. Broken: the ion bonds between NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻ in the ionic lattice.
3. Formed: ion-dipole bonds to water molecules (hydration); the N–H and S–O atomic bonds inside the ions stay intact.

### chem-c01-q02 | points 2 | outcome C1.2 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Hydrogen iodide gas dissolves in water and reacts as a strong acid. Give the equation, and describe which bonds are broken and which are formed at the particle level.
#### Answer
HI(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq); the H–I atomic bond breaks, a new O–H bond forms in water, and ion-dipole bonds hydrate both ions.
#### Solution
1. HI is a strong acid, so it reacts completely: HI(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + I⁻(aq). The solution is written as its ions, never as HI(aq).
2. Broken: the H–I atomic bond (its electron pair stays with the I atom, which becomes I⁻).
3. Formed: a new bond between the proton and a lone pair of a water O atom (the oxonium ion H₃O⁺), plus ion-dipole bonds between the water molecules and H₃O⁺ and I⁻.

### chem-c01-q03 | points 2 | outcome C1.3 | difficulty easy
#### Question
State which bonds are broken when (1) solid iodine evaporates and (2) solid sodium chloride melts. Use this to explain why sodium chloride has a much higher melting point than iodine.
#### Answer
1. Van der Waals bonds between the I₂ molecules; 2. ion bonds in the NaCl lattice. Ion bonds are much stronger, so NaCl melts at a much higher temperature.
#### Solution
1. Iodine is a molecular substance: on evaporation the Van der Waals bonds between the I₂ molecules break; the I–I atomic bond inside each molecule stays intact.
2. Sodium chloride is a salt: melting breaks (part of) the ion bonds in the ionic lattice.
3. Ion bonds are much stronger than Van der Waals bonds, so breaking them costs far more energy: the melting point of NaCl is far above that of I₂.

### chem-c01-q04 | points 3 | outcome C1.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A mixture of ethanol (boiling point 78 °C) and water is distilled. Explain, in terms of the bonds between the particles, (1) why the first distillate is richer in ethanol than the mixture was, and (2) why distillation can never separate the molecules by breaking them apart. (3) A brown dye solution is stirred with activated carbon and then filtered clear. Explain which bonds hold the dye to the carbon.
#### Answer
1. The Van der Waals bonds and hydrogen bridges between ethanol molecules are on balance weaker, so ethanol evaporates more easily; 2. no atomic bonds break, so the molecules stay whole; 3. Van der Waals bonds between the large, rather apolar dye molecules and the surface of the carbon.
#### Solution
1. The substance with the weaker bonds between its molecules evaporates most easily. Ethanol molecules make weaker intermolecular bonds overall than water molecules (which form dense hydrogen-bridge networks), so the vapour above the mixture is richer in ethanol and the condenser returns it as a richer distillate.
2. Evaporation and condensation only break and make the bonds between the molecules; the atomic bonds inside the molecules stay intact, so the distillate consists of the same molecules, only in a changed ratio.
3. Activated carbon is porous and largely apolar, with a huge surface area; the large, rather apolar dye molecules form many Van der Waals bonds with that surface and are filtered off with the carbon (adsorption, not absorption).

### chem-c01-q05 | points 3 | outcome C1.6 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the equations for the complete combustion of (1) butane, C₄H₁₀, and (2) propan-1-ol, C₃H₇OH.
#### Answer
1. 2 C₄H₁₀ + 13 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O; 2. 2 C₃H₇OH + 9 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 8 H₂O
#### Solution
1. C first: 4 CO₂; then H: 10 H atoms → 5 H₂O; then O: 8 × 2 + 5 = 21 O atoms on the right, so 13/2 O₂; doubling gives 2 C₄H₁₀ + 13 O₂ → 8 CO₂ + 10 H₂O.
2. C first: 3 CO₂; then H: 8 H atoms → 4 H₂O; then O: 6 + 4 = 10 O atoms needed on the right, the fuel supplies 1, so 9/2 O₂; doubling gives 2 C₃H₇OH + 9 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 8 H₂O. Do not forget the O atom inside the fuel.
3. Check both with the element balance.

### chem-c01-q06 | points 3 | outcome C1.7 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Classify each reaction as acid-base or redox, and name the donor and the acceptor:
1. NH₃(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq) + H₂O(l)
2. Zn(s) + Fe²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Fe(s)
3. Mg(s) + 2 H₃O⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l)
#### Answer
1. Acid-base; donor H₃O⁺, acceptor NH₃. 2. Redox; donor (reductor) Zn, acceptor (oxidator) Fe²⁺. 3. Redox; donor (reductor) Mg, acceptor (oxidator) H₃O⁺.
#### Solution
1. Only an H⁺ moves, from H₃O⁺ to NH₃: proton transfer, an acid-base reaction. H₃O⁺ is the proton donor and NH₃ the proton acceptor.
2. Zn gives off two electrons (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2 e⁻) and Fe²⁺ takes them up: electron transfer, redox. Zn is the reductor (electron donor), Fe²⁺ the oxidator.
3. Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2 e⁻ and 2 H₃O⁺ + 2 e⁻ → H₂ + 2 H₂O: electrons move, so redox (an acid acting as an oxidator), not acid-base. Mg is the reductor, H₃O⁺ the oxidator.

### chem-c01-q07 | points 4 | outcome C1.8 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A Daniell cell consists of a zinc electrode in a Zn²⁺ solution and a copper electrode in a Cu²⁺ solution, joined by a salt bridge. The standard electrode potentials (Binas) are V₀(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V and V₀(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V.
1. Give the half-reactions at the negative and at the positive electrode.
2. State the direction of the electron flow through the wire.
3. Calculate the cell voltage under standard conditions.
#### Answer
1. Negative: Zn(s) → Zn²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻; positive: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻ → Cu(s). 2. From the zinc to the copper electrode. 3. 1.10 V.
#### Solution
1. The reductor (Zn, lowest V₀) reacts at the negative electrode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2 e⁻. The oxidator (Cu²⁺, highest V₀) reacts at the positive electrode: Cu²⁺ + 2 e⁻ → Cu.
2. Electrons are released at the negative electrode and flow through the external wire to the positive electrode: from zinc to copper. (Inside the cell, ions carry the charge through the salt bridge.)
3. ΔV = V₀(oxidator) − V₀(reductor) = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V. Use both values as printed; do not flip the sign of the reductor's value.

### chem-c01-q08 | points 4 | outcome C1.9 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A solution of copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed with carbon (graphite) electrodes. Use the standard electrode potentials in Binas to predict what forms at each electrode at the start of the electrolysis, and give the total equation. The electrode reactions of water are 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻ → H₂ + 2 OH⁻ (V₀ = −0.83 V) and 2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻ (V₀ = +1.23 V).
#### Answer
Negative electrode: copper forms (Cu²⁺ + 2 e⁻ → Cu); positive electrode: oxygen (2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻). Total: 2 Cu²⁺(aq) + 2 H₂O(l) → 2 Cu(s) + O₂(g) + 4 H⁺(aq).
#### Solution
1. At the negative electrode the strongest oxidator present is reduced. Present: Cu²⁺ (+0.34 V) and H₂O (−0.83 V). Cu²⁺ has the highest V₀, so Cu²⁺ + 2 e⁻ → Cu: copper deposits on the electrode.
2. At the positive electrode the strongest reductor present is oxidised. Present: H₂O (as reductor, V₀ +1.23 V) and SO₄²⁻ (a very weak reductor). Water wins: 2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻. The carbon electrode is inert, so it does not react.
3. Total (multiply the copper half-reaction by two to equalise the electrons): 2 Cu²⁺ + 2 H₂O → 2 Cu + O₂ + 4 H⁺.

### chem-c01-q09 | points 3 | outcome C1.10 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A beaker holds 25 mL of 0.10 M hydrochloric acid; a second beaker holds 25 mL of 0.10 M ethanoic acid. Explain why the two solutions have different pH values, but still need the same volume of 0.10 M caustic soda for complete neutralisation in a titration.
#### Answer
HCl is strong (fully ionised, higher [H₃O⁺], lower pH); ethanoic acid is weak (partly ionised, higher pH). Both contain the same amount of acid, and at the end point all acid has reacted, so they consume the same amount of OH⁻.
#### Solution
1. HCl reacts completely with water: [H₃O⁺] = 0.10 mol L⁻¹, pH = 1.00.
2. Ethanoic acid is in equilibrium CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺: only a small part has reacted, so [H₃O⁺] < 0.10 mol L⁻¹ and the pH is higher (about 2.9).
3. During titration OH⁻ keeps removing protons: CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O, and the equilibrium restores until all the acid has reacted. Both beakers contain 25 × 0.10 = 2.5 mmol of acid, so both need 2.5 mmol of OH⁻: the same volume of 0.10 M caustic soda.

### chem-c01-q10 | points 3 | outcome C1.11 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Give the equations for the following acid-base reactions; leave out spectator ions and use the correct particle notation (strong acid as ions, weak acid as molecules).
1. Hydrochloric acid is dripped onto marble chips (CaCO₃).
2. Hydrochloric acid is added to a sodium acetate (sodium ethanoate) solution.
#### Answer
1. 2 H₃O⁺(aq) + CaCO₃(s) → Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l); 2. H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq) → CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l)
#### Solution
1. Particles present: H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ (strong acid), and CaCO₃ is an insoluble salt (Binas, table of salt solubilities), so write it as (s). The carbonate takes up two protons and leaves as CO₂ gas: 2 H₃O⁺(aq) + CaCO₃(s) → Ca²⁺(aq) + CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l). Cl⁻ is the spectator ion.
2. H₃O⁺ is a much stronger acid than CH₃COOH (Binas, table of acid and base constants), so the ethanoate ion takes the proton: H₃O⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq) → CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l). Na⁺ is the spectator ion.
3. Both equations satisfy the element and charge balance.

### chem-c01-q11 | points 4 | outcome C1.12 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A buffer solution contains ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium chloride in comparable amounts.
1. Give the equation for the reaction when a little potassium hydroxide solution is added.
2. Give the equation for the reaction when a little hydrochloric acid is added.
3. Explain why the mixture of hydrochloric acid and sodium chloride is not a buffer.
#### Answer
1. NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O; 2. NH₃ + H₃O⁺ → NH₄⁺ + H₂O; 3. Cl⁻ does not take up protons, so there is no weak base to catch added acid.
#### Solution
1. Added OH⁻ reacts with the weak acid of the buffer: NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l). The strong base is replaced by the weak base NH₃, so the pH hardly changes.
2. Added H₃O⁺ reacts with the weak base of the buffer: NH₃(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq) + H₂O(l).
3. A buffer needs a weak acid and its conjugate base in comparable amounts. The conjugate base of the strong acid HCl is Cl⁻, which is far too weak as a base to take up added H₃O⁺; so added acid just accumulates as H₃O⁺ and the pH drops.

### chem-c01-q12 | points 3 | outcome C1.13 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Use the standard electrode potentials in Binas (V₀(Br₂/Br⁻) = +1.36 V; V₀(I₂/I⁻) = +0.54 V).
1. Explain whether iodine reacts with a solution of bromide ions.
2. Explain whether bromine reacts with a solution of iodide ions, and give the equation if it does.
#### Answer
1. No reaction: the oxidator I₂ (+0.54 V) is weaker than the oxidator belonging to the reductor Br⁻ (+1.36 V). 2. Yes: Br₂ + 2 I⁻ → 2 Br⁻ + I₂.
#### Solution
1. For a reaction to run, the oxidator must have a higher V₀ than the reductor. Here the oxidator would be I₂ (+0.54 V) and the reductor Br⁻; the belonging oxidator Br₂ has +1.36 V. +0.54 is not higher than +1.36, so there is no reaction (the "reaction" would have to run backwards).
2. Now the oxidator is Br₂ (+1.36 V) and the reductor I⁻ (belonging oxidator I₂, +0.54 V). +1.36 V > +0.54 V, so the reaction runs: Br₂ + 2 I⁻ → 2 Br⁻ + I₂. (Half-reactions: Br₂ + 2 e⁻ → 2 Br⁻ and 2 I⁻ → I₂ + 2 e⁻; add with equal electrons.)

### chem-c01-q13 | points 4 | outcome C1.14 | difficulty medium
#### Question
1. Derive the half-reaction for the couple ClO₃⁻/Cl₂ (chlorate to chlorine) in acidic solution.
2. Give the half-reaction of hydrogen as a reductor in an alkaline fuel cell (electrolyte: potassium hydroxide solution): H₂/H₂O.
#### Answer
1. 2 ClO₃⁻ + 12 H⁺ + 10 e⁻ → Cl₂ + 6 H₂O; 2. H₂ + 2 OH⁻ → 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻
#### Solution
1. Balance Cl: 2 ClO₃⁻ → Cl₂. Balance O with H₂O: 2 ClO₃⁻ → Cl₂ + 6 H₂O. Balance H with H⁺: 2 ClO₃⁻ + 12 H⁺ → Cl₂ + 6 H₂O. Balance charge with electrons: the left side has −2 + 12 = +10, so 10 e⁻ are added on the left: 2 ClO₃⁻ + 12 H⁺ + 10 e⁻ → Cl₂ + 6 H₂O.
2. In acidic form: H₂ → 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻. For basic solution, add 2 OH⁻ to both sides: H₂ + 2 OH⁻ → 2 H⁺ + 2 OH⁻ + 2 e⁻ = 2 H₂O + 2 e⁻. A half-reaction in basic solution contains no H⁺.

### chem-c01-q14 | points 5 | outcome C1.15 | difficulty hard
#### Question
An acidified potassium permanganate solution is decolourised by a hydrogen peroxide solution. The half-reactions (Binas) are:
- MnO₄⁻ + 8 H⁺ + 5 e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4 H₂O
- H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻

Give the total equation for this redox reaction, and check the charge balance.
#### Answer
2 MnO₄⁻ + 6 H⁺ + 5 H₂O₂ → 2 Mn²⁺ + 8 H₂O + 5 O₂; charge +4 on both sides.
#### Solution
1. Equalise the electrons: ×2 for permanganate (10 e⁻) and ×5 for hydrogen peroxide (10 e⁻).
2. Add: 2 MnO₄⁻ + 16 H⁺ + 5 H₂O₂ → 2 Mn²⁺ + 8 H₂O + 5 O₂ + 10 H⁺.
3. Cancel species on both sides (10 H⁺ from each side): 2 MnO₄⁻ + 6 H⁺ + 5 H₂O₂ → 2 Mn²⁺ + 8 H₂O + 5 O₂.
4. Charge balance: left −2 + 6 = +4; right +4. Element balance: Mn 2 = 2, O left 8 + 10 = 18, right 8 + 10 = 18, H 6 + 10 = 16 = 16. The reaction runs because +1.51 V (MnO₄⁻/Mn²⁺) is higher than +0.68 V (O₂/H₂O₂; both Binas).

### chem-c01-q15 | points 5 | outcome C1.16 | difficulty hard
#### Question
1. Give the equation, in structural formulas, for the esterification of propanoic acid with methanol (an equilibrium).
2. The ester formed is boiled with sodium hydroxide solution (saponification). Give the equation in structural formulas, and name the products.
#### Answer
1. CH₃–CH₂–C(=O)–O–H + H–O–CH₃ ⇌ CH₃–CH₂–C(=O)–O–CH₃ + H–O–H (methyl propanoate and water). 2. CH₃–CH₂–C(=O)–O–CH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃–CH₂–C(=O)–O⁻ + HO–CH₃: sodium propanoate and methanol.
#### Solution
1. In a condensation, the acid loses its OH and the alcohol the H of its OH group; a new C–O bond forms: CH₃CH₂COOH + CH₃OH ⇌ CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ + H₂O. The ester is methyl propanoate (an alkyl alkanoate); the O atom of the C–O–C link comes from the alcohol.
2. With OH⁻ the ester is not hydrolysed to the acid but to the alkanoate ion: CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃CH₂COO⁻ + CH₃OH. Products: propanoate (with Na⁺: sodium propanoate) and methanol. Check the bond count on every C atom (four bonds each).
