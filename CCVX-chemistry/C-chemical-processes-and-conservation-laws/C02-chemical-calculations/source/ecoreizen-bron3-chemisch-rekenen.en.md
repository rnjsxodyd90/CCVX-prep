# Source 3: Chemical calculations

## 3.1 Calculating with ratios

Substances can react with each other. You can show this with a reaction equation.

For example, magnesium reacts with a sulfuric acid solution:

Magnesium + sulfuric acid solution  →  magnesium sulfate + hydrogen gas

You can calculate with reaction equations. In BINAS table 99 you can find the relative atomic masses and use them to calculate the molecular mass of substances.

The unit of molecular mass is the u (= unit). In a reaction equation you may also fill in the ratio in u as a ratio in grams. Remember that the unit u also stays the same on the left and on the right of the arrow. So you can also write the reaction between magnesium and sulfuric acid solution as follows:

24.31 u Mg + 98.08 u H₂SO₄ →  120.4 u MgSO₄ + 2.016 u H₂

24.31 g Mg + 98.08 g H₂SO₄ →  120.4 g MgSO₄ + 2.016 g H₂

With a table you can now quickly calculate that 40.4 grams of magnesium are needed to make 200 grams of magnesium sulfate:

*[image not copied. From the text, a ratio table: 24.31 g Mg gives 120.4 g MgSO₄, so 200 g MgSO₄ needs 200 × 24.31 / 120.4 = 40.4 g Mg.]*

You may always keep using this way of calculating, in the final exam too.

In chemistry we also often calculate with gases and with solutions. Then it is more convenient to calculate via the amount of substance. That is what the next sections are about.

Hydrochloric acid (2.10 grams of HCl per 100 ml of solution) is made to react with 25.0 grams of sodium carbonate until all the sodium carbonate has just been used up:

Na₂CO₃ (aq) + 2 HCl (aq) → CO₂ (g) + H₂O (l) + 2NaCl (aq)

Calculate how many grams of HCl are needed to make 25.0 grams of sodium carbonate react completely. Use BINAS table 99.

Calculate how many ml of hydrochloric acid are therefore needed for this.

## 3.2 The amount of substance

The hydrogen atom is the lightest atom and has an atomic mass of 1.00 u.

You might wonder how many hydrogen atoms you need to take to get 1.00 gram of hydrogen atoms. We can calculate this now that we know how heavy one hydrogen atom is in kg. That is given in table 7 of Binas: 1.00 u = 1.66054·10⁻²⁷ kg = 1.66054·10⁻²⁴ gram.

So for 1.00 gram of H atoms you need: 1.00/1.66054·10⁻²⁴ = 6.02·10²³ atoms.

We call the number 6.02·10²³ the Avogadro constant.

The size of this number is beyond our comprehension. For example, 6.02·10²³ ants together weigh more than the entire Earth. *[Editorial: this is wrong. An ant weighs roughly 1 to 5 mg, so 6.02·10²³ ants weigh roughly 10¹⁸ kg, less than a millionth of the Earth's mass (6.0·10²⁴ kg).]*

When we have 6.02·10²³ molecules of a substance, we say that we have an amount of substance of 1.00 mol. Usually we shorten that by saying: we have 1.00 mol of substance. So 1.00 mol of any substance always contains the same number of particles.

> If 1 molecule of water (H₂O) weighs 18.0 u, then 1.00 mol of water weighs 18.0 grams
>
> If 1 molecule of CH₄ weighs 16.0 u, then 1.00 mol of CH₄ weighs exactly 16.0 grams

The molecular mass of H₂O has the same numerical value as the molar mass of H₂O.

The molar masses of many substances are in Binas table 98. A handy table that saves you a lot of calculating.

From now on we work with a new base quantity, ‘the amount of substance’ (n), whose unit is the mole (mol). (The symbols we use are in brackets.)

One mole of copper (formula Cu) weighs 63.55 grams.

One mole of carbon (formula C) weighs 12.01 grams.

One mole of oxygen gas (formula O2) weighs not 16.00 but 32.00 grams.

Worked example:

What is the mass in grams of 0.030 mol of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)?

Answer

You look up the molar mass of glucose in table 98: 180.2 g/mol.

So 1.00 mol of glucose weighs 180.2 grams.

It follows that 0.030 mol therefore weighs 0.030 x 180.2 = 5.4 grams ( m = n(glucose) x M )

## 3.3 Concentration and molarity

If you have a headache, you can take an aspirin. The package leaflet says that a different dose applies to children than to adults. It is important that the number of mg of medicine per litre in the plasma or in the tissue doesn't get too high. And children are simply a lot smaller than adults, so….

The content, or rather the concentration, of the medicine determines whether the medicine works or, above a certain concentration, can even be toxic (poisonous).

By concentration we mean the content of a substance, expressed in mol of substance

per litre of solution. The number of mol per litre is also called molarity.

\[…..\] = n(...)/ V

The symbol for concentration is the so-called concentration brackets, inside which the formula of the substance in question is placed: \[NaCl\] = 0.10 mol/l means that 0.10 mol of the substance NaCl is present per litre of solution.

Worked exercise

Calculate how many grams of NaCl are present in 100 ml of 0.015 mol/l NaCl solution.

Answer

\[NaCl\] = n(NaCl)/v

Rearranging this formula gives: n(NaCl) = \[NaCl\] \* v

So n(NaCl) = 0.015 · 0.100 = 0.0015 mol n(NaCl)= 58.44 g/mol *[Editorial: this should read M(NaCl) = 58.44 g/mol, the molar mass.]*

So the answer is: m(NaCl) = n(NaCl) \* M = 0.0015 \* 58.44 = 0.088 gram.

## 3.5 Mole ratios in reactions

According to Dalton's atomic model, a reaction is a regrouping of atoms into new molecules. That is why we balance a reaction equation, for example the combustion of butane gas:

2 C₄H₁₀(g) + 13 O₂(g) → 8 CO₂ (g) + 10 H₂O(g)

You can read this as: 2 molecules of C₄H₁₀ react with 13 molecules of oxygen to form 8 molecules of CO₂ and 10 molecules of H₂O.

You could also say: 2000 C₄H₁₀ molecules react with 13,000 oxygen molecules to form 8,000 CO₂ molecules and 10,000 H₂O molecules. Or to make it even nicer:

2 mol C₄H₁₀ molecules + 13 mol O₂ molecules → 8 mol CO₂ molecules + 10 mol H₂O molecules

We usually write the rule above as a mole ratio (this is a mathematical relation):

n(C₄H₁₀) / 2 = n(O₂) / 13 = n(CO₂) / 8 = n(H₂O) / 10

Worked exercise

Calculate how many mol of oxygen are needed to burn 3.45 mol of butane.

Answer

From the balanced reaction equation above you can derive the mole ratio between the substance asked for (oxygen) and the given substance (butane):

n(C₄H₁₀) / 2 = n(O₂) / 13

In this ratio you then fill in that 3.45 mol of butane is used:

3.45 / 2 = n(O₂)  / 13 so n(O₂) = 13/2 · 3.45 = 22.4 mol

## 3.6 Calculating with reactions

For this section you are required to know the calculation scheme of §3.4 by heart.

We do calculations with reactions in the same way as much as possible. This is also called SPA. SPA stands for *Systematische Probleem Aanpak* (systematic problem approach). This approach should become clear to you in the example below.

Worked exercise

How many grams of bromine are needed to make 10 g of aluminium react completely?

Answer

**Step 1** Write a balanced reaction equation:

2 Al + 3 Br₂ → 2 AlBr₃

**Step 2** Calculate the amount of substance in mol of the given substance:

Given is aluminium 10 g and MAl = 26.98 g/mol. The ratio table looks as follows:

*[image not copied. From step 4, it gives n(Al) = 10 / 26.98 = 0.371 mol.]*

**Step 3** Determine the mole ratio between the given substance and the substance asked for from the reaction equation:

n(Al) / 2 = n(Br₂) / 3

**Step 4** Calculate the number of mol of the substance asked for:

n(Al) / 2 = n(Br₂) / 3              0.371 / 2 = n(Br₂) / 3                n(Br₂) = 3/2 · 0.371 = 0.557 mol *[Editorial: without rounding n(Al) first, n(Br₂) = 0.556 mol.]*

**Step 5** Calculate what is asked:

Here the number of grams of bromine. So convert from mol of bromine to grams. You need the molar mass of bromine. MBr₂ = 159.80 g/mol. Make the ratio table.

*[image not copied. The answer works out to 0.556 × 159.80 = 89 g bromine, in 2 significant figures because 10 g has 2.]*

The five-step model is a model that brings some order to your calculations. This gets you to the right answer faster. So the five-step model consists of the 5 steps below:

Step 1: A balanced reaction equation

Step 2: Convert the given substance to the amount of substance in mol

Step 3: The mole ratio between the given substance and the substance asked for

Step 4: Combine points 2 and 3 into the amount of the substance asked for, in mol

Step 5: Give the answer to the question in the right unit

Step 4 of the five-step model is a very important point. When you move from one substance to another, you must always look at the mole ratio.

WATCH OUT: don't make the mistake of confusing the mole ratio with the mass ratio!
