# C4 — Reaction kinetics: practice

Ten original questions on the C4 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. T = 298 K and p = p₀ unless stated (V_m = 24.5 L mol⁻¹).

### chem-c04-q01 | points 2 | outcome C4.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain with the particle collision model why 1 g of powdered magnesium reacts much faster with dilute hydrochloric acid than 1 g of magnesium ribbon.
#### Answer
The powder has a much larger surface area of reacting substance, so many more H₃O⁺ particles collide with Mg per second: more collisions per unit of time, so a higher rate.
#### Solution
1. In the collision model, a reaction can start only when the reacting particles collide; the rate follows from the number of effective collisions per second.
2. Dividing the 1 g into powder enormously increases the surface area (surface area of reacting substances); only atoms at the surface can be hit by H₃O⁺.
3. More surface = more collisions per second = higher reaction rate; the total amount of product is unchanged (same 1 g of Mg).

### chem-c04-q02 | points 2 | outcome C4.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Marble chips fizz much faster in 1.0 M hydrochloric acid than in 0.10 M hydrochloric acid of the same temperature. Explain this using the collision model.
#### Answer
In the 1.0 M acid there are ten times as many H₃O⁺ particles per litre, so the number of collisions with the marble surface per second is (about) ten times higher: a higher rate.
#### Solution
1. Rate = number of effective collisions per second; the number of collisions per second with the chip surface is proportional to how many acid particles are present per unit volume.
2. Concentration 1.0 M versus 0.10 M means ten times the particle density of H₃O⁺.
3. So the 1.0 M acid gives far more collisions (and far more effective collisions) per second: the chips fizz faster. The reaction stops sooner too, because the acid is consumed faster.

### chem-c04-q03 | points 3 | outcome C4.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A rule of thumb says a reaction runs two to three times faster for every 10 °C rise in temperature. (1) Use this to explain why milk keeps longer in the fridge (4 °C) than in the kitchen (24 °C). (2) Give the particle explanation: why does a higher temperature speed up reactions so strongly?
#### Answer
1. At 4 °C the spoilage reactions run roughly 2–3² (4–9) times slower than at 24 °C, so the milk spoils much more slowly. 2. At higher temperature the particles move faster: they collide more often (small effect) and, more importantly, a much larger fraction of the collisions has energy at least equal to the activation energy (large effect).
#### Solution
1. 20 °C difference ≈ two steps of 10 °C → rate roughly 2² to 3² times lower in the fridge, i.e. 4–9 times slower.
2. Collision model: faster particles collide more frequently — but that only accounts for a few per cent per 10 °C.
3. The strong temperature effect is in the energy: only collisions with at least the activation energy react; at a higher temperature the fraction of particles with enough energy grows steeply, so the effective collision count rises sharply.

### chem-c04-q04 | points 3 | outcome C4.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
When hydrogen peroxide is poured on a wound, it decomposes very fast (catalase enzyme); on a clean glass plate it decomposes slowly. Explain the role of catalase as a catalyst with an energy diagram in words: what changes in the diagram, and what does not?
#### Answer
Catalase opens a reaction path with a lower activation energy (lower transition state), so many more collisions are effective and the reaction runs much faster; the energy levels of reactant and product (and so the heat of reaction) are unchanged, and the enzyme is not consumed.
#### Solution
1. A catalyst gives the reaction another pathway whose slowest step has a lower activation energy: in the diagram, the hump between H₂O₂ and its products (H₂O + O₂) is lower.
2. At the same temperature a much larger fraction of collisions now has enough energy to pass: faster.
3. Start and end levels are the principal quantities of the reaction itself: ΔH is unchanged (the catalyst does not shift the energy balance), and the catalyst molecules survive the reaction and can act again.
4. Because the same catalyst lowers the barrier for the reverse reaction too, it cannot change an equilibrium position — only the time to reach it (see C05).

### chem-c04-q05 | points 4 | outcome C4.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A student reacts an excess of magnesium ribbon with 50.0 mL of hydrochloric acid and collects the hydrogen. After 60 s, 36.0 mL of H₂ gas has been collected (T = 298 K, p = p₀). Calculate the average reaction rate over these 60 s, expressed as the amount of H₂ formed per second per litre of solution, in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.
#### Answer
4.90·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
#### Solution
1. n(H₂) = V / V_m = 36.0·10⁻³ L / 24.5 L mol⁻¹ = 1.47·10⁻³ mol.
2. Per litre of solution: 1.47·10⁻³ mol / 0.0500 L = 2.94·10⁻² mol L⁻¹ formed in 60 s.
3. Average rate s = Δc / Δt = 2.94·10⁻² / 60 = 4.90·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (three significant figures).
4. This is an average; the instantaneous rate was higher at the start and fell as the acid was used up (concentration effect, C4.1).

### chem-c04-q06 | points 3 | outcome C4.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
The decomposition of ozone by chlorine atoms is described by the steps: (1) Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂, (2) ClO + O₃ → Cl + 2 O₂. (1) What is the overall equation? (2) What is the role of Cl? (3) The rate-determining step is step 1; explain what that means for the overall rate.
#### Answer
1. 2 O₃ → 3 O₂. 2. Cl is a catalyst: it reacts in step 1 but is re-formed in step 2. 3. The rate-determining step is the slowest step; the overall reaction cannot go faster than this step, so the overall rate is set (limited) by step 1.
#### Solution
1. Adding the two steps and cancelling Cl and ClO (they appear on both sides) leaves the overall equation 2 O₃ → 3 O₂.
2. Cl is consumed in step 1 and regenerated in step 2 unchanged: net zero consumption — the definition of a (homogeneous) catalyst; ClO is an intermediate (formed, then consumed).
3. In a multi-step mechanism the steps differ in rate; the slowest is rate-determining. Doubling a faster step has no effect: the chain of helper particles can only turn over as fast as the slow step allows.

### chem-c04-q07 | points 3 | outcome C4.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
In the first step of the saponification of ethyl ethanoate, the OH⁻ ion attacks the carbonyl carbon of the ester. (1) Which particle is the nucleophile and which atom the electrophile? (2) Explain these terms with the charge distribution in the ester.
#### Answer
1. The OH⁻ ion is the nucleophile (electron-pair donor); the carbonyl carbon is the electrophile (electron-pair acceptor). 2. The C=O bond is polar: the O atom pulls the bonding electrons (δ− on O), leaving the C with δ+, so the negative OH⁻ uses a lone pair to bind precisely there.
#### Solution
1. A nucleophile ("nucleus-seeking") is an electron-pair donor, attacking a place with too few electrons; OH⁻, with three lone pairs and a negative charge, is a strong nucleophile.
2. An electrophile ("electron-seeking") is an electron-pair acceptor, a positive or partially positive centre.
3. In the C=O group, O is the more electronegative atom, so C carries δ+: the δ− of OH⁻ is attracted to this spot and forms the new C–O bond; that is the movement of the electron pair the mechanism arrows show.

### chem-c04-q08 | points 3 | outcome C4.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
The reaction of methane with chlorine under UV light is a chain reaction with free radicals: initiation Cl₂ → 2 Cl·; propagation Cl· + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃·, then CH₃· + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl·; termination, for example, 2 CH₃· → C₂H₆. (1) What is a free radical? (2) Why is step 1 of propagation called a chain step? (3) Why does a small amount of light start a large amount of reaction?
#### Answer
1. A free radical is a particle with an unpaired electron (a lone electron), and therefore very reactive. 2. Because each propagation cycle consumes one radical and forms a new one, the chain continues without new initiator. 3. Photons of UV split a few Cl₂ molecules into Cl radicals (initiation), and each radical then keeps the chain going through many propagation cycles before two radicals happen to meet and terminate — so a little light triggers much reaction.
#### Solution
1. Radicals (Cl·, CH₃·) carry one unpaired electron and seek a partner, so they attack molecules.
2. In propagation, a radical reacts to give the product plus another radical: the cycle repeats ("chain") and the reaction keeps running by itself.
3. Initiation needs energy (UV) to split Cl₂; but after that, one Cl· can make many CH₃Cl before a termination step 2 R· → R–R ends the chain. That is why a short flash lights the whole mixture.

### chem-c04-q09 | points 4 | outcome C4.4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A reactant's concentration is measured over time: at t = 0, [A] = 0.100 mol L⁻¹; at t = 20 s, 0.050 mol L⁻¹; at 40 s, 0.025; at 60 s, 0.013. (1) Calculate the average reaction rate in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ over the first 20 s and over the interval 40–60 s. (2) Explain the difference. (3) How would you obtain the instantaneous rate at t = 20 s from the graph?
#### Answer
1. 2.5·10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (0–20 s); 6·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (40–60 s). 2. The rate falls because the concentration falls (fewer collisions per second). 3. Draw the tangent line to the concentration–time curve at t = 20 s and take its slope (with a minus sign for a disappearing reactant).
#### Solution
1. 0–20 s: s = Δc/Δt = (0.100 − 0.050) / 20 = 2.5·10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. 40–60 s: (0.025 − 0.013) / 20 = 6·10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.
2. As A is consumed, its concentration drops; fewer A particles per litre means fewer collisions per second, so the instantaneous rate drops too (the curve flattens).
3. The instantaneous rate at one moment is the slope of the tangent line at that point of the [A]-versus-t curve; taking a big interval only yields an average.

### chem-c04-q10 | points 4 | outcome C4.1 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A lab wants to dissolve a batch of limestone (marble chips, CaCO₃ with surface impurities) in dilute hydrochloric acid as quickly as possible, without using a stronger acid and without boiling the mixture. Give three changes to the practical set-up that will speed up the reaction, and justify each with the collision model.
#### Answer
Grind the chips (larger surface area), stir or shake (removes the locally acid-poor layer at the surface and keeps chips and acid colliding), and/or use a higher acid concentration (more particles per litre). Warming the mixture would speed it up too but is excluded here.
#### Solution
1. Grinding: increases the surface area of reacting substance — only surface atoms can be hit, so powder gives far more collisions per second.
2. Stirring/shaking: sweeps away the reacted solution near the surface and supplies fresh acid constantly, so the effective concentration at the surface stays high; it also knocks loose the CO₂ bubbles that shield the surface.
3. Higher concentration: more H₃O⁺ particles per unit volume collide with the surface each second.
4. (Excluded but worth knowing: a higher temperature raises the fraction of collisions with energy above the activation energy; and a catalyst is not available for this reaction at room temperature.)
