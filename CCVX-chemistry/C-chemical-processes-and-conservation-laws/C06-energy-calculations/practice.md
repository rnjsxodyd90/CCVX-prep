# C6 — Energy calculations: practice

Ten original questions on the C6 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Heats of formation come from the Binas table of heats of formation (standard conditions, T = 298 K): C(s) = 0, H₂(g) = 0, O₂(g) = 0; CH₄(g) = −0.75·10⁵, C₂H₅OH(l) = −2.78·10⁵, CH₃OH(l) = −2.39·10⁵, NH₃(g) = −0.46·10⁵, CO₂(g) = −3.935·10⁵, H₂O(l) = −2.858·10⁵, CaCO₃(s) = −12.07·10⁵, CaO(s) = −6.35·10⁵ J mol⁻¹.

### chem-c06-q01 | points 2 | outcome C6.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Describe the energy diagram of an exothermic reaction: mark the reactants, the products, the transition state, the activation energy and the heat of reaction, and state what changes when a catalyst is present.
#### Answer
The products lie lower in energy than the reactants (the heat of reaction is negative, energy released); between them lies a hump: the transition state, whose height above the reactants is the activation energy. With a catalyst the hump is lower; start and end levels (and the heat of reaction) stay the same.
#### Solution
1. Energy on the vertical axis, reaction progress on the horizontal axis. Exothermic: reactants above products; the difference is the heat of reaction (ΔE < 0).
2. The route over the hump passes through the transition state: from reactants to transition state is the activation energy.
3. A catalyst opens a path with a lower transition state for the forward and the reverse reaction equally: Ea drops, ΔE does not (see C05 for the equilibrium consequence).

### chem-c06-q02 | points 3 | outcome C6.2 | difficulty easy
#### Question
What is meant by the heat of formation of carbon dioxide? Give the corresponding reaction equation and the value in Binas. Explain why the heat of formation of O₂(g) is zero.
#### Answer
The heat of formation is the heat of reaction for forming one mol of the substance from its elements in their normal states: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH_f = −3.935·10⁵ J mol⁻¹. O₂(g) is an element in its normal state, so its heat of formation is zero by definition.
#### Solution
1. The heat of formation of a compound is defined per mol of that compound, starting from the non-decomposable substances (elements) in their standard form.
2. For CO₂: C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), ΔH_f = −3.935·10⁵ J mol⁻¹ (Binas): strongly exothermic.
3. Forming O₂(g) "from its elements" is no reaction at all (O₂(g) → O₂(g)), so every element in its normal state has ΔH_f = 0. This zero point makes formation data usable in reaction-heat sums.

### chem-c06-q03 | points 4 | outcome C6.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Calculate the heat of reaction (ΔE, in J per mol of reaction as written) for the combustion of methane: CH₄(g) + 2 O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l). Heats of formation are in the data lines at the top of this practice file.
#### Answer
ΔE = −8.90·10⁵ J (exothermic)
#### Solution
1. Rule: ΔE = (sum of heats of formation of the products) − (sum of heats of formation of the reactants), each multiplied by its coefficient.
2. Products: CO₂ −3.935·10⁵ + 2 × H₂O(l) −2.858·10⁵ = −9.651·10⁵ J.
3. Reactants: CH₄ −0.75·10⁵ + 2 × 0 (O₂) = −0.75·10⁵ J.
4. ΔE = −9.651·10⁵ − (−0.75·10⁵) = −8.90·10⁵ J per mol of CH₄. Negative, so exothermic — as every combustion.

### chem-c06-q04 | points 4 | outcome C6.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Calculate the heat of reaction of the ammonia synthesis as written: N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) → 2 NH₃(g). Is it wise for the yield to cool the reactor? Answer with your calculated energy effect and what you know about equilibrium and rate.
#### Answer
ΔE = −0.92·10⁵ J per mol of reaction as written (exothermic). Cooling raises the equilibrium fraction of NH₃ (K grows for an exothermic direction) but lowers the rate, so industry chooses a compromise temperature with a catalyst.
#### Solution
1. ΔE = 2 × (−0.46·10⁵) − (0 + 0) = −0.92·10⁵ J per mol of reaction as written.
2. The synthesis is exothermic, so by C05 a lower temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right (K increases): better yield.
3. But a lower temperature means a slower reaction (fewer effective collisions): the equilibrium yield is only reached slowly.
4. Practice: a catalyst (iron) plus a moderately high temperature — a compromise between equilibrium position and rate; and the formed NH₃ is removed (condensed) to keep drawing the equilibrium to the right.

### chem-c06-q05 | points 4 | outcome C6.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Calculate the chemical energy released when 1.00 kg of natural gas, taken to be pure methane, burns completely to CO₂(g) and H₂O(l). Use the heat of reaction from chem-c06-q03 (−8.90·10⁵ J mol⁻¹); M(CH₄) = 16.04 g mol⁻¹.
#### Answer
5.55·10⁷ J (about 55 MJ)
#### Solution
1. n(CH₄) = m / M = 1000 g / 16.04 g mol⁻¹ = 62.3 mol.
2. Heat released: 62.3 × 8.90·10⁵ = 5.55·10⁷ J (chemical energy converted into heat).
3. By the law of conservation of energy, the 5.55·10⁷ J does not vanish: it ends up as heat in the flame gases and the surroundings. This is the chemical energy "in" one kilogram of gas — the number an energy balance of a boiler or a power plant starts from.

### chem-c06-q06 | points 3 | outcome C6.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
The thermolysis of limestone is CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). Calculate the heat of reaction and classify the process as endothermic or exothermic.
#### Answer
ΔE = +1.79·10⁵ J; endothermic.
#### Solution
1. ΔE = [ΔH_f(CaO) + ΔH_f(CO₂)] − ΔH_f(CaCO₃).
2. ΔE = [−6.35·10⁵ + (−3.935·10⁵)] − (−12.07·10⁵) = −10.29·10⁵ + 12.07·10⁵ = +1.79·10⁵ J per mol of CaCO₃.
3. Positive ΔE: energy is consumed, so the process is endothermic — matching the fact that a lime kiln must be kept hot continuously.

### chem-c06-q07 | points 4 | outcome C6.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A gas burner heats 1.00 kg of water from 20 °C to 80 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. (1) Calculate the heat absorbed by the water. (2) Calculate the mass of methane that must burn (to CO₂ and H₂O(l), ΔE = −8.90·10⁵ J mol⁻¹) if 60% of the combustion heat reaches the water.
#### Answer
1. 2.5·10⁵ J. 2. 7.5 g methane.
#### Solution
1. Q = m × c × ΔT = 1000 g × 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ × 60 K = 2.51·10⁵ J → 2.5·10⁵ J (two significant figures, as 60 is given to that precision).
2. The water needs 2.5·10⁵ J; with 60% efficiency the burner must produce 2.5·10⁵ / 0.60 = 4.2·10⁵ J.
3. n(CH₄) = 4.2·10⁵ / 8.90·10⁵ = 0.47 mol; m = 0.47 × 16.04 = 7.5 g. (The first law is honoured: the other 40% warms the pan, the flame gases and the kitchen.)

### chem-c06-q08 | points 3 | outcome C6.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
The formation of photochemical smog involves the reaction N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 NO(g), ΔE = +1.81·10⁵ J. (1) Classify the reaction. (2) Sketch its energy diagram. (3) Why does this reaction nevertheless occur in car engines?
#### Answer
1. Endothermic. 2. Reactants lower than products by 1.81·10⁵ J, with an activation hump between them. 3. Because the temperature in the engine is very high: many collisions then have the (large) activation energy needed, so the reaction runs even though it consumes energy; on cooling, the NO is "frozen" before it can convert back.
#### Solution
1. Positive heat of reaction → endothermic.
2. Diagram: N₂ + O₂ at the lower level, 2 NO at 1.81·10⁵ J higher, transition state above both.
3. Heat is supplied by the engine (combustion of the fuel is strongly exothermic, altogether the system follows the law of conservation of energy); the very high flame temperature provides enough effective collisions. During the quick cooling of the exhaust gases the back reaction becomes too slow to remove the NO, so NO survives into the air.

### chem-c06-q09 | points 4 | outcome C6.3–C6.4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Calculate the heat of combustion of liquid methanol: CH₃OH(l) + 1½ O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2 H₂O(l). A prototype fuel cell converts 40% of this heat into electrical energy; the rest goes to heat. (1) Calculate the reaction heat per mol of methanol. (2) Calculate the electrical energy obtained per mol and compare it with burning the same mol of methanol to heat the room.
#### Answer
1. ΔE = −7.26·10⁵ J mol⁻¹. 2. Electrical: 2.9·10⁵ J mol⁻¹; heat of combustion for the room would deliver the full 7.26·10⁵ J as heat (but no work).
#### Solution
1. ΔE = [−3.935·10⁵ + 2 × (−2.858·10⁵)] − [−2.39·10⁵] = −9.651·10⁵ + 2.39·10⁵ = −7.26·10⁵ J mol⁻¹.
2. Per mol of methanol: 0.40 × 7.26·10⁵ = 2.9·10⁵ J electrical energy (+ 4.4·10⁵ J of residual heat); burned in a burner: up to 7.26·10⁵ J of heat but 0 J of electricity.
3. This is why fuel cells are interesting: converting chemical energy directly into electrical energy skips the lossy heat → engine → generator route (energy conversion, C6.4). The energy is conserved in both cases; only its usefulness differs (see C09).

### chem-c06-q10 | points 5 | outcome C6.4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A comparison is made between hydrogen and methane as fuels, per gram of fuel. The combustion of hydrogen is 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2 H₂O(l). (1) Calculate the energy released per gram for H₂ (M = 2.016 g mol⁻¹) and for CH₄ (M = 16.04 g mol⁻¹, ΔE(combustion) = −8.90·10⁵ J mol⁻¹). (2) Use the comparison to argue why some engineers prefer hydrogen, and name one counter-argument on the production side.
#### Answer
1. H₂: 1.42·10⁵ J g⁻¹; CH₄: 5.55·10⁴ J g⁻¹ — per gram, hydrogen gives about 2.6 times as much energy. 2. Pro: very high energy per gram and a clean product (only water). Contra: hydrogen today is made from natural gas or by electrolysis, which costs a lot of (possibly fossil) energy — the balance only becomes clean with renewable electricity.
#### Solution
1. From 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O(l): ΔE = 2 × (−2.858·10⁵) − 0 = −5.716·10⁵ J per 2 mol H₂. Per mol H₂: 2.858·10⁵ J; per gram: 2.858·10⁵ / 2.016 = 1.42·10⁵ J g⁻¹. For methane: 8.90·10⁵ / 16.04 = 5.55·10⁴ J g⁻¹.
2. Per gram, hydrogen tops every conventional fuel, and its combustion product is water: attractive for vehicles where mass matters.
3. But molecular hydrogen barely occurs on Earth: making it (reforming natural gas: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3 H₂, or electrolysis) costs energy, and then H₂ still has to be compressed or liquefied — with losses. The whole energy balance, not the per-gram number, decides (see F03, C09).
