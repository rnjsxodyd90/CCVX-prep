# C7 — Classification of reactions: practice

Eight original questions on the C7 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. The task is always: classify and justify.

### chem-c07-q01 | points 3 | outcome C7 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Classify each of the following reactions and justify the classification: (1) CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O; (2) 2 H₂O(l) → 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g) driven by direct current; (3) H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2 H₂O.
#### Answer
1. Combustion (and a redox reaction: the C of CH₄ is oxidised, O₂ the oxidator). 2. Electrolysis (decomposition driven by electric current; also redox). 3. Acid-base reaction (proton transfer) between a strong acid and a strong base.
#### Solution
1. A compound of C and H burns completely: combustion; electrons move from the fuel to O₂ — so it is also redox.
2. Decomposition (*ontleding*) of one substance, driven electrically: electrolysis.
3. Only an H⁺ passes from H₃O⁺ to OH⁻: proton transfer — the neutralisation, typical acid-base.
4. Classification asks for the characteristic change, and a reaction can hold more than one label (combustion is also redox).

### chem-c07-q02 | points 2 | outcome C7 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Classify: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) (in a kiln); and a photographic plate darkening in light by the decomposition of AgBr.
#### Answer
The first is thermolysis (decomposition driven by heat); the second is photolysis (decomposition driven by light).
#### Solution
1. Thermolysis: heat splits CaCO₃ into CaO + CO₂ — the kiln reaction.
2. Photolysis: light splits AgBr into Ag (the dark image) and Br₂ — the mechanism of film photography.
3. Both are decomposition reactions; the driver (heat/light/current) fixes the name: thermo-, photo-, electrolysis.

### chem-c07-q03 | points 3 | outcome C7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
For each industrial precipitation/dissolution step below, classify the reaction: (1) adding barium chloride solution to a sulfate solution gives a white solid; (2) mixing solutions of lead nitrate and potassium iodide gives a yellow solid; (3) boiling glycerol tristearate with caustic soda gives soap and glycerol.
#### Answer
1. Precipitation (Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s)). 2. Precipitation (Pb²⁺ + 2 I⁻ → PbI₂(s)). 3. Saponification (ester + OH⁻ → carboxylate salt + alcohol).
#### Solution
1. Two solutions, one insoluble salt out: precipitation; Binas solubility table shows BaSO₄ is insoluble.
2. Same pattern; PbI₂ is a striking yellow precipitate.
3. An ester hydrolysed in strong base — the fat to soap conversion — is called saponification (a hydrolysis variant with the alkali taking the acid part as its salt).

### chem-c07-q04 | points 3 | outcome C7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Classify the following organic reactions: (1) ethene + bromine → 1,2-dibromoethane; (2) methane + chlorine (UV) → chloromethane + HCl; (3) ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl ethanoate + water.
#### Answer
1. Addition (a molecule is added across the C=C double bond; a single product). 2. Substitution (an H atom of methane is replaced by Cl; free-radical mechanism under UV). 3. Condensation, more specifically ester formation (two molecules join, water splits off; an equilibrium reaction).
#### Solution
1. One bond of the C=C opens and two new bonds form with Br; nothing is released: addition.
2. Cl replaces H; a radical chain mechanism (see C04 q08): substitution — and because radicals are involved, also classifiable as a free-radical mechanism reaction.
3. Two molecules condense to an ester while H₂O leaves: condensation/esterification; its reverse is hydrolysis.
4. These three are the classic trio of C7 in organic chemistry: addition, substitution, condensation — read the products to classify.

### chem-c07-q05 | points 3 | outcome C7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A student writes: "dissolving and precipitation are reverse processes, so dissolution is not a chemical classification." Comment on this, distinguishing physical dissolving from reaction types, by choosing: is NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) a precipitation/dissolution-class event, and is CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 5 H₂O the same event type?
#### Answer
Both are dissolution events: the ionic lattice separates into hydrated ions; precipitation (formation of an insoluble salt from solutions) is indeed its reverse. The list however treats them for use when naming what happens in a mixture — the dissolution of these two salts is the same event type, and a hydrate's dissolving additionally releases its crystal water.
#### Solution
1. Dissolution = the processes of C1.1: lattice bonds break, ion-dipole bonds form; no new substances form, but the event is treated by chemists and asked in classifications.
2. Precipitation is its reverse: hydrated ions assemble into a solid lattice (Binas solubility decides).
3. With a hydrate, the crystal water is freed as solvent water: same class, one more product line.

### chem-c07-q06 | points 4 | outcome C7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Assign each reaction to two applicable classes: (1) 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂; (2) NH₃ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ NH₄⁺ + H₂O; (3) polymerisation of many ethene molecules into polyethene.
#### Answer
1. Decomposition and redox (O is simultaneously oxidised and reduced — disproportionation). 2. Acid-base and equilibrium (reversible) reaction. 3. Polymerisation and addition reaction (repeated addition).
#### Solution
1. One substance splits: decomposition; in H₂O₂ the O is at −1 and ends in −2 (H₂O) and 0 (O₂): redox by self-transfer (disproportionation).
2. Proton transfer, and it does not run to the end: acid-base + equilibrium.
3. Repeating addition of C=C monomers: addition polymerisation — addition at every step.
4. Double labels show that C7 classes overlap; choose the most informative name (for q1: decomposition is the headline).

### chem-c07-q07 | points 3 | outcome C7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Methanol is synthesised: CO + 2 H₂ → CH₃OH. Classify this reaction among the C7 types and justify the classification with the atoms and bonds involved. Also classify its reverse.
#### Answer
A synthesis reaction (two substances join into one; a C–H bond forms and the C≡O bond is reduced to C–O with new O–H and C–H bonds). Its reverse — methanol breaking down to CO + H₂ — is a decomposition.
#### Solution
1. Synthesis means several reactants form one product (the opposite of decomposition): CO and H₂ combine into a single CH₃OH molecule.
2. Bonds: the reactants' molecules break open and new bonds (C–H ×2 extra, C–O, O–H) form in one product.
3. The reverse, CH₃OH → CO + 2 H₂, is the decomposition — classification mirrors the direction of writing.

### chem-c07-q08 | points 4 | outcome C7 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A biofuel plant runs the step: triglyceride + 3 CH₃OH ⇌ 3 FAME (methyl esters) + glycerol. (1) Give two classifications for this reaction. (2) Given another reaction in the same plant, someone labels "KOH + H₃O⁺ → K⁺ + 2 H₂O" as a precipitation. Correct the classification with reasons. (3) Explain why "reversible/equilibrium reaction" competes with other labels and how C7 wants you to use it.
#### Answer
1. Transesterification (an ester exchange) and condensation-type; also reversible/equilibrium. 2. Not a precipitation: no solid forms; the potassium hydroxide is a base reacting with acid — an acid-base reaction (neutralisation): H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2 H₂O, with K⁺ as spectator. 3. "Reversible/equilibrium" is a label about direction, independent of the reaction type: every reaction can in principle run as an equilibrium; use it next to the type (e.g. "esterification, as an equilibrium").
#### Solution
1. The alcohol part of the ester swaps (glycerol ↔ methanol on the fatty acid chain): transesterification, with a small molecule released along the chain steps and as an equilibrium (⇌).
2. A precipitation ends in an insoluble salt; K⁺ and the acid's anion stay in solution, and H₂O forms: the event is proton transfer — acid-base.
3. Labels of C7 sit on different axes: direction (irreversible vs reversible/equilibrium), driver (thermo/photo/electro), mechanism (radical), and chemistry (acid-base, redox, precipitation, condensation, addition, substitution, etc.); a good answer states the chemistry and the direction separately.
