# C7 — Classification of Reactions: notes

These are English explanations of every learning outcome in [README.md](README.md), and every topic is *drafted*: written from the CCVX and CvTE syllabi and standard VWO chemistry, not yet checked against an open source text or exam question (see [PLAN.md](../../PLAN.md) for the statuses).

Dutch terms are in brackets. A code such as *(CvTE M1.4)* points to the matching specification in the [CvTE 2027 syllabus](../../syllabus/cvte-syllabus-scheikunde-vwo-2027.pdf); Binas tables are named by subject, because their numbers differ between editions. This subdomain is school-exam only in the Netherlands, so the CvTE syllabus doesn't specify it: *(SLO C7)* points to the [SLO handreiking](https://www.slo.nl/handreikingen/havo-vwo/handreiking-se-schei-hv/toetsen-schoolexamen/vakinhoudelijke-0/), which names the same concepts, and CvTE codes mark overlap with the central exam.

## Thermolysis, photolysis and electrolysis

- All three are decomposition reactions (*ontledingsreacties*): one compound splits into two or more new substances. They differ in the energy that drives them, and because decompositions are usually endothermic, that energy has to be supplied all the time. *(CvTE R1.3)*
- Thermolysis (*thermolyse*): decomposition by heating, e.g. burning limestone, CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). *(CvTE R1.3)*
- Photolysis (*fotolyse*): decomposition by light, e.g. silver chloride darkening in light as silver forms, 2 AgCl(s) → 2 Ag(s) + Cl₂(g). UV light splits molecules in the upper atmosphere in the same way ([C08](../C08-technological-aspects/notes.md)). *(CvTE R1.3)*
- Electrolysis (*elektrolyse*): decomposition by an electric current from a power source, which turns electrical energy into chemical energy, e.g. water into hydrogen and oxygen, 2 H₂O(l) → 2 H₂(g) + O₂(g). *(CvTE R1.3, T6.5)*
- In an electrolysis the reduction takes place at the negative electrode, 2 H₂O(l) + 2 e⁻ → H₂(g) + 2 OH⁻(aq), and the oxidation at the positive electrode, 2 H₂O(l) → O₂(g) + 4 H⁺(aq) + 4 e⁻, so every electrolysis is also a redox reaction. *(CvTE R1.3, T6.5)*
- **Trap:** in an electrolysis the reduction is at the negative electrode, but in a battery the reductor reacts at the negative electrode. Dutch exams say positive and negative electrode (*positieve en negatieve elektrode*), not anode and cathode.

## Hydrolysis and saponification

- Hydrolysis (*hydrolyse*): a bond in a molecule is broken by reaction with water, and the H and the OH of the water molecule end up on the two fragments. It is the reverse of a condensation reaction. Example, an ester: CH₃–COO–CH₃ + H₂O → CH₃–COOH + CH₃–OH (methyl ethanoate gives ethanoic acid and methanol). Digestion breaks down proteins, starch and fats by hydrolysis ([G01](../../G-society-chemistry-and-technology/G01-chemistry-of-life/notes.md)). *(CvTE R1.3, R1.16)*
- Saponification (*verzeping*): the hydrolysis of an ester by a strong base such as caustic soda (*natronloog*). The base is used up, and the products are an alcohol and the negative ion of the carbon acid (*carbonzuur*), not the acid itself: CH₃–COO–CH₃ + OH⁻ → CH₃–COO⁻ + CH₃–OH. *(CvTE R1.16)*
- Saponifying a fat (a triglyceride, here glyceryl tristearate) gives propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) and soap, the salts of fatty acids: (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₃C₃H₅ + 3 OH⁻ → C₃H₅(OH)₃ + 3 C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻. With caustic soda the soap is sodium stearate. *(CvTE R1.16, L1.3)*
- **Trap:** in a saponification OH⁻ is a reactant (3 OH⁻ per fat molecule), not a catalyst. An acid, by contrast, only catalyses the hydrolysis of an ester and is formed again.

## Addition and substitution

- Addition (*additie*): two molecules combine into one. In organic chemistry a molecule adds to a double (or triple) bond, which becomes a single (or double) bond, and there is only one product: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂Br–CH₂Br (1,2-dibromoethane). Bromine loses its colour in this reaction, which is the usual test for C=C. Adding H₂ or H₂O in the same way gives ethane or ethanol. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.15)*
- With buta-1,3-diene, Br₂ can add to one C=C bond (1,2-addition, giving CH₂Br–CHBr–CH=CH₂) or to C1 and C4, with a new double bond in the middle (1,4-addition): CH₂=CH–CH=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂Br–CH=CH–CH₂Br. More in [C01](../C01-chemical-processes/notes.md). *(CvTE R1.15)*
- Substitution (*substitutie*): an atom or group in a molecule is replaced by another one, so two reactants give two products. Examples: methane and chlorine in UV light, CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl (a radical mechanism, see below), and a halogen alkane with hydroxide ions, CH₃–CH₂Br + OH⁻ → CH₃–CH₂OH + Br⁻. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.16)*
- **Trap:** count the products. Addition to a C=C bond gives one product; if HCl or HBr forms as well, the reaction was a substitution. With halogens, alkenes react by addition and alkanes, which have no double bond, by substitution.

## Precipitation and dissolution

- Dissolution (*oplossen*): a substance spreads out between the particles of a solvent. When a salt dissolves in water, the ion lattice breaks up and the ions are surrounded by water molecules (hydration, *hydratatie*, through ion-dipole bonds): NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). A molecular substance keeps its molecules intact; only the bonds between its molecules break, and new hydrogen bridges or Van der Waals bonds with water form: C₆H₁₂O₆(s) → C₆H₁₂O₆(aq). *(CvTE M3.9)*
- Some substances react with water as they dissolve, such as an acid that forms oxonium ions: HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), which is also an acid-base reaction ([C01](../C01-chemical-processes/notes.md)). *(CvTE R1.7)*
- Precipitation (*neerslagreactie*): when two solutions are mixed, ions that together form a poorly soluble salt combine into a solid, the precipitate (*neerslag*), e.g. the white precipitate with which chloride is detected: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s). The Binas table of solubilities of salts shows which combinations precipitate. *(CvTE M3.10)*
- The equation contains only the ions that form the solid; the other ions stay dissolved (spectator ions, *tribune-ionen*). Precipitation is used to detect an ion, to remove an unwanted ion and then filter it off ([D01](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D01-chemical-techniques/notes.md)), and to make a poorly soluble salt. *(CvTE M3.10)*
- **Trap:** leave the spectator ions out and write the solid as a ratio formula with (s). A dissolved salt is written as separate ions, Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), not as NaCl(aq).

## Redox reactions and combustion

- Redox reaction (*redoxreactie*): electrons move from a reductor (*reductor*, the electron donor) to an oxidator (*oxidator*, the electron acceptor), and the reaction can be split into two half-reactions. Example: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s), from the half-reactions Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2 e⁻ and Cu²⁺ + 2 e⁻ → Cu. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.11–R1.13)*
- It runs because, in the Binas table of standard electrode potentials, the oxidator Cu²⁺ (V₀ = +0.34 V) has a higher V₀ than the reductor Zn (V₀ of Zn²⁺/Zn = −0.76 V). *(CvTE R1.10)*
- Combustion (*verbranding*): a reaction with oxygen that releases heat (usually with flames) and gives oxides. Complete combustion of a compound of C, H (and O) gives only CO₂ and H₂O: C₂H₅OH(l) + 3 O₂(g) → 2 CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l). With too little oxygen the combustion is incomplete and CO or soot (C) forms as well: 2 CH₄(g) + 3 O₂(g) → 2 CO(g) + 4 H₂O(l). *(CvTE R1.2, R1.3)*
- A fuel only starts burning above its ignition temperature (*ontbrandingstemperatuur*), and a fire needs fuel, oxygen and that temperature at the same time. *(CvTE R5.1)*
- Every combustion is a redox reaction, with O₂ as the oxidator and the fuel as the reductor. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.11)*
- **Trap:** when a question asks for the oxidator in a combustion, the answer is O₂. "Incomplete" doesn't mean "less CO₂": it means that CO or C appears among the products.

## Acid-base reactions

- Acid-base reaction (*zuur-basereactie*): an H⁺ ion (a proton) moves from an acid (the proton donor) to a base (the proton acceptor). No electrons are transferred, so it isn't a redox reaction. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.7)*
- Examples: hydrochloric acid (*zoutzuur*) with caustic soda, H₃O⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → 2 H₂O(l); a weak acid with a strong base, CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l); a carbonate with an acid, CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2 H₃O⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + 3 H₂O(l). *(CvTE R1.5–R1.7)*
- **Trap:** write a weak acid as its molecule (CH₃COOH), because it is mostly not ionised, and leave out spectator ions such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻. Strong and weak acids and buffers are in [C01](../C01-chemical-processes/notes.md) and [C05](../C05-chemical-equilibrium/notes.md).

## Condensation and ester formation

- Condensation reaction (*condensatiereactie*): two molecules join and a small molecule, usually H₂O, is split off; it is the reverse of hydrolysis. Example, two molecules of the amino acid glycine form a dipeptide with a peptide bond: H₂N–CH₂–COOH + H₂N–CH₂–COOH → H₂N–CH₂–CO–NH–CH₂–COOH + H₂O. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.15)*
- Ester formation (*estervorming*, also *verestering*): a carbon acid and an alcohol condense to an ester (CCVX: alkoxy alkanoate, *alkylalkanoaat*) and water. The reaction is slow and reversible, so an acid is added as a catalyst and an equilibrium forms: CH₃–COOH + CH₃–CH₂–OH ⇌ CH₃–COO–CH₂–CH₃ + H₂O (ethanoic acid and ethanol give ethyl ethanoate). *(CvTE R1.3, R1.16)*
- The CvTE also names transesterification (*omestering*): an ester swaps its alcohol part for another alcohol, CH₃–COO–CH₂–CH₃ + CH₃–OH ⇌ CH₃–COO–CH₃ + CH₃–CH₂–OH. Biodiesel is made this way from a fat and methanol. *(CvTE R1.3)*
- **Trap:** a condensation reaction has nothing to do with condensing, the phase change from gas to liquid (*condenseren*). And ester formation is both a condensation reaction and an equilibrium reaction, so it belongs in both classes.

## Polymerisation

- Polymerisation (*polymerisatie*): many small molecules (monomers) bond into very long chains (polymers). *(CvTE R2.1)*
- Addition polymerisation: monomers with a C=C bond add to each other and nothing is split off, e.g. polyethene from ethene, n CH₂=CH₂ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–. It runs by a radical mechanism. *(CvTE R2.1, R2.2)*
- Condensation polymerisation (polycondensation, *polycondensatie*): monomers with two reactive groups each, such as –OH, –COOH and –NH₂, link by condensation, and every new link splits off H₂O. Polyesters, polyamides and proteins form this way. *(CvTE R2.1)*
- Monomers, repeating units and the mechanism are in [D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md).
- **Trap:** an addition polymer made from C=C monomers has only C atoms in its main chain; O or N atoms in the main chain (ester or amide links) show that it is a condensation polymer.

## Reversible and equilibrium reactions

- A reversible reaction (*omkeerbare reactie*) can run in both directions. In a closed system the forward and the reverse reaction go on until their rates are equal: a dynamic equilibrium (*evenwicht*; the reaction is an *evenwichtsreactie*) in which all concentrations stay constant. It is written with a double arrow, as in N₂(g) + 3 H₂(g) ⇌ 2 NH₃(g). *(CvTE R4.1)*
- A reaction that runs until one of the reactants is used up goes to completion (*aflopende reactie*) and is written with a single arrow. *(CvTE R4.1)*
- **Trap:** at equilibrium the reactions haven't stopped, and the concentrations are constant, not equal. A catalyst makes the equilibrium form sooner but doesn't shift it ([C05](../C05-chemical-equilibrium/notes.md)).

## Synthesis

- Synthesis (*synthese*): making a wanted substance from other substances. The simplest case builds a compound from its elements, the reverse of a decomposition: Fe(s) + S(s) → FeS(s). *(SLO C7)*
- A synthesis usually has one or more steps, each of a type in this list. Ethanol, for example, is made by addition of water to ethene, CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O → CH₃–CH₂–OH, and can then be turned into an ester by condensation with a carbon acid. Polymer synthesis is in [D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md). *(CvTE R1.3)*
- **Trap:** "synthesis" names the purpose (making a substance), not what happens to the particles, so each step of a synthesis also has its own type (addition, condensation, substitution and so on).

## Free radical mechanism

- A free radical (*radicaal*) is a particle with an unpaired electron, written with a dot: Cl•, CH₃•. Radicals are very reactive. *(CvTE M1.4, R2.5)*
- In a radical mechanism (*radicaalmechanisme*) the reaction runs as a chain of steps. Example, the substitution CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl in UV light: *(CvTE R2.4, R2.5)*
    - initiation (*initiatie*): light splits chlorine molecules into radicals, Cl₂ → 2 Cl•;
    - propagation (*propagatie*): a radical reacts with a molecule and forms a new radical, Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃• and CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•;
    - termination (*terminatie*): two radicals combine, Cl• + Cl• → Cl₂, CH₃• + Cl• → CH₃Cl or CH₃• + CH₃• → C₂H₆.
- Adding up the two propagation steps gives the overall equation. The Cl• used in the first step is formed again in the second, so one Cl• can make many CH₃Cl molecules. Mechanisms in general are in [C04](../C04-reaction-kinetics/notes.md). *(CvTE R2.4)*
- The same three kinds of step occur in addition polymerisation ([D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md)) and in the breakdown of ozone by CFCs ([C08](../C08-technological-aspects/notes.md)).
- **Trap:** in propagation the number of radicals stays the same; only termination removes them. Put the dot on the atom with the unpaired electron, the C in CH₃•.

## Classifying a reaction

- In the Netherlands the reactions themselves are central-exam material, but classifying them is school-exam material. *(SLO C7)*
- To classify a reaction, ask what happens to the particles:
    - Does one substance split into several (decomposition: thermolysis, photolysis, electrolysis), or do several combine into one (addition, synthesis of a compound from its elements)?
    - Is a particle transferred: electrons (redox, combustion) or H⁺ (acid-base)?
    - Is water taken up (hydrolysis, saponification) or split off (condensation, ester formation, polycondensation)?
    - Is an atom or group swapped (substitution)?
    - Do ions form a solid (precipitation), or does a solid spread into a solvent (dissolution)?
    - Can the reaction run back (reversible, equilibrium), and by which steps does it run (radical mechanism)?
- One reaction often belongs to several classes: a combustion is also a redox reaction, an electrolysis is a decomposition and a redox reaction, ester formation is a condensation and an equilibrium, saponification is a hydrolysis, the chlorination of methane is a substitution with a radical mechanism, and precipitation is the reverse of dissolving a poorly soluble salt.
- **Trap:** when asked to classify a reaction, give the reason in terms of the particles ("an H⁺ ion moves from CH₃COOH to OH⁻, so it is an acid-base reaction"), not just the name.

## CCVX versus the Dutch syllabus

- **In both:** the reactions themselves. The central exam asks candidates to recognise addition, condensation (ester and amide/peptide formation), redox, acid-base, hydrolysis, decomposition (electrolysis, photolysis, thermolysis), substitution and combustion *(CvTE R1.3)*, and it covers equilibria *(CvTE R4.1)*, polymerisation *(CvTE R2.1–R2.3)*, radicals *(CvTE R2.5)*, dissolving *(CvTE M3.9)* and the solubility of salts *(CvTE M3.10)*. Classifying the reactions is in both as well, but in the Netherlands it is school-exam material *(SLO C7)*.
- **Dutch syllabus only:** transesterification (*omestering*), listed with ester formation *(CvTE R1.3)*.
- **CCVX only:** saponification and precipitation as named reaction types. The CvTE list doesn't name them, although saponification is a hydrolysis and precipitation follows from the solubility of salts.
