# E4 — New materials: practice

Six original questions on the E4 learning outcomes, in CCVX style.

### chem-e04-q01 | points 3 | outcome E4 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A display manufacturer puts a nanocoating of indium tin oxide (ITO) on its screens: a transparent, electrically conductive layer a few tens of nanometres thick. (1) Explain what "nano" means in the material sense here. (2) Why is thickness at that scale itself a functional property? (3) Name one application-driven requirement that follows from the chosen material combination.
#### Answer
1. "Nano" refers to a material dimension of tens to hundreds of nanometres (here the coating thickness), where surface effects dominate. 2. At a few nanometres the layer is thin enough that light passes (few atoms to absorb/scatter it) while electrons can still flow in the plane of the film: transparency and conductivity coincide only because the film is nano-thin. 3. The layer must attach firmly to glass/plastic and resist scratching and flaking, so adhesion and mechanical durability drive the coating process as much as conductivity does.
#### Solution
1. Nanotechnology works at 1–100(0) nm, the regime where the material is mostly surface.
2. A bulk ITO sheet would be opaque; only the nanometre-scale thickness makes it a transparent conductor.
3. Practical requirement: the film must survive bending and touch — the choice of material (ITO, not e.g. gold film) follows the required properties package: conductive, transparent, durable, processable.

### chem-e04-q02 | points 3 | outcome E4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Antibacterial door handles are sometimes covered with a silver-ion nanocoating. Explain how the nanoscale structure of the coating relates to its function and its durability, and give one safety/environmental question the hospital should ask the supplier.
#### Answer
A nano-structured silver layer presents a very large effective silver surface, so Ag⁺ ions are released steadily enough to damage bacteria at the surface (microstructure → function), while only being a few nm thick, so it lasts years and wears without bulk change; question: how much free Ag⁺ enters the environment (washing water of the cleaners) and does the biocidal dose continue to work once selective pressure drives resistant bacteria?
#### Solution
1. Silver ions are biocidal; the coating works by constantly supplying them at the surface.
2. Nano-structure: enormous surface-to-volume, so a tiny mass of silver performs antibacterially for years.
3. Responsible procurement asks for the full life picture: emission into waste water, and the resistance question — materials in hospitals carry responsibilities beyond their function (link G02).

### chem-e04-q03 | points 3 | outcome E4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Ceramic hip implants (alumina, Al₂O₃) replace metallic hip implants in some patients. Argue from the lattice type: which property makes ceramics attractive for implants, and which property makes them risky — and name one development that reduces the risk.
#### Answer
Attractive: the ionic/covalent lattice of Al₂O₃ is extremely hard, wear-resistant (hard particles do not loosen and inflame) and it does not release metal ions (inert: no corrosion). Risky: the same lattice type is brittle (no layer sliding; a crack cascades through), so under impact the implant can shatter — see chem-b04-q09. Development: composite ceramics with zirconia particles that toughen (crack tips are arrested), or hip cups with metal backings that carry the load.
#### Solution
1. The lattice argument from B04: ionic/atomic lattices are hard and chemically inert but brittle.
2. Wear particles from metals cause immune responses (corrosion products from body fluids); alumina's inertness avoids that.
3. Materials research answers brittleness with structure: toughening particles, grain size control, composites (metal backing, ceramic sliding surface): new materials are structure engineering (molecular structure aspect of E4).

### chem-e04-q04 | points 4 | outcome E4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
New generations of antibiotics are scarce. Describe with molecular structure reasoning how bacteria defeat a β-lactam antibiotic (like the penicillins), and what a "new application" in pharmaceutical research does about it.
#### Answer
A β-lactam works because its strained four-membered ring is reactive: it binds and blocks the bacterial cell-wall enzymes (transpeptidases) — a precise molecular-shape fit. Bacteria counter it most often with β-lactamase enzymes, which open that ring hydrolytically (a nucleophilic attack on the ring's C=O) and destroy the drug before it reaches its target. Research answers with (a) ring variants less readily hydrolysed (structure tweaks), and (b) β-lactamase inhibitors co-administered that sacrifice themselves to the enzyme — a setup in which molecular structure decides the therapy's fate.
#### Solution
1. Function follows shape: the β-lactam ring fits the bacterial transpeptidase like a key in a lock and clamps it irreversibly.
2. Bacterial resistance = evolution of a counter-key: β-lactamase cuts the ring open.
3. Pharma research then designs around the failure: stabilised rings, inhibitor cocktails — exactly the "pharmaceutical research" line of the E4 outcomes.

### chem-e04-q05 | points 4 | outcome E4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A start-up proposes a "super-hydrophobic nanocoating" for solar panels, so that dust rinses off with rain. (1) Which molecular/surface structure gives the property? (2) Give one benefit for the application in an existing market and one risk to check.
#### Answer
1. A surface with nanoscale roughness plus apolar chemistry (like the lotus leaf): droplets touch only the tips, water cannot wet (hydrogen bridges inside the drop win over any attraction), so drops bead up and roll off, taking dust with them. 2. Benefit: solar panels stay clean — more kWh without washing. Risk: the coating must survive UV and sandstorm abrasion, and its own breakdown products must be harmless — durability and environmental safety of nanomaterials are the open checks.
#### Solution
1. Hydrophobicity = apolar surface (no hydrogen bridges with water) on top of nano-texture (air pockets under drops): the lotus effect.
2. Applied gain: cleaning is the hidden cost of solar farms; a self-cleaning surface pays.
3. New materials bring new questions: weathering of a nano-surface and what it releases — the E4 judgment includes its safety.

### chem-e04-q06 | points 4 | outcome E4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Explain with one existing market (e.g. smartphone touchscreens) and one new market (e.g. implant medicine) how developments in new materials create applications, naming in each case the chemical property the market buys: ITO coatings for screens, and bioinert ceramics in orthopaedics.
#### Answer
Smartphone screen: the market buys an invisible conductor — ITO's combination of electrical conductivity with optical transparency (only possible as a nano-thin film) made the touch layer possible; before ITO scaling, resistive thick films would have made screens dull — new material = new product category. Orthopaedics: the market buys an implant the body tolerates: the bioinert, wear-free ceramic lattice (no corrosion products, hard and smooth articulation), first made durable enough by toughened ceramics — opening joint replacement in younger, more active patients. In both: a molecular/lattice property, engineered at the right scale, becomes a saleable application in an existing or new market.
#### Solution
1. Markets pay for function; function rests on microstructure (the whole E1/E4 point). For touch screens: conducting + transparent (two properties that exclude each other in bulk materials).
2. For orthopaedics: bioinertness + wear resistance.
3. A new application happens when these properties become controllable (nanocoatings for ITO, toughening for ceramics) — the E4 reasoning chain from material properties to market applications.
