## E1.1 Microstructure and macroscopic properties

### How a described study uses the relationship

- An exam question describes a study in health, materials or food production, such as a new implant material, a packaging film or a medicine. It asks you to explain an observed property from the particles, or to choose the structure that gives the wanted property. *(CvTE M5.1)*
- Reason in three steps: the feature at particle level (a group, a bond type, a lattice), the interaction or reaction it causes, and the macroscopic property that follows. When a question asks for a description at micro level (*op microniveau*), the answer must name particles, groups or bonds.
- The CvTE syllabus uses three levels: micro (atoms, groups, bonds, molecules, ions), meso (how particles are organised, such as the crystals in a metal or the fibres in a polymer) and macro (the properties of substances and materials). *(CvTE M6.1)*
- Most of the theory is in [B03](../../B-substances-and-materials/B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md) and [B04](../../B-substances-and-materials/B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md), and polymer synthesis is in [D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md). This page focuses on applying it.

### Mobility of charge carriers and conductivity

- A substance conducts electric current (conductivity, *geleidbaarheid*) only if it contains charged particles that can move (mobility of charge carriers, *beweeglijkheid van ladingsdragers*): free electrons in metals and graphite, or free ions in molten salts and in solutions of salts, acids and bases. *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE M5.1)*
- So a salt conducts as a liquid or in solution but not as a solid, where its ions are fixed in the ion lattice. Molecular substances don't conduct unless they form ions with water, as hydrogen chloride does: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Graphite and diamond are both atomic lattices of carbon, but only graphite conducts. In a graphite layer each C atom forms three atomic bonds (covalent bonds), and its fourth valence electron can move through the layer; in diamond all four valence electrons are in atomic bonds. *(CvTE M3.2)*
- Conductivity also depends on how easily the carriers move. In a metal it drops as the temperature rises, because the more strongly vibrating metal ions hinder the electrons. Foreign atoms in an alloy hinder them too, which is why electrical wiring is made of very pure copper. In a salt solution conductivity rises with temperature, because the ions move faster.
- Application, analysis: H₃O⁺ and OH⁻ move through water much faster than other ions. When hydrochloric acid is titrated with caustic soda, the reaction H₃O⁺ + OH⁻ → 2 H₂O replaces the fast H₃O⁺ ions by slower Na⁺ ions. The conductivity falls until the equivalence point and rises again as excess OH⁻ is added, so the minimum marks the equivalence point.
- Application, materials: plastics normally don't conduct. A polymer with alternating single and double bonds along the chain, such as polyethyne (–CH=CH–)ₙ, conducts once some electrons have been removed or added (doping). Such conducting polymers are used in electronics.
- **Trap:** a solid salt doesn't conduct: the ions are present but can't move. And the free electrons of a metal have nothing to do with a "free electron pair" (a lone pair, below).

### Characteristic groups and reactivity

- **Free electron pair** (*vrij elektronenpaar, niet-bindend elektronenpaar*), drawn as a dash or two dots in a Lewis structure. An atom with a lone pair can bind an electron-poor particle: with H⁺ it acts as a base, with a δ+ carbon atom as a nucleophile (*nucleofiel*). The N atom in ammonia and in amines (alkanoic amines) is the standard example: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, and in the same way CH₃–NH₂ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃–NH₃⁺ + OH⁻. *(CvTE M5.1; CvTE R1.6; CvTE R2.5)*
    - Application, health: many medicines contain an amine group. In the acidic stomach the N atom takes up H⁺, so the molecule becomes charged: it dissolves well in water but crosses cell membranes poorly ([E02](../E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md)).
    - Application, materials: when an amide bond forms, as in nylon and in proteins, the lone pair on N attacks the δ+ C atom of a carboxyl group ([D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md)).
- **Free radical** (*radicaal*): a particle with an unpaired electron, written with a dot (Cl•, HO•, CH₃•). It is very reactive because the unpaired electron tends to form a pair: the radical takes an H atom from another molecule or adds to a C=C bond. That often produces a new radical, so a chain reaction follows. Radicals form when UV light or heat breaks an atomic bond: Cl₂ → 2 Cl•, then Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃• and CH₃• + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•. *(CvTE M5.1; CvTE R2.5)*
    - Application, materials: addition polymerisation starts with a radical from an initiator (initiation, propagation and termination, [D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md)).
    - Application, food and health: oxygen attacks the unsaturated fatty acid chains in oils through a radical chain reaction, which makes the oil rancid; radicals also damage DNA and proteins. Antioxidants such as vitamin E (fat-soluble) and vitamin C (water-soluble) react with radicals and form a much less reactive radical, which stops the chain.
- **Multiple atomic bond** (*meervoudige atoombinding*): a C=C or C≡C bond takes up other atoms in an addition reaction, for example with H₂, Br₂, H₂O or HCl: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂Br–CH₂Br. Bromine water losing its colour shows multiple bonds. Multiple bonds also absorb UV light (below). *(CvTE M5.1; CvTE R1.15)*
    - Application, food: hardening vegetable oil, traditionally done for margarine, adds H₂ to some of the C=C bonds in the fatty acid chains with a nickel catalyst. With fewer double bonds the chains are straighter and pack more closely, so the Van der Waals bonds (molecular bonds) between them are stronger and the melting point rises (fat versus oil, [B04](../../B-substances-and-materials/B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md)). Partial hardening can turn cis double bonds into trans double bonds (trans fats, [E02](../E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md)).
- **Dipole/polar atomic bond** (*dipool, polaire atoombinding*): in a bond between atoms with different electronegativity, the more electronegative atom carries δ− and the other δ+ (C=O, O–H, N–H, and C–Cl, which is polar by electronegativity but not by the CCVX rule that counts only bonds of N, O or F as polar). A δ+ carbon atom attracts nucleophiles, so halogen alkanes, carbon acids (carboxylic acids), esters (alkoxy alkanoates, *alkylalkanoaten*) and amides react there: CH₃–Cl + OH⁻ → CH₃–OH + Cl⁻ is a substitution and CH₃–COO–CH₃ + H₂O → CH₃–COOH + CH₃–OH is the hydrolysis of an ester. Polar bonds can also make the molecule a dipole, and O–H and N–H bonds allow hydrogen bridges (hydrogen bonds), which affects solubility ([B03](../../B-substances-and-materials/B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md)). *(CvTE M5.1; CvTE M3.5; CvTE R2.5)*
    - Application, health and materials: water and enzymes attack the δ+ C atom of ester and amide (peptide) bonds, which is why polyesters and proteins can be broken down (biodegradability, below).
- **Trap:** a nucleophile has a lone pair or a negative charge and attacks a δ+ atom, never a δ− atom. A radical is not an ion: Cl• is neutral with seven valence electrons, while Cl⁻ has a full octet.

### Lattices and deformability

- **Metallic lattices** (*metaalroosters*): metal ions in a "sea" of free electrons. The metallic bond doesn't point in fixed directions, so layers of atoms can slide past each other without the lattice falling apart: metals can be hammered, rolled and drawn into wire (deformability, *vervormbaarheid*). *(CvTE M3.2; CvTE M4.1)*
    - Lattice errors (*roosterfouten*) are places where the regular stacking is disturbed, such as a missing atom or a foreign atom; a piece of metal also consists of many small crystals with boundaries between them. Foreign atoms of a different size and the boundaries between crystals make it harder for layers to slide, so the metal becomes harder and less deformable.
    - Alloys (*legeringen*) use this on purpose: atoms of a second element disturb the lattice, so an alloy is usually harder and less deformable than the pure metal. Examples are bronze (copper with tin) and steel (iron with a little carbon). *(CvTE M5.1; CvTE M4.2)*
    - Influence of temperature: at a higher temperature the metal ions vibrate more strongly and the metal deforms more easily, which is why steel is forged and rolled while hot. Bending or hammering a metal at room temperature makes it harder and more brittle (a wire bent back and forth a few times breaks); heating it and cooling it slowly (annealing) makes it soft again. *(CvTE M5.1)*
    - Application, health: hip-implant stems are often made of a titanium alloy, which is stronger than pure titanium and, like titanium, doesn't corrode in the body.
- **Lattice/structure of polymers** (*rooster/structuur van polymeren*): long chains held together by Van der Waals bonds, sometimes with hydrogen bridges between the chains, as in polyamides, or with atomic bonds between the chains (crosslinks). *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE M5.1)*
    - Chain length (*ketenlengte*): longer chains touch each other over a larger length and get more entangled, so the total Van der Waals attraction is larger: the material is stronger and softens at a higher temperature. Polyethene with extremely long chains is used for the wear surfaces of hip and knee implants and, as aligned fibres (Dyneema), for ropes. *(CvTE R2.3)*
    - Types of monomers (*soorten monomeren*): the groups in the monomers decide which bonds form between the chains. Polar groups give dipole–dipole bonds (C–Cl in PVC, polar by electronegativity) or hydrogen bridges (the N–H and C=O of the amide groups in nylon), which make the material stiffer and raise the softening temperature compared with polyethene. A monomer with three reactive groups, such as propane-1,2,3-triol in a polyester, or with two C=C bonds, forms crosslinks. *(CvTE R2.1)*
    - Crosslinks: thermoplastics (*thermoplasten*) have no crosslinks; on heating the chains slide apart, so the material softens and can be remoulded and recycled. Thermosets (*thermoharders*) are networks with many crosslinks: they stay hard when heated and decompose instead of melting, like epoxy glue and Bakelite. A few crosslinks give an elastic rubber: vulcanised rubber has sulfur bridges between its chains. *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE R2.3)*
    - Plasticizers (*weekmakers*): small molecules mixed in between the chains. They push the chains apart and weaken the bonds between them, so the chains slide more easily and the material becomes soft and flexible. Rigid PVC (drainpipes) contains none; soft PVC (garden hoses, medical tubing, blood bags) contains a lot. Plasticizers aren't bound to the chains, so they can slowly leak out, and some phthalate plasticizers are restricted in toys because of health concerns. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- **Crystal structure of ceramic materials** (*kristalstructuur van keramische materialen*): ceramics are inorganic, non-metallic solids, made by shaping a powder and heating it below its melting point (sintering). In an ion lattice (Al₂O₃, ZrO₂) or an atomic lattice (SiC, Si₃N₄) every particle is held by strong bonds in all directions, so ceramics are hard, heat-resistant and usually electrical insulators. They are also brittle: in an ion lattice, shifting a layer brings ions of the same charge next to each other, which repel; in an atomic lattice, atomic bonds would have to break. So a ceramic cracks instead of bending. *(CvTE M3.2; CvTE M4.2)*
    - Application, health: hip-implant heads of Al₂O₃ or ZrO₂ and zirconia dental crowns are hard, wear very little and don't react in the body. More in [E04](../E04-new-materials/notes.md).
- **Trap:** when a thermoplastic softens, the Van der Waals bonds between the chains break, not the atomic bonds in the chains. Crosslinks are atomic bonds, not hydrogen bridges. A plasticizer is a separate substance mixed in, not a group on the chain.

### Multiple bonds and UV-light sensitivity

- UV-light sensitivity (*uv-lichtgevoeligheid*): UV light carries enough energy to break atomic bonds. Molecules absorb UV mainly at multiple bonds (C=C, C=O), most strongly at alternating (conjugated) double bonds and benzene rings. The absorbed energy can split a bond into radicals. In a polymer this breaks chains, so the material becomes brittle and discoloured, or it forms new crosslinks, so the material becomes hard and brittle. *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE M5.1)*
- Application, materials: natural rubber has a C=C bond in every repeating unit, and sunlight and ozone make it hard and cracked; car tyres contain carbon black, which absorbs the UV. Polymers with only C–C and C–H bonds absorb little UV, but traces of C=O or C=C groups still make them degrade slowly outdoors, so UV stabilisers are added.
- Application, materials: UV curing uses the effect on purpose. A coating, ink or gel nail polish made of monomers with C=C bonds and a photo-initiator turns into a crosslinked solid within seconds under a UV lamp. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Application, health: sunscreens contain molecules with conjugated double bonds and benzene rings that absorb UV and release the energy as heat. UV-B that does reach the skin can join two neighbouring thymine bases in DNA through their C=C bonds, which can lead to mutations.
- Application, food: unsaturated oils go rancid faster in light, so they are best kept in dark bottles.
- **Trap:** UV sensitivity comes from light absorbed by multiple bonds and the radicals that follow, not from heat. Crosslinking by UV can be the goal (curing) or the damage (embrittlement).

### Types of metal atoms and corrosion susceptibility

- How easily a metal corrodes (corrosion susceptibility, *corrosiegevoeligheid*) depends on whether a protective layer forms and on how noble the metal is.
- **Bound metal-oxide layer** (*gebonden metaaloxide laagje*): some metals form a thin, dense oxide layer that sticks to the metal and keeps oxygen and water out. Examples are aluminium (4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃), chromium in stainless steel (Cr₂O₃), titanium (TiO₂) and zinc. Rust on iron is porous and flakes off, so the iron underneath keeps corroding. *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE M5.1)*
    - Application: anodising thickens the Al₂O₃ layer by making aluminium the positive electrode in an electrolysis. Titanium implants and stainless-steel surgical instruments owe their corrosion resistance to their oxide layer. The layer on aluminium dissolves in strongly acidic and strongly basic solutions, after which the metal itself reacts.
- **Standard electrode potential** (*standaardelektrodepotentiaal*) and nobility (*edelheid*): the lower V⁰ of the couple Mⁿ⁺/M, the stronger the metal is as a reductor and the more easily it corrodes; such a metal is less noble (*onedel*). From the Binas table of standard electrode potentials: Mg²⁺/Mg −2.37 V, Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76 V, Fe²⁺/Fe −0.44 V, Pb²⁺/Pb −0.13 V, Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34 V, Ag⁺/Ag +0.80 V. *(CvTE R1.10; CvTE M4.1)*
    - A reaction can take place when V⁰ of the oxidator is higher than V⁰ of the reductor. Metals with V⁰ below 0.00 V (H⁺/H₂), such as magnesium, zinc and iron, can react with acids, for example Zn + 2 H⁺ → Zn²⁺ + H₂; copper and silver can't (they do react with nitric acid, but there the nitrate ion is the oxidator, not H⁺). *(CvTE R1.10; CvTE R1.13)*
    - Rusting: oxygen dissolved in water (O₂ + 2 H₂O/OH⁻, +0.40 V) oxidises iron (−0.44 V). The half-reactions Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2 e⁻ and O₂ + 2 H₂O + 4 e⁻ → 4 OH⁻ add up to 2 Fe + O₂ + 2 H₂O → 2 Fe²⁺ + 4 OH⁻. The Fe²⁺ is oxidised further to rust, which is iron(III) oxide with water.
    - Application: zinc (−0.76 V) on galvanised steel or in blocks on a ship's hull is a stronger reductor than iron (−0.44 V), so the zinc corrodes instead of the iron, even where the zinc layer is scratched. The other way round, iron in contact with copper (+0.34 V) corrodes faster than iron alone.
- **Trap:** aluminium is far less noble than iron (look up its V⁰ in Binas), yet it hardly corrodes, because of its bound oxide layer. So the electrode potential alone doesn't predict corrosion in practice.

### Molecular structure and solubility

- Solubility (*oplosbaarheid*) in water follows from the characteristic groups. Hydrophilic (*hydrofiel*) groups, such as OH, NH₂, COOH and C=O, and charged groups such as COO⁻ and NH₃⁺, form hydrogen bridges or ion–dipole bonds with water. Hydrophobic (*hydrofoob*) parts, such as hydrocarbon chains and benzene rings, form only Van der Waals bonds. *(CvTE M3.6; CvTE M3.7; CvTE M5.1)*
- The balance between the two decides. Methanol, ethanol and propan-1-ol mix with water in all proportions, butan-1-ol only partly, and alcohols with longer chains hardly at all. Glucose, with five OH groups, dissolves very well; fats don't dissolve in water.
- Application, health: vitamin C, with many OH groups, is water-soluble, and an excess leaves the body in urine. Vitamins A, D, E and K are mostly hydrocarbon, dissolve in fat and are stored in fatty tissue. Medicines with an amine group are often sold as a salt (a hydrochloride), because the charged form dissolves much better in water.
- Application, food: an emulsifier such as lecithin in mayonnaise has a hydrophilic and a hydrophobic part and keeps oil droplets spread through the water. *(CvTE M2.3)*
- **Trap:** one OH group doesn't make a molecule water-soluble: compare the size of the hydrophobic part with the number of hydrophilic groups. And being polar isn't enough: dissolving in water needs hydrogen bridges or ion–dipole bonds with water.

### Molecular structure and water repellence (water-binding)

- A surface that exposes only C–H or C–F bonds binds water very weakly: it has no O–H or N–H groups, and the F atoms of C–F bonds attract water molecules only weakly, so water forms drops that roll off (water repellence, *waterafstotendheid*): polyethene, wax, silicone coatings and PTFE ("Teflon", –(CF₂–CF₂)ₙ–). A material with many hydrophilic groups binds water (water-binding capacity, *waterbindend vermogen*): cotton, which is cellulose with many OH groups, absorbs water. *(CvTE M3.8; CvTE M4.2; CvTE M5.1)*
- Superabsorbent polymers (SAP) in nappies are crosslinked sodium polypropenoate (sodium polyacrylate), with a COO⁻ Na⁺ group on every second C atom of the chain: –CH₂–CH(COO⁻ Na⁺)–. Water forms ion–dipole bonds with the Na⁺ and COO⁻ ions and hydrogen bridges with the COO⁻ groups. The negative COO⁻ groups repel each other, so the network stretches out and the gel holds up to hundreds of times its own mass of pure water, and much less of urine, which contains salts. The crosslinks stop the chains from dissolving.
- Application: a nappy combines both properties, with a polyethene back sheet that keeps the liquid in and a SAP core that binds it. Soft contact lenses are hydrogels: crosslinked polymers with OH groups that hold water.
- **Trap:** a superabsorbent is very hydrophilic but doesn't dissolve, because of its crosslinks; without them the same chains would dissolve.

### Molecular structure and biodegradability of polymers

- A biodegradable polymer (biodegradability, *biodegradeerbaarheid*) is one that micro-organisms break down with enzymes that hydrolyse bonds in the main chain, and the fragments end up as CO₂ and H₂O. That requires hydrolysable bonds: ester bonds (polyesters such as PLA), amide bonds (polyamides, proteins) or the bonds between the sugar units in starch and cellulose. Polyalkenes such as polyethene, polypropene and polystyrene have only C–C and C–H bonds in the chain and persist for a very long time. *(CvTE M5.1; CvTE R1.3)*
- PLA (polylactic acid, *polymelkzuur*) is a polyester of lactic acid (2-hydroxypropanoic acid), which is made by fermenting sugar or starch. Each lactic acid molecule has an OH and a COOH group, so the chain is what esterification (condensation) of these molecules gives: 2 CH₃–CH(OH)–COOH → CH₃–CH(OH)–CO–O–CH(CH₃)–COOH + H₂O. Hydrolysis is the reverse reaction, so water and enzymes can break the chain again. *(CvTE R1.3; CvTE R2.1)*
- Application, health: dissolving stitches are polyesters of lactic acid and hydroxyethanoic acid (glycolic acid). They hydrolyse in the body, so they don't have to be removed.
- Application, food: PLA cups and packaging break down in an industrial composting plant, which is warm and moist, but only slowly in soil or seawater.
- **Trap:** bio-based isn't the same as biodegradable: polyethene made from sugarcane ethanol is still polyethene and doesn't degrade. A hydrolysable bond is necessary but not enough: PET is a polyester but hardly degrades in nature, because its stiff, partly crystalline, hydrophobic chains keep water and enzymes away from the ester bonds.
