# E1 — Chemical research: practice

Eight original questions on the E1 learning outcome: reasoning from microstructure to macroscopic properties inside a described study (health, materials, food production).

### chem-e01-q01 | points 3 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
In a study on "plastic electronics", a film of polyacetylene (a chain with alternating single and double C–C bonds) is found to conduct electricity, while polyethene film does not. Explain this difference using the bonding structure of the two polymers.
#### Answer
The alternating double bonds of polyacetylene let electrons delocalise along the chain, so charge carriers become mobile; polyethene has only single C–C bonds with all electrons fixed in shared pairs, so nothing can move and the film insulates.
#### Solution
1. Conduction needs mobile charge carriers (see B04). A chain of alternating single and double bonds is conjugated: the electron clouds of the double bonds overlap along the chain, and electrons can travel its length.
2. Polyethene: only σ bonds and localised electron pairs — no delocalised electrons, no conduction; insulator.
3. Microstructure (multiple atomic bonds in conjugation) explains the macroscopic property (conductivity) — the exact link E1 asks you to make in a described study.

### chem-e01-q02 | points 3 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Many drugs are amines that are sold as their "hydrochloride salts". In a study, the free-amine form of a drug barely dissolves in water, but the hydrochloride form dissolves well. Explain this with the structure of the amine and what happens in hydrochloric acid.
#### Answer
The amine group has a free electron pair on N, so in acid the molecule takes up H⁺: R–NH₂ + H₃O⁺ → R–NH₃⁺ + H₂O. The resulting ion is much better hydrated (ion-dipole bonds) than the neutral molecule, so the salt dissolves well.
#### Solution
1. A free electron pair makes the amine a base (proton acceptor, see C01/G01 chemistry): in the strongly acidic stomach fluid the amine is protonated to R–NH₃⁺.
2. An ion forms strong ion-dipole bonds with water, whereas the neutral amine is mostly apolar: the salt is far more soluble.
3. That is why drug companies sell amine drugs as salts: better solubility, better uptake — microstructure (free electron pair) to macro property (solubility/dissolution rate).

### chem-e01-q03 | points 3 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A hospital wants implants that stay inside the body for decades. The study compares stainless steel with pure iron and picks stainless steel. Use the nobility of metals and protective oxide layers to explain this choice.
#### Answer
Stainless steel contains chromium, which covers the surface with a dense, self-healing oxide layer (a bound metal oxide layer) that seals the metal from water and oxygen, so corrosion is stopped; pure iron rusts, because its oxide is porous and falls off, exposing fresh metal.
#### Solution
1. Corrosion = oxidation of the metal by H₂O/O₂; its tendency follows from the standard electrode potentials (the nobility of the metals, Binas).
2. Chromium (in stainless steel) is not noble — but it forms an extremely adherent Cr₂O₃ layer: a passivating protective layer that repairs itself when scratched.
3. For a long-lived implant, the bound oxide layer beats the pure metal: macro property (no corrosion in the body) from microstructure (the surface layer) — with Al₂O₃ on aluminium the analogue in B04.

### chem-e01-q04 | points 3 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A herbicide must reach the leaf's inner tissue through the waxy, apolar leaf skin, but be sprayed from a water tank. In the study, the active molecule carries a long apolar chain plus a salt-like –COO⁻Na⁺ group. Explain how exactly this structure lets it do both jobs.
#### Answer
The apolar chain is hydrophobic and dissolves into/pass through the waxy leaf skin (Van der Waals bonds with the wax); the ionic –COO⁻ group is hydrophilic and keeps the molecule soluble in the spray water (ion-dipole bonds) — one molecule can move through both worlds.
#### Solution
1. The waxy cuticle of a leaf is apolar: a purely polar molecule does not penetrate it (hydrophilic = sticks to water instead).
2. A purely apolar molecule penetrates the wax but cannot be delivered from water.
3. The dual-functional group design — a hydrophilic/ionic part and an apolar part — gives amphiphilic behaviour (like an emulsifier), solving both steps: dissolve in the spray, penetrate the leaf.

### chem-e01-q05 | points 4 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A research team makes cotton rain jackets: untreated cotton gets wet, treated cotton repels water. The treatment coats the fibres with a fluorinated polymer. Explain the difference at the molecular level (cotton's OH groups vs the coating's C–F surface), using hydrophilic/hydrophobic and the hydrogen bridges of water.
#### Answer
Cotton's cellulose is covered in –OH groups: they make hydrogen bridges with water, so the fabric absorbs water (hydrophilic). The fluoropolymer surface is extremely apolar: water cannot make hydrogen bridges with it, and the drops only touch their own hydrogen-bridged network — they ball up and roll off (hydrophobic/water repellent).
#### Solution
1. Cellulose chains bear many –OH groups; the δ+ of water's H atoms and the δ− of the O atoms form hydrogen bridges to them: the water binds and the fibre wets.
2. The C–F bonds of the coating are polar in the bond but the surface presents hardly any H-bonding partner; water molecules prefer their own hydrogen-bridge network and bead up.
3. Requirement E1: from characteristic groups (–OH vs C–F) to water repellence/wetting — and a common reason rain gear uses fluorinated or waxy coatings.

### chem-e01-q06 | points 4 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Two carrier bags are compared in a composting study: one of polyethene (PE), one of polylactic acid (PLA, a polyester). After 90 days under industrial compost conditions the PLA bag has fallen apart and mineralised, the PE bag is intact. Explain this from the molecular structure, and state which group of micro-organism enzymes does the job.
#### Answer
The PLA backbone consists of ester links (–COO–), which are hydrolysed to acid + alcohol groups by the esterases of microbes (and by hot moist compost); the PE backbone is a pure C–C chain with no attackable functional group, so it cannot be digested.
#### Solution
1. Microbial degradation starts where enzymes can bind and react: ester, amide or glycosidic links are targets; plain hydrocarbon chains are not.
2. In PLA the repeating unit contains an ester group: enzymes (esterases) hydrolyse –COO– + H₂O → –COOH + HO– (see C01), shortening the chains until the fragments can be metabolised to CO₂ and water — biodegradability by structure.
3. In PE there is no such group along the C–C backbone; UV and heat can crack it slowly, but microbes cannot digest it: the bag stays intact.
4. The lesson of the study: "biodegradable" follows from characteristic groups in the structure, not from the word "plant-based".

### chem-e01-q07 | points 3 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
In a materials study, fibre made of ultra-high-molar-mass polyethylene (very long chains) is far stronger than the same volume of low-molar-mass PE. Explain this with the chains.
#### Answer
Longer chains have far more Van der Waals contacts with neighbouring chains and they entangle more, so pulling the fibre apart means freeing very many chains from each other (or breaking atomic bonds): higher strength per volume.
#### Solution
1. The strength of a polymer is set by how much holds the chains together: chain length, crosslinks, plasticizers (see the B04/E1 list).
2. Long chains present a large mutual contact area, so the cumulative Van der Waals attraction between chains is large; entanglement adds mechanical locking.
3. Short chains slide apart already at low load. This is the exact reason polymer engineers steer chain length via initiator amount (see D03): microstructure to macro property.

### chem-e01-q08 | points 3 | outcome E1.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A study finds that coloured plastics degrade quicker in sunlight than white ones, and that the degradation starts in the coloured molecules with double bonds. Explain why molecules with more multiple bonds are more UV-sensitive, and what the "white" pigment does instead.
#### Answer
Multiple (double) bonds absorb UV light; the absorbed energy can break bonds and start radical decomposition, so plastics with many/conjugated double bonds are more UV-sensitive. White pigment reflects/scatters the light before much of it is absorbed.
#### Solution
1. UV-photons carry enough energy to break atomic bonds or excite electrons; single σ bonds absorb little of it, but double bonds — especially conjugated systems, which also give colour — absorb strongly.
2. Where absorption happens, bonds break and free radicals form, starting chain decomposition (compare C04's radical mechanisms): embrittlement and crumbling of the plastic.
3. White pigments like TiO₂ particles scatter the light (reflection) instead of absorbing it: less photochemistry, slower ageing.
