# E2 — Selectivity and specificity: practice

Eight original questions on the E2 learning outcomes, in CCVX style.

### chem-e02-q01 | points 3 | outcome E2.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Draw/describe the cis- and trans-isomers of but-2-ene, and explain why these are different substances while rotating a single bond never creates an isomer of ethene.
#### Answer
In cis-but-2-ene the two CH₃ groups sit on the same side of the double bond, in trans-but-2-ene on opposite sides. Around a C=C double bond no free rotation is possible, so the two forms cannot merge into each other and are distinct substances (cis/trans isomerism); around a single C–C bond, rotation interconverts the forms, so they are not isomers.
#### Solution
1. But-2-ene: CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃. With the two CH₃ groups on the same side: cis-but-2-ene; on opposite sides: trans-but-2-ene.
2. The double bond is rigid: the second bond of the double bond prevents twisting, so the cis and the trans arrangement are fixed — and they differ in properties (different boiling points, polarity).
3. Around a single bond, groups rotate freely: the "arrangements" are conformations, not separate substances.

### chem-e02-q02 | points 3 | outcome E2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Lactic acid has the structural formula CH₃–CH(OH)–COOH. (1) Mark the asymmetric carbon atom and name the four different groups attached to it. (2) What special property do molecules with one asymmetric carbon atom have?
#### Answer
1. The central C is asymmetric: the four groups are CH₃, H, OH and COOH. 2. It exists as two mirror-image forms (enantiomers, mirror-image isomerism), which cannot be superimposed and which bend polarised light in opposite directions (optical activity).
#### Solution
1. An asymmetric carbon atom carries four different groups — here –CH₃, –H, –OH and –COOH (the two "sides" of the original chain are not the same: CH₃ ≠ COOH).
2. Like left and right hands, the two arrangements of the four groups around this C cannot be rotated onto each other: two enantiomers.
3. Enantiomers have the same melting point, density and most chemistry, but they rotate the plane of polarised light in opposite ways and can act very differently in the body (enzymes are themselves chiral).

### chem-e02-q03 | points 3 | outcome E2.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
How many asymmetric carbon atoms does each of these amino acids have? Alanine: CH₃–CH(NH₂)–COOH. Glycine: H₂N–CH₂–COOH. Threonine: CH₃–CH(OH)–CH(NH₂)–COOH.
#### Answer
Alanine: 1; glycine: 0; threonine: 2.
#### Solution
1. Alanine's middle C bears CH₃ / H / NH₂ / COOH — four different groups: one asymmetric C.
2. Glycine's CH₂ bears two H atoms: not asymmetric, so zero.
3. Threonine has two C atoms with four different groups each (the CH(OH) carbon and the CH(NH₂) carbon): two asymmetric carbons, so four stereoisomers are possible.
4. Rule: an asymmetric C needs four different attached groups; an H twice or a CH₃ twice kills asymmetry.

### chem-e02-q04 | points 4 | outcome E2.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
An enzyme solution converts substrate S to product P. When S is added at increasing concentrations, the conversion rate first rises proportionally and then levels off to a maximum. Explain this curve with the enzyme-substrate model (E + S ⇌ ES → E + P).
#### Answer
At low [S], free enzyme is abundant: every extra S quickly finds an enzyme, so the rate rises with [S]. As [S] grows, more and more enzyme sits occupied in ES-complexes; at saturation every enzyme molecule works at full speed, so the rate flattens to a maximum (the turnover speed of the enzymes) — extra S just waits.
#### Solution
1. The enzyme E binds S into the enzyme-substrate complex ES; the reaction happens in the complex and releases P, returning E.
2. Rate needs ES; at small [S], [ES] grows with [S] (more collisions): first-order-looking rise.
3. When [ES] reaches the total amount of enzyme, every enzyme is busy: [ES] can grow no more — the rate plateaus at the maximum set by the enzyme amount and the turnover number; raising [S] no longer helps.

### chem-e02-q05 | points 3 | outcome E2.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Urease converts urea but leaves the very similar molecule biurette untouched. Explain this specificity with the active site of the enzyme.
#### Answer
The active site is a pocket whose spatial shape and functional groups fit urea exactly: biurette has a different shape and different charge distribution, so it cannot bind into the ES-complex and is not converted.
#### Solution
1. An enzyme binds its substrate in the active position (active site), where precisely placed functional groups hold the substrate (hydrogen bridges, ion-dipole bonds) — spatial complement.
2. Biurette misses that complement, so no ES-complex, no reaction: specificity.
3. Specificity is why enzymes catalyse single reactions at body temperature, and why a tiny change in a drug molecule can remove or create an effect — molecules are read by shape.

### chem-e02-q06 | points 3 | outcome E2.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Pepsin (in the stomach, pH ≈ 2) and trypsin (small intestine, pH ≈ 8) have very different pH optima, and both stop working when the pH moves far from the optimum. Explain the bell shape of the rate-versus-pH curve with the enzyme's structure.
#### Answer
The active site keeps its working shape only near one pH: the protonation state of its own acidic and basic groups (–COOH/–COO⁻, –NH₂/–NH₃⁺) depends on the pH, and with it the shape and the binding of the substrate. Far from the optimum the structure denatures or the substrate no longer fits, so the rate falls off on both sides — a bell curve.
#### Solution
1. An enzyme is a protein; its folding is held by hydrogen bridges and ionic interactions between its own groups, which are pH-sensitive.
2. Raising or lowering pH shifts the balance H₃O⁺/OH⁻, changing which groups carry charge: the active site deforms and substrate binding fails (at extremes the whole protein denatures).
3. Every enzyme therefore has a pH optimum: pepsin at the stomach's acid, trypsin at the intestine's mild base — matching the workplace.

### chem-e02-q07 | points 3 | outcome E2.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Amylase converts starch fastest at about 37 °C; at 5 °C it is slow, at 90 °C it has stopped. Explain both sides of the temperature optimum with the particle collision model and with the protein structure.
#### Answer
Between 5 and 37 °C the rate rises, because warmer particles collide more often and more energetically (more effective collisions). Above the optimum the protein denatures — its hydrogen bridges break and the active site unfolds — so the rate collapses; at 90 °C the active site is ruined (usually irreversibly), and the rate is zero.
#### Solution
1. Ordinary kinetics (C04): rising temperature speeds up every step, including the enzyme's conversion.
2. But the enzyme depends on its delicate 3D structure, held mainly by hydrogen bridges (see B04 on protein structures); too much thermal motion breaks these bridges: denaturation.
3. The result is a peak at the body's temperature for human enzymes; cooling slows reversibly, overheating kills — the fridges of chem-c04-q03 and the sterilising autoclave of surgery are the same idea.

### chem-e02-q08 | points 4 | outcome E2.4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A new painkiller must reach the brain. Its molecule is a base, R–NH₂. The project reports: the free base passes the blood-brain barrier better than its salt, while the salt is better for injection in water. Explain both observations with (1) the structure of the cell membranes and (2) the pH in the body.
#### Answer
1. Cell membranes are phospholipid bilayers with a hydrophobic core: apolar (uncharged) molecules pass through them; the charged R–NH₃⁺ ion does not. 2. In stomach acid (pH ≈ 2) the base is protonated to R–NH₃⁺ (good solubility, no membrane passage), in blood (pH 7.4) part of it is free (R–NH₂, crossing membranes).
#### Solution
1. The double layer of phospholipids lets fat-soluble, uncharged molecules through and keeps ions out: the brain's tissue is shielded by especially tight membranes (the blood-brain barrier).
2. The free base R–NH₂ is uncharged (mostly unbranched/uncharged at body pH), so it crosses; the hydrochloride form R–NH₃⁺Cl⁻ is ionic and stays in the water phase.
3. The pH sets the ratio: base + H₃O⁺ ⇌ acid form + H₂O drives the amine into its ionic form at low pH and back partly at pH 7.4 — so injection salt form dissolves well, while in the blood the neutral fraction crosses into the brain; this interplay of pH and hydrophobicity is exactly what E2.4 names.
