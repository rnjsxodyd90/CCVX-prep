## E2.1 Stereoisomerism

- Stereoisomers (*stereo-isomeren*) have the same molecular formula and the same sequence of bonded atoms, but a different arrangement in space. Structural isomers differ in the sequence of the atoms ([B01](../../B-substances-and-materials/B01-particle-models/notes.md)). The outcome asks you to link this spatial structure to the properties of a substance. *(CvTE M1.18)*

### Cis/trans isomerism

- Rotation around a C=C double bond is blocked. If each of the two C atoms of the double bond carries two different groups, there are two isomers (cis/trans isomerism, *cis/trans isomerie*): cis, with the matching groups on the same side of the double bond, and trans, with them on opposite sides. A ring also blocks rotation, so ring compounds can show cis/trans isomerism too. *(CvTE M1.18)*
- Example: but-2-ene, CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃, exists as cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene. But-1-ene, CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃, has no cis/trans isomers, because one C atom of its double bond carries two H atoms.
- Draw the isomers with the bond angles of about 120° around the double bond (steric number 3), so that the difference shows; in a condensed formula they look the same.
- The isomers are different substances: cis- and trans-butenedioic acid, HOOC–CH=CH–COOH, differ a lot in melting point and solubility in water.
- Application, food: natural unsaturated fatty acids are almost all cis. The cis double bond puts a kink in the chain, so the chains pack loosely and the fat is an oil at room temperature. Trans fatty acids, formed when oil is partly hardened, have straighter chains; they raise the risk of cardiovascular disease, and their amount in food is now strictly limited.
- Application, health: in the eye, light turns a cis double bond in retinal into a trans double bond. The change in shape of the molecule starts the nerve signal.
- **Trap:** check both C atoms of the double bond. If either of them carries two identical groups (two H atoms, two CH₃ groups), there is no cis/trans isomerism.

### Mirror-image isomerism: the asymmetric carbon atom

- A C atom with four different groups is an asymmetric carbon atom (*asymmetrisch koolstofatoom*, often marked C*). The molecule can't be superimposed on its mirror image, just as a left hand can't be superimposed on a right hand, so there are two mirror-image isomers: enantiomers (*enantiomeren, spiegelbeeldisomeren*). Swapping any two groups on C* gives the other enantiomer. *(CvTE M1.18)*
- Example: lactic acid, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid, CH₃–CH(OH)–COOH. Its C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and COOH, so it is asymmetric and lactic acid has two enantiomers; muscles make only one of them.
- Example: alanine, 2-aminopropanoic acid, CH₃–CH(NH₂)–COOH, also has an asymmetric C2. Proteins are built from only one enantiomer of each amino acid, the L-form. Glycine, aminoethanoic acid, H₂N–CH₂–COOH, has two H atoms on C2 and no asymmetric C atom.
- Enantiomers have the same melting point, boiling point and solubility. They differ in the direction in which they rotate polarised light (optical activity, [D04](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D04-molecular-modelling/notes.md)) and in how they interact with other asymmetric molecules, such as enzymes and receptors, which are built from L-amino acids. So the two enantiomers of carvone smell different, one of spearmint and the other of caraway, and the two enantiomers of a medicine can have different effects ([E04](../E04-new-materials/notes.md)).
- Counting: a molecule with n asymmetric C atoms has at most 2ⁿ stereoisomers. Glucose in its open-chain form has four asymmetric C atoms (C2 to C5), so its structural formula stands for 2⁴ = 16 stereoisomers. A symmetric molecule can have fewer: 2,3-dihydroxybutanedioic acid (tartaric acid) has two asymmetric C atoms but three stereoisomers instead of four. A double bond with cis/trans isomerism doubles the number as well: pent-3-en-2-ol, CH₃–CH(OH)–CH=CH–CH₃, has one asymmetric C atom and one such double bond, so 2 × 2 = 4 stereoisomers.
- **Trap:** compare the whole groups, not only the first atom of each. In butan-2-ol, CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₂–CH₃, C2 carries CH₃ and C₂H₅, which are different, so C2 is asymmetric. In pentan-3-ol, CH₃–CH₂–CH(OH)–CH₂–CH₃, C3 carries two C₂H₅ groups, so it isn't. A C atom in a double bond, or with two H atoms, is never asymmetric.

## E2.2 Enzyme kinetics

- Enzymes are proteins that act as biocatalysts (*biokatalysatoren*): they offer a reaction path with a lower activation energy, so the reaction runs fast at body temperature, and they come out of the reaction unchanged. They don't change the heat of reaction ΔE or the position of an equilibrium; the equilibrium only sets in faster. *(CvTE L2.1; CvTE R3.2; CvTE R4.3)*
- The mechanism has two steps. The substrate binds in the active position (active site, *actieve plaats*) and forms the enzyme–substrate complex (*enzym-substraatcomplex*). The reaction takes place in the complex, the product splits off (*afsplitsing van een product*), and the free enzyme takes up the next substrate molecule. In short: E + S ⇌ ES → E + P. *(CvTE L2.1)*
- Qualitative kinetics: at a low substrate concentration most active positions are free, and the rate rises almost in proportion to the substrate concentration. At a high concentration every active position is occupied nearly all the time: the enzyme is saturated, the rate reaches a maximum, and adding substrate no longer helps. Then only more enzyme, or a higher temperature up to the optimum, raises the rate. *(CvTE L2.1)*
- Example: lactase hydrolyses lactose (milk sugar) to glucose and galactose: C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆. People who make too little lactase can't digest lactose; adding lactase to milk gives lactose-free milk. Catalase, found in the liver and most other tissues, breaks down hydrogen peroxide: 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂.
- In an energy diagram, the enzyme-catalysed path has a lower activation energy than the uncatalysed reaction, with the same starting and final energy levels ([C10](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C10-activation-energy/notes.md), [C04](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C04-reaction-kinetics/notes.md)). *(CvTE R5.1; CvTE R5.2)*
- **Trap:** at saturation the rate no longer depends on the substrate concentration, so "more collisions" is the wrong explanation there. An enzyme isn't used up in the reaction, but it can be denatured (E2.3), which is something else.

## E2.3 Specificity and selectivity of enzymes

- The active position is a pocket or groove in the folded protein. The side groups of the amino acids around it hold the substrate with hydrogen bridges (hydrogen bonds), ion bonds between charged groups (–COO⁻ and –NH₃⁺) and Van der Waals bonds (molecular bonds). Only a molecule with the right shape, and the right groups in the right places, fits and binds. This is the lock-and-key idea; in reality the active position changes shape a little when the substrate binds (induced fit). *(CvTE L2.2; CvTE L1.3)*
- Specificity (*specificiteit*): an enzyme accepts only one substrate, or a small group of similar substrates, and catalyses one type of reaction. Lactase hydrolyses lactose but not sucrose, which needs another enzyme. *(CvTE L2.2)*
- Selectivity (*selectiviteit*): of all the products that could form, the enzyme makes one. The active position is itself asymmetric, because the enzyme is built from L-amino acids, so an enzyme usually binds and converts only one of two enantiomers and forms a single enantiomer as its product. Textbooks don't always separate the two terms sharply; in an answer, explain with the shape and the functional groups. *(CvTE L2.2)*
- pH optimum (*pH-optimum*): acidic and basic side groups change their charge with the pH: a carboxyl group is –COOH at low pH and –COO⁻ at high pH, an amino group –NH₃⁺ at low pH and –NH₂ at high pH. Away from the optimum the charges in the active position no longer match the substrate, and the ion bonds that hold the folded shape change, so the enzyme works more slowly; far from the optimum it denatures. Each enzyme's optimum suits the place where it works: pepsin in the stomach about pH 2, trypsin in the small intestine about pH 8, amylase in saliva about pH 7. *(CvTE L2.2)*
- Temperature optimum (*temperatuuroptimum*): heating speeds up the reaction, because the particles collide more often and with more energy. Above a certain temperature, however, the hydrogen bridges, ion bonds and Van der Waals bonds that hold the folded shape break: the enzyme denatures (*denaturatie*), the active position loses its shape, and the rate falls steeply. Human enzymes work best around body temperature (37 °C); enzymes from bacteria living in hot springs work at about 70 °C and are used to copy DNA (PCR). *(CvTE L2.2)*
- Denaturation changes the secondary and tertiary structure, while the peptide bonds, and so the primary structure, stay intact ([G01](../../G-society-chemistry-and-technology/G01-chemistry-of-life/notes.md)). Denaturation by heat is usually irreversible, as in a boiled egg. At a low temperature an enzyme is slow but not denatured, which is why cooling slows down food spoilage. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- Applications: rennet (chymosin) curdles milk in cheese making; amylases turn starch into sugars in brewing; biological washing powders contain enzymes that work at low washing temperatures. Many medicines are inhibitors that fit the active position of an enzyme and block it, such as penicillin ([E04](../E04-new-materials/notes.md)).
- **Trap:** the drop above the temperature optimum is denaturation, not "the enzyme dying" (enzymes aren't alive) and not fewer collisions. The outcome asks for shape and functional groups, so mention the charges or the hydrogen bridges, not only "it fits".

## E2.4 Transport of substances in the body

- Membranes: a cell membrane is a double layer of phospholipids (*fosfolipiden*). A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head (with a charged phosphate group) and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails, and in water the molecules form a bilayer with the tails inside and the heads facing the water on both sides. *(CvTE L2.3; CvTE L1.5)*
- Hydrophobic/hydrophilic (*hydrofoob/hydrofiel*): small apolar molecules, such as O₂, CO₂, steroid hormones and uncharged medicine molecules, dissolve in the hydrophobic interior of the membrane and diffuse through it. Ions and large polar molecules, such as glucose and amino acids, can't; they pass through channel and carrier proteins in the membrane. In the blood, hydrophilic substances dissolve in the plasma, while hydrophobic ones (fats, cholesterol, fat-soluble vitamins, many medicines) travel bound to proteins or packed in lipoproteins. *(CvTE L2.3; CvTE M3.6)*
- pH: the charge of an acidic or basic group depends on the pH. A weak acid HA is mainly uncharged in the very acidic stomach and mainly charged (A⁻) at the pH of blood (about 7.4); a weak base such as an amine is mainly charged in the stomach. The uncharged form crosses membranes much more easily. Aspirin, for example, is a weak acid: C₉H₈O₄ + H₂O ⇌ C₉H₇O₄⁻ + H₃O⁺. *(CvTE L2.3; CvTE R&A1.6)*
- Worked example: for an acid with K<sub>z</sub> = 1.8·10⁻⁵ (the value for ethanoic acid), the equilibrium condition gives [A⁻]/[HA] = K<sub>z</sub> / [H₃O⁺]. At pH 2.00, [H₃O⁺] = 1.0·10⁻² mol L⁻¹, so the ratio is 1.8·10⁻³ and only 0.18% of the acid is charged. At pH 7.40, [H₃O⁺] = 4.0·10⁻⁸ mol L⁻¹, so the ratio is 4.5·10² and practically all of it is charged. K<sub>z</sub> calculations are in [C05](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C05-chemical-equilibrium/notes.md). *(CvTE R&A1.4; CvTE R&A1.6)*
- Amino acids carry an acidic and a basic group; at the pH of blood they are ions with both charges, such as ⁺H₃N–CH(CH₃)–COO⁻ for alanine, which is why they need carrier proteins to cross membranes.
- **Trap:** dissolving well in blood and crossing membranes well are opposite demands: the charged form dissolves in water, the uncharged form passes the membrane. A weak acid is mostly uncharged in the stomach, yet most of it is still absorbed in the small intestine, because that surface is so much larger.
