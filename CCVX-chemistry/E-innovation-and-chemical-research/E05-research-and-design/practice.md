# E5 — Research and design: practice

Six original questions on the E5 learning outcomes (formulate, plan, execute, report), in CCVX style. E5 is about reasoning with research practice, so the "answers" are model expositions of the expected skill.

### chem-e05-q01 | points 3 | outcome E5 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A student reads: "Drinking a sports drink during exercise improves performance." Turn this into (1) a research question that can actually be investigated, and (2) a testable hypothesis.
#### Answer
1. Research question: "What is the influence of taking a glucose-electrolyte drink every 15 minutes during 60 minutes of ergometer cycling at 70% of maximum load on the average delivered power in the final 10 minutes, compared with water, in trained 17–19-year-old cyclists?" 2. Hypothesis: "If the drink supplies energy and salts at the rate they are lost, then the average power in the final 10 minutes is higher after the drink than after water (at the same riders and the same load)."
#### Solution
1. A researchable question names the subject group, the independent variable (the drink), the measured variables (power), and the comparison (water).
2. A hypothesis is the lab coat of the question: an if-then statement with a mechanism ("because energy and salts must be replaced").
3. The loop: question → hypothesis → plan → test → conclusion (this question preloads the scheme, in the E5 sequence).

### chem-e05-q02 | points 3 | outcome E5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A student wants to study "how temperature affects the rate of a tablet-solubility experiment". Fill in the design table: independent variable, dependent variable, two controlled variables, one expected source of error.
#### Answer
Independent: water-bath temperature (in 5 steps from 10 °C to 50 °C). Dependent: time for the tablet to dissolve completely (no residue). Controlled: volume of water (e.g. 200.0 mL), stirring method and speed (magnetic stirrer at the same bar and rpm), particle size/brand of the tablet. Error source: judging the "completely dissolved" moment by eye — camera with a defined endpoint or disappearance of the last swirl streaks is better.
#### Solution
1. The variable you choose on purpose is independent; what you measure is dependent.
2. Everything else that could influence the result is held equal (water volume, stirring, tablet batch and size, vessel shape): that's control of variables, the heart of a clean experiment.
3. A good plan names its likely error source and manages it: endpoint definition is a classic.

### chem-e05-q03 | points 4 | outcome E5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Design the work plan for testing the hypothesis from chem-e05-q01, covering: number of test persons, the sessions, randomisation, blinding, and the data to record.
#### Answer
12 test persons in two identical load sessions (drink / water), separated by ≥ 2 days for recovery; order randomised per person (6 start with drink, 6 with water) to cancel learning/fatigue effects; sessions double-blind if the drinks can be flavoured alike, else single-blind with the person not told; record per 5 minutes the average power and heart rate, all variables else equal (room temperature, warm-up protocol, previous meal standardised).
#### Solution
1. N = 12 gives spread without heroes; paired design (everyone is their own control) removes person-to-person variation.
2. Randomised crossover order corrects for day form and learning effects; blinding removes expectation bias.
3. Standardise everything else (last meal, warm-up, bike setting): the only difference between the sessions should be the drink.
4. Data logging tied to time: power per block, not one end feeling; and pre-register the endpoint (last 10 minutes).

### chem-e05-q04 | points 4 | outcome E5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Measurement series of a dissolving-rate experiment: time to full dissolve of a tablet at 10 °C steps: 298, 220, 160, 122, 92 s. (1) Plot/describe the trend (what kind of relation between temperature and time). (2) From this, would a statement "the rate doubles per 10 °C" hold? (3) Name one quantity you would have to record to turn the trend into a rate relationship.
#### Answer
1. The dissolution time falls strongly with temperature (steep at low T, flattening high: a curved relation, not linear). 2. Roughly yes over this span: each 10 °C step multiplies the rate by about 1.3–1.4 (time ratios: 298/220 ≈ 1.35, 220/160 ≈ 1.4, 160/122 ≈ 1.3, 122/92 ≈ 1.3), so "a good deal faster but not a doubling" in this range. 3. You must record which amount of substance actually dissolved per unit time (or the tablet's unit composition/mass) — the time alone gives a relation of temperature to an event's time, not a rate in mol s⁻¹.
#### Solution
1. Describe the curve in words as you would for the report: monotone decreasing, flattening — an asymptotic/fan relation.
2. Worked ratio check: the per-step factor ≈ 1.3–1.4, not ×2.
3. Turn time into rate: rate = amount reacted/dissolved / time; you need the amount (mass of active ingredient) per tablet (compare chem-c04-q05).

### chem-e05-q05 | points 4 | outcome E5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A report on the sports-drink study of chem-e05-q03 shows that the drink group delivers on average 3% more power in the last 10 minutes, with a spread between riders of 8 percentage points both sides. (1) Give the precisely worded conclusion, justified and bounded. (2) Which follow-up study would you propose to strengthen or refute it?
#### Answer
1. Conclusion: "Under the conditions of this study (trained 17–19-year-olds, 60 min at 70% load, crossover), taking a glucose-electrolyte drink every 15 minutes coincided with on average 3% higher power over the final 10 minutes, but with an inter-individual spread of ±8% the effect is too small to confirm for every rider — the data weakly support the hypothesis." 2. Follow-up: larger sample or repeated sessions per rider to shrink spread, and a blinded placebo formulation with identical taste/colour to exclude expectation bias.
#### Solution
1. A conclusion answers the research question with data, gives the average effect, its spread and its limits (no over-claim): "weakly supports" is the honest strength here.
2. Every study suggests its own sequel: larger N or a smarter design; and a rigor check of bias sources (a blind placebo for taste).
3. The skill is in saying no more than the measurements bear — the E5/A09-style discipline of judging.

### chem-e05-q06 | points 4 | outcome E5 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Write the abstract of your sports-drink study report in ±100 words, following the structure: question, approach, result, conclusion.
#### Answer
Question: does a glucose-electrolyte drink improve endurance performance of trained young cyclists at 70% load? Approach: 12 riders, crossover design, randomised order, identical lab conditions, power registration every 5 min over 60 min, drink vs water. Result: average power over the last 10 minutes was 3% higher with the drink, with a large inter-individual spread of ±8%. Conclusion: the drink yields a small average gain under these conditions, but the effect is not significant per individual rider; a larger, fully blinded study with the same protocol is proposed. (≈ 90 words)
#### Solution
1. Structure: question → method → key numbers → bounded conclusion; an abstract carries those four in order.
2. Honest precision: "small", "not significant per rider" and the proposal sentence — matching chem-e05-q05's conclusion.
3. A presentation matters as much as a lab result: the last step of every E5 run is communicating what can and cannot be claimed.
