# E3 — Sustainability: practice

Six original questions on the E3 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Green-chemistry formulas (atom economy, E-factor, environmental quotient EQ) are given here and must be applied; Binas values for molar masses.

### chem-e03-q01 | points 3 | outcome E3 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Calculate the atom economy of the industrial production of ethene oxide (epoxyethane) by the direct route 2 CH₂=CH₂ + O₂ → 2 C₂H₄O (M(ethene) = 28.05, M(ethene oxide) = 44.05 g mol⁻¹), and state what an atom economy of 100%, as here, tells you classically about a route.
#### Answer
100%: all atoms of the reactants are in the desired product — the elegant case of an addition-type reaction with no by-products at all.
#### Solution
1. Atom economy = mass of desired product / total mass of starting materials = 2 × 44.05 / (2 × 28.05 + 2 × 16.00) = 88.10 / 88.10 = 100%.
2. All atoms of ethene and oxygen are in the product: nothing is lost as by-products (in the balanced equation).
3. Such a route is the green ideal at the equation level (but atom economy is blind to catalysts, excess and yield: see E-factor).

### chem-e03-q02 | points 4 | outcome E3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Classical production of ethene oxide ran through chlorohydrin: CH₂=CH₂ + Cl₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → C₂H₄O + CaCl₂ + H₂O. (1) Calculate its atom economy (M(CH₂=CH₂) = 28.05, Cl₂ = 70.90, Ca(OH)₂ = 74.10, C₂H₄O = 44.05). (2) Compare with chem-e03-q01 and make the sustainability argument.
#### Answer
1. 25.5%. 2. The chlorohydrin route wastes three quarters of the mass into CaCl₂ and water: high E-factor production, plus it consumes chlorine made in the energy-hungry chlor-alkali process — the direct oxidation route (Q1) is the green winner on every axis.
#### Solution
1. Atom economy = 44.05 / (28.05 + 70.90 + 74.10) × 100% = 44.05 / 173.05 = 25.5%.
2. The other 75% of the mass becomes CaCl₂ waste and water.
3. The comparison of the two routes is the classic sustainability lesson: chloride-based routes with by-products drive salt waste and chlorine production (with its own legacy of mercury-cell pollution), the modern direct route avoids them — sustainability runs through the balanced equation.

### chem-e03-q03 | points 4 | outcome E3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Environmental quotient. Process A delivers 1.0 kg of product with 2.0 kg of NaCl (Q = 1) and 0.10 kg of a chromium salt (Q = 100) as waste; process B the same product with 5.0 kg of NaCl only. (1) Compute E-factor and EQ for both. (2) Which process would you choose, and why is the E-factor alone not enough here?
#### Answer
1. A: E = 2.1, EQ = 12; B: E = 5.0, EQ = 5.0. 2. Choose B: its higher E-factor is composed of harmless salt waste; A's chromium salt (Q = 100) dominates the environmental quotient: the E-factor only counts kilograms, but EQ weighs them by toxicity — for a fair sustainability judgment, what the waste is matters at least as much as how much there is.
#### Solution
1. E_A = (2.0 + 0.10) / 1.0 = 2.1; EQ_A = (2.0 × 1 + 0.10 × 100) / 1.0 = (2.0 + 10) / 1.0 = 12. E_B = 5.0 / 1.0 = 5.0; EQ_B = 5.0 / 1.0 = 5.0.
2. A's tiny 0.10 kg of chromium salt is worth 10 "toxic-kg" on the EQ scale (the correction Q exists precisely because heavy-metal waste is much worse than harmless salt waste).
3. Conclusion: EQ tells you what E hides. Sustainability is not a kilogram contest: substance identity counts (this combined E/Q usage is exactly what E3 wants you to handle).

### chem-e03-q04 | points 3 | outcome E3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A plant raises the yield of a low-yielding ring-closure step from 40% to 72%. Explain with numbers (basis 100 g of starting material) what this does to (1) waste mass and to (2) the E-factor; product and by-product masses ignored, only conversion losses count here.
#### Answer
At 40%: 60 g left-over from the 100 g feed went to waste → waste 60 g for 40 g product: E = 60/40 = 1.5. At 72%: 28 g waste for 72 g product: E = 28/72 ≈ 0.39. Nearly a fourfold waste reduction per kilogram of product.
#### Solution
1. Yield (uitkomst/rendement) of the step = converted fraction; the rest is, for this counting, waste or must be recycled.
2. 40%: waste = 60 g; product = 40 g → E = 1.5. 72%: waste = 28 g; product = 72 g → E = 0.39.
3. Even "yield work" is green chemistry: fewer unconverted starting chemicals per kilogram of product = smaller E-factor, fewer emissions, less raw material to deliver — a direct relation between yield and sustainability.

### chem-e03-q05 | points 3 | outcome E3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Which of these conversions of chemical energy fits "fossil fuels" and which "electrochemistry" — and what is the sustainability difference when the same redox reaction supplies the energy? (A) burning methanol in a burner for heat, (B) methanol in a fuel cell for electricity.
#### Answer
A = fossil-type use of fuel energy: chemical energy to heat, with combustion limitations (efficiency, exhaust gases). B = electrochemistry: the same redox reaction in an electrochemical cell, electrons via the external circuit: higher useful fraction, and cold exhaust — but only as green as the origin of the methanol.
#### Solution
1. A burner converts chemical energy nearly fully to heat, but heat of that temperature can only partially become work in any motor downstream (thermal path limits, see C06/C09).
2. A fuel cell lets the same reaction run in one electrochemical step with electrons moving through a wire (see C01): it skips the heat engine's losses — the typical win of the electrochemical route.
3. Sustainability verdict stays with the source: fossil methanol just moves the emissions upstream; green methanol (from biomass or captured CO₂ + renewable H₂) makes the electrified route genuinely cleaner.

### chem-e03-q06 | points 4 | outcome E3 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A journalistic claim says a newly proposed synthesis is "100% sustainable because it is 100% atom-efficient". Criticise this claim with three other aspects from the E3 list, each with one sentence, and finish with a one-sentence verdict.
#### Answer
E-factor: real production never meets the equation's clean numbers — conversion and purification steps add waste. Energy effect: the route's heat and cooling demands come from somewhere (fossil?). Resource origin: 100% atom efficiency from fossil feedstock is still fossil. Verdict: atom economy is a fine filter for equations but sustainability is a whole-process balance, and the claim hides everything the equation cannot see.
#### Solution
1. Atom economy is a property of the balanced equation; sustainability asks about the entire process (see the trap in the notes).
2. Perfect routes can have terrible process metrics: low yield (see chem-e03-q04), hard separations, toxic catalysts, dangerous conditions, and a fossil feedstock.
3. A sound evaluation works through the checklist: resources → energy → waste (E-factor/EQ) → yield and recycling — and then concludes, not before.
