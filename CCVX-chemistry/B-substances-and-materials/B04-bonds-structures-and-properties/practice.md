# B4 — Bonds, structures and properties: practice

Ten original questions on the B4 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Use Binas for standard electrode potentials and melting points.

### chem-b04-q01 | points 2 | outcome B4.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain, in terms of charge carriers, why (1) copper conducts electricity as a solid, (2) solid sodium chloride does not, and (3) a sodium chloride solution does.
#### Answer
1. Copper's metal lattice holds free (delocalised) electrons that move through the whole lattice. 2. In solid NaCl the ions are fixed in the lattice: charge carriers exist but cannot move. 3. In solution the hydrated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions move freely and carry the current.
#### Solution
1. Conduction needs charge carriers that are both present and mobile. In a metal the valence electrons are delocalised over all atoms: they flow under a voltage.
2. Solid NaCl has charge carriers (ions) but they are immobile in the lattice: no conduction.
3. Dissolving gives the ions freedom: the solution conducts (electrolyte). Both conditions — presence and mobility — must hold.

### chem-b04-q02 | points 3 | outcome B4.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Explain at the level of the metallic lattice why (1) a metal like copper is malleable (can be hammered into sheets) rather than brittle, and (2) an alloy such as bronze is harder than pure copper.
#### Answer
1. Lattices of equal-sized metal atoms let layers slide over each other, and the free electrons keep holding the whole together (the metallic bond is not directional), so the shape changes without breaking. 2. Atoms of the second metal (different size) disturb the lattice (lattice errors); layers then slide less easily, so the alloy is harder.
#### Solution
1. In the metallic lattice all positive ions swim in one shared electron sea; when layers shift, the electron sea shifts with them and the bond is not broken: malleability (deformability).
2. Bronze (copper with tin): the tin atoms occupy copper lattice positions but have a different size, distorting the lattice. Sliding a layer now has to pass these bumps, which blocks movement: the alloy deforms less easily and is harder. The same trick makes steel harder than pure iron.

### chem-b04-q03 | points 3 | outcome B4.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
The standard electrode potentials (Binas) are V₀(Fe²⁺/Fe) = −0.45 V and V₀(Al³⁺/Al) = −1.66 V. (1) Which of the two metals is the stronger reductor, and what is it called when such a metal is eaten away by reaction with moist air? (2) Explain why aluminium, the stronger reductor, still hardly corrodes in practice while iron does.
#### Answer
1. Aluminium (lower V₀). The eating away is corrosion. 2. Aluminium is protected by a dense oxide layer (Al₂O₃) that sticks to the surface and closes the metal off from oxygen and water; rust on iron is porous and flakes off, so fresh iron keeps being attacked.
#### Solution
1. The lower V₀, the stronger the reductor: Al (−1.66 V) is much less "noble" than Fe (−0.45 V); iron is more noble than aluminium. Corrosion is the oxidation of a metal by oxidators from the environment (O₂ with water).
2. V₀ says what can react, not whether the product protects. Aluminium forms a continuous, adherent Al₂O₃ skin that passivates the metal; iron's rust lets water and oxygen through, so corrosion continues.
3. Protective layers are also made artificially: paint, plastic coating, or zinc on galvanised iron (zinc also protects sacrificially, because it is the stronger reductor).

### chem-b04-q04 | points 3 | outcome B4.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A polyethylene bucket softens and deforms over a pan of boiling water; a melamine kitchen worktop does not. (1) What are the names of these two classes of plastics? (2) Explain the difference with the structure of their polymer chains.
#### Answer
1. Polyethylene is a thermoplastic, melamine resin a thermosetting plastic (thermoset). 2. Thermoplastics: separate chains held together by Van der Waals bonds, which loosen on heating, so the material softens and can be remoulded. Thermosets: chains tied together by crosslinks (atomic bonds between chains), which do not loosen on heating — the material stays hard (and chars rather than melts when overheated).
#### Solution
1. Thermoplastic = softens reversibly on heating; thermoset = stays rigid, because its structure was fixed when it was made.
2. PE chains are long separate molecules attracting each other only by Van der Waals bonds; warming gives the chains mobility.
3. In a thermoset such as melamine resin (or bakelite) the chains are connected by crosslinks — real atomic bonds in all directions — so the whole object is, in effect, one molecule: heating cannot soften it.
4. Practical link: crosslinks also explain UV sensitivity and brittleness of some plastics, and why thermosets cannot be recycled by remelting.

### chem-b04-q05 | points 2 | outcome B4.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Pure PVC is a stiff plastic; the PVC of shower curtains and garden hoses is flexible. What is added to achieve this, and how does it work at the chain level?
#### Answer
Plasticizers; small molecules that take places between the polymer chains, so that the chains can slide along each other more easily (their mutual Van der Waals bonding is reduced), making the material flexible.
#### Solution
1. Additives that soften a polymer are plasticizers (*weekmakers*).
2. A plasticizer molecule sits between the chains and holds them somewhat apart, lowering the intermolecular attraction; the chains then slide past each other: the rigid thermoplastic becomes flexible.
3. Plasticizers slowly evaporate or wash out, which is why old PVC hardens — a classic structure-to-property link (B4.1).

### chem-b04-q06 | points 3 | outcome B4.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Use the VSEPR model: give the steric number, the shape of the molecule (or ion) and the bond angle for (1) CH₄, (2) NH₃, (3) BCl₃.
#### Answer
1. Steric number 4; tetrahedral; 109.5°. 2. Steric number 4 (one lone pair); trigonal pyramidal; a little less than 109.5° (107°). 3. Steric number 3; trigonal planar; 120°.
#### Solution
1. CH₄: C has four bonding pairs, no lone pairs → steric number 4 → tetrahedron with bond angles ≈ 109° (109.5°).
2. NH₃: N has three bonds + one lone pair = 4 electron domains: the electron geometry is tetrahedral, but the shape of the molecule is pyramidal; the lone pair presses the bonds slightly together (to about 107°).
3. BCl₃: B has only three bonding pairs → steric number 3 → flat triangle, 120°. (Compare steric number 2, e.g. CO₂: linear, 180°.)

### chem-b04-q07 | points 3 | outcome B4.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
For each molecule, state whether it is a dipole molecule and justify it from the spatial structure and the charge distribution: (1) CO₂, (2) H₂O, (3) CCl₄, (4) CHCl₃.
#### Answer
1. No: linear and symmetrical — the two equal polar bonds cancel each other. 2. Yes: bent shape, so the δ− on O and δ+ on the H side do not cancel. 3. No: tetrahedral and symmetrical — four identical C–Cl dipoles cancel. 4. Yes: the H and the three Cl atoms are unequal, so the tetrahedron is not symmetric; the charge distribution has a δ+ end near H and a δ− end at the Cl side.
#### Solution
1. Whether a molecule is a dipole asks two things: are the bonds polar (electronegativity differences), and is the molecule symmetric? A symmetric arrangement makes the partial charges cancel as vectors.
2. CO₂ is linear O=C=O: the two C=O dipoles point exactly opposite each other, net zero.
3. H₂O is bent (VSEPR, two lone pairs on O): the O end is δ− and the H side δ+ — a dipole, and hence hydrogen bridges.
4. CCl₄ is a perfect tetrahedron with four identical poles: no net dipole. CHCl₃ replaces one Cl by H: the symmetry is broken, so CHCl₃ is a (weak) dipole.

### chem-b04-q08 | points 3 | outcome B4.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Beef dripping is solid at room temperature, sunflower oil is liquid. Both are fats/oils (triglycerides). Use the spatial structure of their hydrocarbon chains to explain the difference.
#### Answer
Solid fats hold mostly saturated chains (straight, no double bonds), which pack closely in a regular crystal with many Van der Waals contacts — already solid at room temperature. Oils hold mostly unsaturated chains, whose cis double bonds put a kink in the chain; the kinked chains cannot pack closely, so the Van der Waals bonding is weaker and the substance is liquid at room temperature.
#### Solution
1. Triglyceride chains that are saturated are zig-zag straight and can lie closely together: many Van der Waals contacts per chain, higher melting range: a fat.
2. Unsaturated chains contain one or more C=C double bonds in the cis form, which force a fixed bend (kink) in the chain.
3. Kinked chains stack badly: fewer and weaker contacts between chains, so less energy is needed to disorganise them — the substance melts below room temperature: an oil.
4. The same spatial logic explains why hydrogenation (hardening oil into margarine) raises the melting range: the kinks are removed.

### chem-b04-q09 | points 4 | outcome B4.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Porcelain (a ceramic) and steel (an alloy) are both "hard", yet a dropped cup shatters while a dropped spoon only dents. Explain this contrast from the bonding structure of a ceramic (ionic/atomic lattice) and of a metal lattice, naming the properties hardness and brittleness.
#### Answer
A ceramic's ionic/atomic lattice cannot let layers slide: shifting a layer brings equal charges next to each other (or would have to break directional atomic bonds), so the lattice cracks — hard but brittle. Steel's metal lattice lets layers slide as the free-electron sea follows along: it deforms rather than cracks — hard and tough (not brittle).
#### Solution
1. Both materials resist scratching (hard), but brittleness asks what happens under an impact.
2. In an ionic lattice each ion is locked among ions of the opposite charge; a small shift puts equal charges face to face, the repulsion tears planes apart, and there are no electrons to glue it: the cup shatters. A covalent (atomic) lattice would have to break many directional bonds to deform: equally brittle.
3. In a metal lattice the layers slide and the delocalised electrons restore the bonding behind: the spoon absorbs the blow by deforming.
4. Conclusion: hardness is about resistance to deformation; brittleness about failing by fracture instead of flow. The lattice type decides which you get.

### chem-b04-q10 | points 4 | outcome B4.5 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The secondary structure of proteins (α-helix, β-sheet) is destroyed by heating, while the primary structure (the sequence of amino acids in the chain) survives boiling water. (1) Which bonds hold the secondary structure together, and between which groups? (2) Explain why heating destroys this structure. (3) Explain why the primary structure survives the same heating. (4) Link this to what happens to a protein when you fry an egg.
#### Answer
1. Hydrogen bridges between the C=O group of one peptide bond and the N–H group of another further along the chain. 2. At higher temperature the thermal movement breaks these (weak) hydrogen bridges, so the helix/sheet unrolls. 3. The primary structure is held by atomic bonds (peptide bonds), which need far more energy than boiling water provides. 4. The egg proteins denature: secondary structure unrolls, chains tangle and form new cross-bonds — the liquid white sets solid; it is a structural change of the protein, not a decomposition of the chain.
#### Solution
1. The spatial folding of the chain (α-helix, β-sheet) is maintained by hydrogen bridges between backbone C=O and N–H groups (B3/B4 knowledge of hydrogen bridges in O/N-containing molecules).
2. Hydrogen bridges are weak intermolecular bonds; the extra thermal motion at 50–100 °C is enough to break them, so the regular folding is lost: denaturation.
3. Along the chain the amino acids are connected by peptide bonds — atomic bonds an order of magnitude stronger; boiling water does not break them (hydrolysis of peptide bonds needs harsher conditions, e.g., strong acid or enzymes).
4. Frying an egg: heating denatures the albumin — the folded chains open up and then aggregate into a network that fixes water — the white sets. The amino acid sequence is unchanged; the structure is, irreversibly.
