## B4.1 Structure and properties of substances

### Electrical conductivity

- A substance conducts electricity (*elektrisch geleidingsvermogen*) only when it contains charge carriers (*ladingdragers*) that can move: free electrons or mobile ions. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Electrons: metals conduct as solids and as liquids, through their free electrons. Graphite conducts along its layers, through the mobile electrons in them ([B03](../B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md)). *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Ions: a salt conducts only when molten (l) or dissolved (aq), because only then can its ions move. In solid NaCl the ions are fixed in the lattice, so it doesn't conduct, but molten NaCl and NaCl(aq) do. In a salt solution the current is carried by ions, not by electrons. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Molecular substances don't conduct, as solid, liquid or gas, because molecules carry no charge: pure water hardly conducts, and a sugar solution doesn't. A molecular substance that ionises in water does give a conducting solution, as hydrogen chloride does: HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). *(CvTE M4.1)*
- So a conductivity test shows the type of substance: conducting as a solid means a metal (or graphite); conducting when molten, but not as a solid, means a salt; conducting neither as a solid nor when molten means a molecular substance. Atomic lattices such as diamond and quartz don't conduct as solids either. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- **Trap:** a solid salt consists of charged particles but doesn't conduct: the charges must also be able to move.
- **Trap:** a solution that conducts doesn't prove that the substance is a salt. Hydrogen chloride is a molecular substance, but its solution conducts because it forms ions with water; the test that separates a salt from a molecular substance is conduction when molten.

### Deformability

- Metals are deformable (*vervormbaar*): they can be hammered, bent and drawn into wire. The layers of ions in the metallic lattice can slide over each other while the free electrons keep holding them together; the metallic bond acts in all directions, so it doesn't break when the layers shift. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Lattice errors (*roosterfouten*), such as atoms of a different size in an alloy, disturb the regular layers so that they can't slide as easily: the metal becomes harder and less deformable. This is why steel is harder than pure iron and bronze harder than pure copper. How temperature affects this is in [E01](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E01-chemical-research/notes.md). *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE M5.1)*
- Salts are brittle (*bros*): when a force shifts one layer of the ionic lattice over the next by one ion, ions of the same charge come next to each other, repel each other, and the crystal splits. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Thermoplastics (*thermoplasten*) consist of long chains without crosslinks, held together only by bonds between the chains: Van der Waals bonds, sometimes hydrogen bridges. When heated these bonds weaken, the chains slide past each other and the plastic softens and melts, so it can be moulded again and recycled by melting. Examples: polyethene (PE), PVC and PET. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Thermosetting plastics (*thermoharders*) have crosslinks (*crosslinks*): atomic bonds (covalent bonds) between the chains, which make one three-dimensional network. They don't soften when heated, because that would mean breaking atomic bonds; at high temperature they decompose (char) instead of melting. Examples: Bakelite and cured epoxy resin. How crosslinks form is in [D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md). *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Plasticizers (*weekmakers*) are small molecules mixed in between the polymer chains. They push the chains further apart, which weakens the Van der Waals bonds between them, so the chains slide more easily and the plastic becomes soft and flexible. Soft PVC, as in garden hoses and cable insulation, contains plasticizers; hard PVC, as in pipes, doesn't. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- **Trap:** a thermoset doesn't melt at a higher temperature than a thermoplastic; it doesn't melt at all, because its chains are joined by atomic bonds.

### UV-light sensitivity

- Multiple atomic bonds (C=C, C=O) absorb UV light. The absorbed energy can break a bond, which forms radicals (*radicalen*). These react further: they cut chains, so the material gets weaker, or join chains with new crosslinks, so it gets harder. Either way the material becomes brittle and can crack. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- So polymers with C=C bonds, such as natural rubber, are more sensitive to UV light than polyethene, which has only single bonds. Plastics for outdoor use contain UV stabilisers. *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE M5.1)*
- **Trap:** UV damage doesn't only break chains. New crosslinks also make a plastic brittle, even though they join chains together.

### Corrosion susceptibility

- A metal corrodes (*corroderen*) when it is oxidised: it acts as a reductor and gives off electrons. When iron rusts, Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2 e⁻ and O₂ + 2 H₂O + 4 e⁻ → 4 OH⁻, overall 2 Fe + O₂ + 2 H₂O → 2 Fe²⁺ + 4 OH⁻. The Fe²⁺ ions are then oxidised further to iron(III) compounds, rust. Redox reactions are in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md). *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Nobility (*edelheid*) follows from the standard electrode potential of the metal ion/metal couple in the Binas table of standard electrode potentials. The lower the potential, the stronger the metal is as a reductor, the less noble (*minder edel*) it is and the more easily it corrodes. From Binas: Ag⁺/Ag +0.80 V, Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34 V, Pb²⁺/Pb −0.13 V, Fe²⁺/Fe −0.45 V, Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76 V, Mg²⁺/Mg −2.37 V and Na⁺/Na −2.71 V. *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE R1.10)*
- So silver and copper are nobler than iron, and zinc and magnesium less noble; gold and platinum are nobler still and hardly corrode. Iron rusts because the oxidator, oxygen with water (+0.40 V), has a higher standard electrode potential than the reductor, iron (Fe²⁺/Fe, −0.45 V). *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE R1.10)*
- A protective layer (*beschermende laag*) keeps oxygen and water away from the metal. Aluminium is less noble than iron, yet it hardly corrodes: it at once forms a thin, dense layer of aluminium oxide that sticks firmly to the metal, 4 Al + 3 O₂ → 2 Al₂O₃. Rust, by contrast, is porous and flakes off, so iron keeps rusting. *(CvTE M4.1; CvTE M5.1)*
- Galvanised steel (*verzinkt staal*) has a layer of zinc. Where the layer is scratched, the zinc, which is less noble (−0.76 V) than iron (−0.45 V), is oxidised instead of the iron: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2 e⁻. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- Stainless steel contains chromium, which forms a thin layer of chromium oxide on the surface; the layer protects the steel and repairs itself after a scratch. Paint, oil and plastic coatings also keep air and water away from iron. *(CvTE M4.1)*
- **Trap:** a less noble metal doesn't always corrode faster in practice. Aluminium is less noble than iron, but its oxide layer protects it; nobility only tells you how the bare metals compare.

## B4.2 Composites, polymers, alloys and ceramics

- The properties of these materials follow from their components and how these are arranged in space: from the particles (micro level) up to fibres, grains and layers that you can see under a microscope (meso level, *mesoniveau*). *(CvTE M4.2; CvTE M6.1)*
- Composites (*composieten*) combine two or more materials so that each makes up for a weakness of the other. In reinforced concrete (*gewapend beton*) the concrete resists compression and the steel bars resist tension; in glass-fibre or carbon-fibre reinforced plastics the fibres give strength and the plastic holds them together. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Polymers (*polymeren*) consist of long chain molecules. Their properties depend on chain length, crosslinks, side groups and additives such as plasticizers (B4.1). *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Alloys (*legeringen*) are metals with atoms of other elements built into the lattice ([B02](../B02-properties-and-models/notes.md)). Ceramic materials (*keramische materialen*), such as porcelain, brick and aluminium oxide, consist of ionic and atomic lattices; more in [E04](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E04-new-materials/notes.md). *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Deformability: metals and alloys deform because their layers slide; thermoplastics deform when warm; thermosets and ceramics hardly deform but break. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Conductivity (*geleidend vermogen*): metals and alloys conduct through their free electrons. Most polymers and ceramics have no mobile charge carriers and insulate, which is why cables have a plastic coating. Polymers with a long chain of alternating single and double bonds can be made to conduct. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Water repellence and water-binding capacity (*waterafstotendheid*, *waterbindend vermogen*): apolar polymers such as polyethene repel water, and polymers with many hydrophilic groups bind it. Superabsorbent sodium polyacrylate in nappies has a COO⁻ group on every second C atom of the chain, with Na⁺ ions alongside. Water molecules are bound by hydrogen bridges to the COO⁻ groups and by ion-dipole bonds to the Na⁺ ions, and a few crosslinks stop the polymer from dissolving, so it swells into a gel that holds many times its own mass of water. *(CvTE M4.2; CvTE M5.1)*
- Corrosion susceptibility: metals and alloys can corrode (B4.1), stainless steel much less than ordinary steel; polymers and ceramics don't corrode. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- UV-light sensitivity: polymers with multiple bonds degrade in sunlight (B4.1); metals and ceramics don't. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Combustibility (*brandbaarheid*): polymers are carbon compounds and burn; polyethene burns completely to CO₂ and H₂O, and PVC also releases HCl when it burns. Ceramics don't burn. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Hardness (*hardheid*): ceramics are very hard because of their strong ionic or atomic lattices; alloys are harder than the pure metals, and thermosets harder than thermoplastics. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- Brittleness (*brosheid*): ceramics are brittle, like salts and atomic lattices, because their layers can't slide without breaking bonds or bringing ions of the same charge together. Thermosets are brittle too, and plastics become brittle after UV damage. *(CvTE M4.2)*
- **Trap:** a composite is not an alloy or a mixture at particle level: its components stay separate and can be seen at meso level, like the fibres in a plastic or the steel bars in concrete.

## B4.3 VSEPR theory

- Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory: the electron pairs around a central atom repel each other and get as far apart as possible. Both bonding pairs and lone pairs count. *(CvTE M1.16)*
- The steric number (*omringingsgetal*) of an atom is the number of atoms bonded to it plus the number of lone pairs on it. A double or triple bond counts once, because it points in one direction. Start from the Lewis structure ([B01](../B01-particle-models/notes.md)). *(CvTE M1.16; CvTE M1.15)*
- Steric number 4: tetrahedral (*tetraëder*), bond angle about 109°. CH₄ and NH₄⁺ (four bonded atoms) are tetrahedral. In NH₃ (three bonded atoms and one lone pair) the four electron pairs point to the corners of a tetrahedron, but the atoms form a pyramid; H₂O (two bonded atoms and two lone pairs) is bent. This model gives about 109° for both; the real angles are slightly smaller, about 107° in NH₃ and 104.5° in H₂O, because lone pairs repel more strongly. *(CvTE M1.16)*
- Steric number 3: planar (*plat vlak*), bond angle about 120°. Examples: BF₃ (boron has only three electron pairs), each C atom in ethene CH₂=CH₂, the C atom in methanal H₂C=O and the C atom in the carbonate ion CO₃²⁻. *(CvTE M1.16)*
- Steric number 2: linear (*lineair*), bond angle 180°. Examples: CO₂ (O=C=O), each C atom in ethyne HC≡CH, and HCN (H–C≡N). *(CvTE M1.16)*
- The theory also works for parts of molecules. In ethanol, CH₃–CH₂–OH, both C atoms are tetrahedral and the O atom has steric number 4 (two bonded atoms and two lone pairs), so the C–O–H part is bent. In ethanoic acid, CH₃–COOH, the C atom of the COOH group has steric number 3 and is planar. *(CvTE M1.16)*
- **Trap:** the steric number is not the number of bonds. Lone pairs count and a double bond counts once: the C atom in CO₂ forms four bonds but has steric number 2.
- **Trap:** BF₃ is planar but NH₃ is a pyramid, although both have three atoms around the central atom: N also has a lone pair.

## B4.4 Dipole molecules

- A molecule is a dipole (*dipool*) when it has polar bonds and their partial charges don't cancel, so that the centre of the δ+ charge and the centre of the δ− charge lie in different places. Whether they cancel depends on the spatial structure and its symmetry (*symmetrie*). *(CvTE M3.5)*
- Method: find the polar bonds (B3.3 in [B03](../B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md)); find the shape with VSEPR (B4.3); then check whether the polar bonds are arranged symmetrically, so that they cancel. *(CvTE M3.5)*
- Dipoles: H₂O is bent, so its two O–H bonds don't cancel, and NH₃ is a pyramid, so its three N–H bonds don't cancel. *(CvTE M3.5)*
- No dipole: CO₂ is linear, with its two C=O bonds pointing in opposite directions, and BF₃ is planar, with its three B–F bonds at 120°. In both the polar bonds cancel. *(CvTE M3.5)*
- CHCl₃ is a dipole and CCl₄ is not: in the tetrahedron of CCl₄ the four C–Cl bonds cancel, but in CHCl₃ one of them is replaced by an apolar C–H bond. This reasoning needs electronegativity, because C–Cl is polar by electronegativity but not by the N/O/F rule (B3.3). With fluorine it works under both rules: CHF₃ is a dipole and CF₄ is not. *(CvTE M3.5)*
- Molecules with only apolar bonds, such as Cl₂ and the alkanes (C–H counts as apolar), are not dipoles. *(CvTE M3.5)*
- Dipole molecules attract each other with dipole-dipole bonds, which raises the boiling point, and they mix with water more easily ([B03](../B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md)). *(CvTE M3.1; CvTE M3.7)*
- **Trap:** polar bonds alone don't make a dipole: CO₂ has polar bonds but no dipole. And a Lewis structure drawn flat, with H–O–H in a straight line, doesn't show the real shape; work out the shape with VSEPR first.

## B4.5 Spatial structure, fats and proteins

### Difference between fat and oil

- Fats and oils are triglycerides (*triglyceriden*): esters of glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) with three fatty acids (*vetzuren*). A fat (*vet*) is solid at room temperature, an oil (*olie*) liquid. Their structure is in [G01](../../G-society-chemistry-and-technology/G01-chemistry-of-life/notes.md). *(CvTE L1.3)*
- Saturated fatty acids (*verzadigde vetzuren*), such as stearic acid C₁₇H₃₅COOH, have only C–C single bonds in the chain. Their chains are straight (a zigzag), so the molecules pack closely, with a large contact area and strong Van der Waals bonds: triglycerides with mostly saturated chains are fats, such as butter. *(CvTE L1.3; CvTE M3.1)*
- Unsaturated fatty acids (*onverzadigde vetzuren*), such as oleic acid C₁₇H₃₃COOH (one C=C) and linoleic acid C₁₇H₃₁COOH (two C=C), have C=C double bonds, mostly in the cis form. Each cis double bond puts a bend in the chain, so the molecules can't pack closely, the Van der Waals bonds are weaker and the melting point is lower: triglycerides with many unsaturated chains are oils, such as olive oil and sunflower oil. *(CvTE L1.3; CvTE M3.1)*
- Hardening (*vetharding*) turns an oil into a solid fat: hydrogen is added to the C=C bonds, with nickel as the catalyst, so the chains become saturated and straight. Shown for the free acid, oleic acid becomes stearic acid: C₁₇H₃₃COOH + H₂ → C₁₇H₃₅COOH. Addition reactions are in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md). *(CvTE L1.3; CvTE R1.3)*
- **Trap:** it is the bent shape, not a charge or polarity of the double bond, that keeps the chains of an oil apart. A trans double bond hardly bends the chain, so trans fatty acids pack almost like saturated ones (cis and trans are in [E02](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md)).
- **Trap:** "unsaturated" here means C=C double bonds in the chain; it has nothing to do with an unsaturated solution.

### Secondary structure of proteins

- A protein is a long chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, –CO–NH–. The primary structure (*primaire structuur*) is the order of the amino acids. The secondary structure (*secundaire structuur*) is the way parts of the chain fold locally into regular shapes, held together by hydrogen bridges between the C=O group of one peptide bond and the N–H group of another. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- In an α-helix (*alfahelix*) the chain is coiled into a spiral. Each C=O group forms a hydrogen bridge with the N–H group of the amino acid four places further along the same chain, and the side groups point outwards. The protein keratin in hair and wool is mainly α-helix. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- In a β-sheet (*bètaplaat*) stretched parts of the chain lie side by side, with hydrogen bridges between the C=O and N–H groups of neighbouring parts; the side groups point alternately above and below the sheet. The neighbouring parts can belong to the same chain or to different chains. The protein of silk is mainly β-sheet. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- Draw these hydrogen bridges as dotted lines between the O atom of a C=O group and the H atom of an N–H group. *(CvTE Bijlage 3E)*
- The tertiary structure (*tertiaire structuur*), the folding of the whole chain held by bonds between the side groups, is in [G01](../../G-society-chemistry-and-technology/G01-chemistry-of-life/notes.md). Heating breaks hydrogen bridges, so a protein loses its shape and with it its function; this is why egg white turns solid when it is cooked. *(CvTE L1.3)*
- **Trap:** the secondary structure is held by hydrogen bridges between the C=O and N–H groups of the backbone, not by the side groups; bonds between side groups give the tertiary structure.

## CCVX versus the Dutch syllabus

- **In both:** everything above.
- **CCVX only:** lattice errors (*roosterfouten*) under deformability (B4.1); the CvTE text names only the lattice structure (M4.1).
- **Dutch syllabus only:** the α-helix, the β-sheet and triglycerides are named explicitly (L1.3); CCVX says only "secondary structure" and "fat and oil".
- **Numbering:** VSEPR (B4.3) is M1.16 in the Dutch particle models, dipoles (B4.4) are M3.5, and fats and protein structure (B4.5) are L1.3.
- **Polar bonds:** for B4.4 CCVX finds polar bonds with the N/O/F rule and the Dutch syllabus with electronegativity; see [B03](../B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md).
