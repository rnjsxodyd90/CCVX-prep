# B1 — Particle models: practice

Fifteen original questions on the B1 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Use Binas: the periodic table and the tables of the elements and of atomic masses.

### chem-b01-q01 | points 2 | outcome B1.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in (1) an atom of iron-56 (atomic number 26) and (2) the ion Fe³⁺ formed from it.
#### Answer
1. 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 26 electrons. 2. 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.
#### Solution
1. The atomic number 26 equals the number of protons; a neutral atom has the same number of electrons; neutrons = 56 − 26 = 30.
2. The charge of an ion comes only from the electron cloud: Fe³⁺ has lost 3 electrons, so 26 − 3 = 23 electrons; the nucleus is untouched.

### chem-b01-q02 | points 2 | outcome B1.2 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain why lithium, sodium and potassium, which stand above each other in group 1 of the periodic table, react in much the same way (for example, each with water to a hydroxide and hydrogen), and why the reaction gets more violent down the group.
#### Answer
They all have one valence electron (same outer shell occupation), so they react the same way; down the group the outer electron sits further from the nucleus and is given off more easily, so the reaction is more violent.
#### Solution
1. Elements in one group have the same number of valence electrons, and the valence electrons control the chemistry → each alkali metal gives off one electron (M → M⁺ + e⁻): 2 M + 2 H₂O → 2 M⁺ + 2 OH⁻ + H₂.
2. Down the group a shell is added each period, so the valence electron is less strongly attracted and more easily given off: potassium reacts more violently than sodium, sodium more than lithium.

### chem-b01-q03 | points 2 | outcome B1.3 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Use the shell model to explain why calcium forms Ca²⁺ ions and oxygen forms O²⁻ ions.
#### Answer
Ca (2, 8, 8, 2) gives off its two valence electrons to reach a full outer shell (the argon configuration): Ca²⁺. O (2, 6) takes up two electrons to complete its outer shell (the neon configuration): O²⁻.
#### Solution
1. Calcium has 20 electrons: 2, 8, 8, 2. Losing the 2 electrons of the outer shell leaves the stable noble-gas configuration, so Ca²⁺.
2. Oxygen has 8 electrons: 2, 6. Taking up 2 electrons completes the outer shell, so O²⁻.
3. This tendency to a full outer shell is the octet rule (electrovalence): the ion charge follows from the group position.

### chem-b01-q04 | points 3 | outcome B1.4–B1.5 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the symbol of: (1) cobalt, (2) manganese, (3) silicon, (4) boron, (5) tin, (6) chromium, and the name of: (7) Ni, (8) Pt, (9) Li.
#### Answer
1. Co; 2. Mn; 3. Si; 4. B; 5. Sn; 6. Cr; 7. nickel; 8. platinum; 9. lithium.
#### Solution
1–6. Co, Mn, Si, B, Sn (*stannum*), Cr. Watch the classic traps: Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide; Si is silicon, not sulfur (S).
7–9. Ni nickel, Pt platinum, Li lithium. These less common elements are on the CCVX lists B1.4 and B1.5, so learn them with the periodic table.

### chem-b01-q05 | points 3 | outcome B1.6 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Give the molecular formula of: (1) nitrogen dioxide, (2) phosphoric acid, (3) sulfur trioxide, (4) hydrogen peroxide, (5) glucose, (6) ammonia.
#### Answer
1. NO₂; 2. H₃PO₄; 3. SO₃; 4. H₂O₂; 5. C₆H₁₂O₆; 6. NH₃
#### Solution
1–4. NO₂, H₃PO₄, SO₃, H₂O₂ — all on the B1.6 list; do not confuse nitrogen monoxide NO with nitrogen dioxide NO₂, or sulfur dioxide SO₂ with sulfur trioxide SO₃.

### chem-b01-q06 | points 3 | outcome B1.7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Give the systematic (IUPAC) name of each salt: (1) FeCl₂, (2) (NH₄)₂CO₃, (3) Na₂S₂O₃, (4) Cu(NO₃)₂.
#### Answer
1. iron(II) chloride; 2. ammonium carbonate; 3. sodium thiosulfate; 4. copper(II) nitrate
#### Solution
1. Fe can be 2+ or 3+; here 2 Cl⁻ balance Fe²⁺: iron(II) chloride — the Roman numeral is compulsory when an ion can have more than one charge.
2. NH₄⁺ and CO₃²⁻: ammonium carbonate.
3. Na⁺ and S₂O₃²⁻ (thiosulfate, on the B1.7 list): sodium thiosulfate.
4. Cu²⁺ with two NO₃⁻: copper(II) nitrate.

### chem-b01-q07 | points 3 | outcome B1.10–B1.11 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Give the formula of: (1) calcium phosphate, (2) tin(II) chloride, (3) potassium permanganate, (4) iron(II) sulfate heptahydrate.
#### Answer
1. Ca₃(PO₄)₂; 2. SnCl₂; 3. KMnO₄; 4. FeSO₄·7H₂O
#### Solution
1. Ca²⁺ (2+) and PO₄³⁻ (3−): smallest common multiple is 6, so three Ca and two phosphate groups: Ca₃(PO₄)₂ — brackets around the composite ion.
2. Tin(II) means Sn²⁺, so two chlorides: SnCl₂.
3. K⁺ and MnO₄⁻ (permanganate): KMnO₄.
4. Fe²⁺ and SO₄²⁻: FeSO₄, and "heptahydrate" means seven crystal-water molecules after the dot: FeSO₄·7H₂O.

### chem-b01-q08 | points 3 | outcome B1.11 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Epsom salt is magnesium sulfate heptahydrate, MgSO₄·7H₂O. (1) Explain what the "· 7 H₂O" in the formula means. (2) Calculate the mass percentage of water in Epsom salt.
#### Answer
1. Each formula unit of the salt contains seven molecules of crystal water, built into the crystal lattice. 2. 51.2%.
#### Solution
1. The dot-reads "with": crystal water; the solid lattice holds seven H₂O molecules per MgSO₄ unit; they are released on heating (the white powder that remains is anhydrous MgSO₄).
2. M(MgSO₄·7H₂O) = 24.31 + 32.07 + 4 × 16.00 + 7 × 18.015 = 246.5 g mol⁻¹; the 7 waters weigh 126.1 g mol⁻¹.
3. Water percentage: 126.1 / 246.5 × 100% = 51.2%.

### chem-b01-q09 | points 4 | outcome B1.12 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Draw the Lewis structures (describe each atom's bonds and lone pairs) of: (1) ammonia, NH₃; (2) carbon dioxide, CO₂.
#### Answer
1. N with three N–H bonds and one lone pair. 2. A linear molecule O=C=O; each O has two lone pairs; the C has none.
#### Solution
1. N has 5 valence electrons, +3 × 1 from H gives 8 around N: three shared pairs (N–H) and one lone pair on N. The shape is trigonal pyramidal (steric number 4).
2. C (4 valence electrons) between two O atoms (6 each): each O shares two pairs, giving a double bond O=C=O; each O keeps two lone pairs; all atoms reach 8 electrons and the molecule is linear (steric number 2).
3. Count the electrons: NH₃ has 5 + 3 = 8; CO₂ has 4 + 12 = 16; the drawings use exactly those.

### chem-b01-q10 | points 4 | outcome B1.12–B1.13 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Draw the two resonance (mesomeric) structures of ozone, O₃, give the formal charge on each O atom and use resonance to explain why the two O–O bonds are equally long — between a single and a double bond in length.
#### Answer
O=O⁺–O⁻ ↔ ⁻O–O⁺=O; the central O carries +1, the singly bonded O carries −1, the doubly bonded O carries 0 in each structure. Resonance: the true structure is the average, so each O–O bond is "1.5 bonds" long and both are equal.
#### Solution
1. Ozone has 18 valence electrons (3 × 6). Drawing O–O–O with all octets complete forces one single bond and one double bond: O=O–O, and the mirror form.
2. Formal charge = valence electrons − (lone-pair electrons + half the bonding electrons): the central O has 6 − (2 + 3) = +1; the doubly bonded O has 6 − (4 + 2) = 0; the singly bonded O has 6 − (6 + 1) = −1.
3. Reality is not one structure or the other but their superposition (resonance): the bond in question is 50% of the time single and 50% double, so both O–O bonds are identical with bond order 1.5.

### chem-b01-q11 | points 3 | outcome B1.14 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Draw the structural formulas of all structural isomers with the molecular formula C₄H₁₀ and name them. Explain why these different molecules are called isomers.
#### Answer
CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ (butane) and CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃ (2-methylpropane); same molecular formula, different structural formulas.
#### Solution
1. C₄H₁₀ can be a straight chain of four carbons: butane.
2. or a chain of three carbons with a CH₃ branch on the middle one: 2-methylpropane.
3. Both have the same molecular formula C₄H₁₀ but a different arrangement of the atoms (structural formulas), so they are structural isomers — different substances with different properties (different boiling points: butane −0.5 °C, 2-methylpropane −12 °C, per Binas).

### chem-b01-q12 | points 4 | outcome B1.15–B1.16 | difficulty medium
#### Question
For each compound, name the functional/characteristic group and give the systematic name: (1) CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃, (2) CH₃–CH₂–CHO, (3) CH₃–CO–CH₃, (4) CH₃–CH₂–COO–CH₃.
#### Answer
1. Hydroxyl group; propan-2-ol. 2. Aldehyde group; propanal. 3. Keto (carbonyl) group; propanone. 4. Ester group; methyl propanoate.
#### Solution
1. –OH on carbon 2 of a three-carbon chain: a hydroxyl group makes an alkanol, numbered from the end nearest the –OH: propan-2-ol.
2. –CHO (C=O at the end of the chain, counted as C1): propanal.
3. C=O in the middle of the chain (ketone): propanone (propan-2-one; the position is trivial here).
4. –COO– links a propanoate part (3 C, from the acid) and a methyl part (from the alcohol): an ester, named alkyl alkanoate: methyl propanoate.

### chem-b01-q13 | points 4 | outcome B1.16 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Give the systematic IUPAC name of: (1) CH₃–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–OH, (2) CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃, (3) CH₃–CH₂–CO–CH₂–CH₃, (4) CH₃–CH₂–O–CH₂–CH₃.
#### Answer
1. 2-methylbutan-1-ol; 2. but-1-ene; 3. pentan-3-one; 4. ethoxyethane
#### Solution
1. Longest chain containing the –OH carbon: four carbons, numbered to give –OH the lowest number: butan-1-ol with a methyl branch on C2: 2-methylbutan-1-ol.
2. Four carbons with a double bond starting at C1 (numbered from the end nearest the double bond): but-1-ene.
3. Five carbons with C=O on carbon 3: pentan-3-one.
4. An ether (CCVX: alkoxy alkane): the ethoxy group –O–CH₂CH₃ on ethane: ethoxyethane.

### chem-b01-q14 | points 4 | outcome B1.16 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Give the structural formula of: (1) 3-methylpentanoic acid, (2) 1,2-dibromoethene, (3) propyl ethanoate, (4) cyclohexane.
#### Answer
1. CH₃–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–COOH; 2. CHBr=CHBr; 3. CH₃–COO–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃; 4. a ring of six CH₂ groups
#### Solution
1. A carboxylic acid is numbered from the COOH carbon: pentanoic acid is HOOC–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ with a methyl on C3: HOOC–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃ (written from the CH₃ end: CH₃–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–COOH).
2. Two bromines, one on each double-bond carbon: CHBr=CHBr (it has cis and trans forms, but only the connectivity is asked).
3. Ethanoate (2 C, acid part) with propyl (3 C, alcohol part): CH₃COOCH₂CH₂CH₃.
4. Six CH₂ groups joined in a ring: C₆H₁₂, the commonest cycloalkane.

### chem-b01-q15 | points 5 | outcome B1.14–B1.16 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Give the structural formulas and systematic names of all structural isomers of C₅H₁₂ (there are three), and explain why systematic names are needed at all here.
#### Answer
CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ (pentane); CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃ (2-methylbutane); C(CH₃)₄ (2,2-dimethylpropane). Without systematic rules, different people would name the same substance differently and one substance could be mistaken for another.
#### Solution
1. Straight chain of five C: pentane.
2. Chain of four C with one methyl branch: the branch sits on C2 (putting it on C3 gives the same molecule seen from the other end): 2-methylbutane.
3. Chain of three C with two methyl branches on the middle C: 2,2-dimethylpropane, C(CH₃)₄.
4. No other arrangement is possible without repeating one of these. All three are C₅H₁₂, so the molecular formula says nothing about which substance is meant; the systematic name tells the branching exactly, which also fixes different boiling points (36.1 °C, 27.7 °C, 9.5 °C — Binas, table of hydrocarbons: the more branches, the lower the boiling point).
