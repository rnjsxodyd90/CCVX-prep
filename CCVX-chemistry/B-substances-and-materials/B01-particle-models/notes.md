These are English explanations of every learning outcome in [README.md](README.md), and every topic is *drafted*: written from the CCVX and CvTE syllabi and standard VWO chemistry, not yet checked against an open source text or exam question (see [PLAN.md](../../PLAN.md) for the statuses).

Dutch terms are in brackets. A code such as *(CvTE M1.4)* points to the matching specification in the [CvTE 2027 syllabus](../../syllabus/cvte-syllabus-scheikunde-vwo-2027.pdf); Binas tables are named by subject, because their numbers differ between editions.

## B1.1 Atoms, free radicals and ions

### The nucleus

- An atom (*atoom*) has a tiny nucleus (*atoomkern*) of protons (*protonen*) and neutrons (*neutronen*), surrounded by electrons (*elektronen*) in the electron cloud (*elektronenwolk*). Nearly all of the mass is in the nucleus. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- The atomic number Z (*atoomnummer*) is the number of protons, and it decides which element the atom is. The mass number A (*massagetal*) is the number of protons plus neutrons, so the number of neutrons is A − Z. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- The notation puts the mass number top left and the atomic number bottom left: ³⁷₁₇Cl has 17 protons and 37 − 17 = 20 neutrons. Carbon-13 (C-13) is written ¹³C. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- Isotopes (*isotopen*) are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, so with different mass numbers: ¹²C, ¹³C and ¹⁴C all have 6 protons, but 6, 7 and 8 neutrons. Their electron clouds are the same, so they react the same way. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- The atomic masses in the Binas periodic table are averages over the natural isotopes, which is why they aren't whole numbers. Natural chlorine is 75.76% ³⁵Cl (34.97 u) and 24.24% ³⁷Cl (36.97 u): 0.7576 × 34.97 u + 0.2424 × 36.97 u = 35.45 u. *(CvTE M1.4, R&A1.2)*

### The electron cloud

- The electrons are arranged in shells (*schillen*) K, L, M, N, … (n = 1, 2, 3, 4, …). Shell n holds at most 2n² electrons: K 2, L 8, M 18, N 32. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- Up to calcium the shells fill in order and the outer shell never holds more than 8 electrons. So after argon (2,8,8) the next electron starts the N shell: potassium is (2,8,8,1) and calcium (2,8,8,2), although the M shell could take 18. The M shell only fills up to 18 from scandium to zinc. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- This list of electrons per shell is the simple electron configuration (*elektronenconfiguratie*): Na (2,8,1), Cl (2,8,7), Ar (2,8,8). The Dutch syllabus asks for it for periods 1–3, without s, p and d orbitals. *(CvTE M1.4)*

### Charge and mass

- The particles of an atom *(CvTE M1.4)*:

| Particle | Charge | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| proton | +1 (+1.602·10⁻¹⁹ C) | 1.673·10⁻²⁷ kg, about 1 u |
| neutron | 0 | 1.675·10⁻²⁷ kg, about 1 u |
| electron | −1 (−1.602·10⁻¹⁹ C) | 9.109·10⁻³¹ kg, 1/1836 of the proton mass |

- Charges are counted in elementary charges (1.602·10⁻¹⁹ C). The atomic mass unit u (*atomaire massa-eenheid*) is 1.661·10⁻²⁷ kg, one twelfth of the mass of a ¹²C atom. *(CvTE M1.3, M1.4)*
- An atom has as many electrons as protons, so it is neutral. *(CvTE M1.4)*

### Ions and free radicals

- An ion (*ion*) has lost or gained electrons; its charge is the number of protons minus the number of electrons. Na⁺ has 11 protons and 10 electrons (2,8); Cl⁻ has 17 protons and 18 electrons (2,8,8). *(CvTE M1.4)*
- Metal atoms lose their valence electrons, Na → Na⁺ + e⁻, and non-metals take up electrons: Cl₂ + 2 e⁻ → 2 Cl⁻. Both end up with the configuration of a noble gas (B1.3). *(CvTE M1.4, M1.6)*
- A composite ion (*samengesteld ion*) is a group of atoms held together by atomic bonds (covalent bonds, *atoombindingen*) that carries a charge as a whole, such as NH₄⁺, OH⁻ and SO₄²⁻ (B1.7, B1.12). *(CvTE M1.4, M1.15)*
- A free radical (*radicaal*) is a particle with an unpaired electron, written with a dot: H•, Cl•, CH₃•. Radicals form when an atomic bond breaks and each atom keeps one electron of the shared pair, for example in UV light: Cl₂ → 2 Cl•. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- Radicals are very reactive, because they react to pair their electron: CH₄ + Cl• → CH₃• + HCl. That step makes a new radical, which is how radical chain reactions work, including addition polymerisation ([D03](../../D-development-of-chemical-knowledge/D03-chemical-synthesis/notes.md)). *(CvTE M1.4, M5.1)*
- A molecule with an odd number of valence electrons is a radical too: nitrogen monoxide NO (5 + 6 = 11) and nitrogen dioxide NO₂ (5 + 2 × 6 = 17) from B1.6. *(CvTE M1.4)*
- **Trap:** a radical is not an ion. Cl• has 17 electrons and no charge; Cl⁻ has 18 electrons, a negative charge and a full octet.
- **Trap:** forming an ion changes only the number of electrons. The protons, and so the element and the mass number, stay the same.

## B1.2 The periodic table

- The periodic table (*periodiek systeem*) lists the elements in order of atomic number. A row is a period (*periode*) and a column a group (*groep*); the groups are numbered 1–18, as in the Binas periodic table. *(CvTE M1.5)*
- The position follows from the atomic number. The period number is the number of occupied shells, and in groups 1, 2 and 13–18 the last digit of the group number is the number of valence electrons (helium has 2). Sulfur, Z = 16, is in period 3 and group 16: S (2,8,6). *(CvTE M1.5, M1.6)*
- The named groups *(CvTE M1.5)*:

| Group | Name | Elements | Valence electrons |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | alkali metals (*alkalimetalen*) | Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs | 1 |
| 2 | alkaline earth metals (*aardalkalimetalen*) | Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba | 2 |
| 17 | halogens (*halogenen*) | F, Cl, Br, I | 7 |
| 18 | noble gases (*edelgassen*) | He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn | 8 (He 2) |

- Hydrogen sits above group 1 but is a non-metal, not an alkali metal. *(CvTE M1.5)*
- Metals (*metalen*) are on the left and at the bottom, and most elements are metals. Non-metals (*niet-metalen*) are at the top right. Along the staircase line from boron to astatine are the metalloids (*halfmetalen*), such as B, Si, Ge and As, with properties in between. *(CvTE M1.5)*
- Elements in a group react alike, because they have the same number of valence electrons (B1.3). Down a group the atoms get bigger, one shell per period, and the properties change gradually *(CvTE M1.5)*:
    - alkali metals get more reactive: lithium reacts calmly with water, sodium fast, and potassium so fast that the hydrogen catches fire. The reaction is 2 Na(s) + 2 H₂O(l) → 2 Na⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq) + H₂(g). Their melting points go down;
    - halogens get less reactive: F₂ is the strongest oxidator (oxidizing agent), as the standard electrode potentials in Binas show (F₂/F⁻ +2.87 V, Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 V, I₂/I⁻ +0.54 V). So chlorine takes electrons from iodide: Cl₂(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → 2 Cl⁻(aq) + I₂(aq). Their melting and boiling points go up: at room temperature F₂ and Cl₂ are gases, Br₂ is a liquid and I₂ a solid;
    - noble gases are monatomic gases that hardly react.
- **Trap:** reactivity goes up down group 1 but down group 17. Metals react by losing electrons, which gets easier as the outer shell gets further from the nucleus; halogens react by gaining one, which gets harder (B1.3).

## B1.3 Electron cloud and valence

- Valence electrons (*valentie-elektronen*) are the electrons in the outer shell. They decide how an atom bonds and reacts; the inner shells don't take part. *(CvTE M1.6)*
- The octet rule (*octetregel*): atoms gain, lose or share electrons until their outer shell holds 8, the configuration of a noble gas. Hydrogen ends with 2, like helium, and so does Li⁺. The noble gases already have a full outer shell, which is why they hardly react. *(CvTE M1.6)*
- Electrovalence (*elektrovalentie*) is the charge of the ion an atom forms. Metals give away their valence electrons; non-metals take up as many as they need for an octet. *(CvTE M1.6)*
- Covalence (*covalentie*) is the number of atomic bonds an atom forms: the number of electrons it is short of an octet (hydrogen: of 2). Its other valence electrons form lone pairs. *(CvTE M1.6)*

| Atom | Valence electrons | Electrovalence | Covalence | Lone pairs in a molecule |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na, K (group 1) | 1 | 1+ (Na⁺, K⁺) | – | – |
| Mg, Ca (group 2) | 2 | 2+ (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺) | – | – |
| Al (group 13) | 3 | 3+ (Al³⁺) | – | – |
| H | 1 | 1+ (H⁺) | 1 | 0 |
| C, Si (group 14) | 4 | – | 4 | 0 |
| N (group 15) | 5 | 3− (N³⁻) | 3 | 1 |
| O, S (group 16) | 6 | 2− (O²⁻, S²⁻) | 2 | 2 |
| F, Cl, Br, I (group 17) | 7 | 1− (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) | 1 | 3 |

- Group properties follow from this. Elements in one group have the same number of valence electrons, so the same ion charge and covalence: NaCl and KCl, H₂O and H₂S. Down a group the outer shell is further from the nucleus, so a metal loses its electrons more easily (more reactive) and a non-metal attracts an extra electron less strongly (less reactive). *(CvTE M1.6)*
- C, N, O and F never get more than 8 outer electrons. Larger atoms such as P and S can share more pairs, as in PCl₅ and SF₆. *(CvTE M1.6)*
- **Trap:** the number of valence electrons and the covalence are different numbers: oxygen has 6 valence electrons but forms 2 bonds.
- **Trap:** the charges of the Fe, Cu, Zn, Ag, Au, Hg, Pb, Sn and U ions don't follow from the octet rule. Learn them from the list in B1.7.

### Binary molecular compounds

- A binary molecular compound (*binaire moleculaire stof*) is built from two non-metals. Its name gives the number of each atom with a prefix: mono (1), di (2), tri (3), tetra (4), penta (5), hexa (6); the second element ends in -ide (Dutch *-ide*). The first element doesn't get mono, so CO is carbon monoxide. Usually the element further left in the periodic table comes first. *(CvTE M1.2)*
- Examples: N₂O dinitrogen monoxide (*distikstofmono-oxide*, laughing gas), N₂O₄ dinitrogen tetraoxide (also written tetroxide; *distikstoftetraoxide*), PCl₃ phosphorus trichloride (*fosfortrichloride*), CS₂ carbon disulfide (*koolstofdisulfide*), SF₆ sulfur hexafluoride (*zwavelhexafluoride*). *(CvTE M1.2)*

### Elements

- Elements are non-decomposable substances (*niet-ontleedbare stoffen*); what that means at particle level is in [B02](../B02-properties-and-models/notes.md). Their formulas *(CvTE M1.1, M1.8)*:
    - H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂ are diatomic molecules, and ozone O₃ is a second form of oxygen;
    - the noble gases are single atoms: He, Ne, Ar;
    - C, Si and B are written as the symbol, because they form an atomic lattice (*atoomrooster*);
    - sulfur and phosphorus form S₈ and P₄ molecules, but equations often write S and P, as in S(s) + O₂(g) → SO₂(g);
    - metals are written as the symbol: Fe(s), Cu(s), Hg(l).

## B1.8 Acids

- An acid (*zuur*) is a particle that gives off an H⁺ ion (a proton) to a base. In water the H⁺ goes to a water molecule, which becomes an oxonium ion H₃O⁺. *(CvTE R1.3, R1.5)*
- HCl, hydrogen chloride: strong and monoprotic (*eenwaardig*). Its solution is hydrochloric acid (*zoutzuur*), H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq): HCl(g) + H₂O(l) → H₃O⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). *(CvTE R1.4, R1.5)*
- HNO₃, nitric acid: strong and monoprotic: HNO₃ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + NO₃⁻. *(CvTE R1.5)*
- H₂SO₄, sulfuric acid: diprotic (*tweewaardig*), so each molecule can give off two H⁺, one at a time. The first step is complete (strong), H₂SO₄ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + HSO₄⁻, and the second is not: HSO₄⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + SO₄²⁻. *(CvTE R1.5)*
- H₂O + CO₂, written 'H₂CO₃' ('carbonic acid', *koolzuur*): carbon dioxide makes water weakly acidic, CO₂ + 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HCO₃⁻. The quotes are there because H₂CO₃ molecules hardly exist; the solution mainly contains dissolved CO₂. It is a weak diprotic acid: HCO₃⁻ can give off the second H⁺. *(CvTE R1.5)*
- H₃PO₄, phosphoric acid: weak and triprotic (*driewaardig*). It gives off its three H⁺ one at a time, via H₂PO₄⁻ and HPO₄²⁻ to PO₄³⁻; the first step is H₃PO₄ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + H₂PO₄⁻. *(CvTE R1.5)*
- CH₃COOH, acetic acid (ethanoic acid): weak and monoprotic, because only the H of the COOH group comes off: CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CH₃COO⁻, with K<sub>z</sub> = 1.8·10⁻⁵ (Binas). Other carbon acids (*carbonzuren*) react the same way. *(CvTE R1.5, M1.3)*
- In inorganic acids the acidic H is written first (HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, H₃PO₄); in carbon acids it is the H of the COOH group, written at the end. *(CvTE R1.5)*
- Strong acids react completely with water, weak acids only partly, in an equilibrium. The difference and acid-base reactions are in [C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md), K<sub>z</sub> and pH in [C05](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C05-chemical-equilibrium/notes.md); the Binas table of K<sub>z</sub> and K<sub>b</sub> values gives the strengths. *(CvTE R1.7, R1.8)*
- **Trap:** count only the acidic H atoms. CH₃COOH has four H atoms but is monoprotic, and CH₄ and CH₃–CH₂–OH aren't acids at all.

## B1.9 Bases

- A base (*base*) is a particle that takes up an H⁺ ion. It binds the H⁺ with a lone pair (B1.12). *(CvTE R1.3, R1.6)*
- NH₃, ammonia: a weak base, NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, with K<sub>b</sub> = 1.8·10⁻⁵ (Binas). Its solution is Dutch *ammonia*, NH₃(aq). *(CvTE R1.4, R1.6)*
- OH⁻, hydroxide: a strong base, as in caustic soda (*natronloog*, Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)): OH⁻ + H₃O⁺ → 2 H₂O. *(CvTE R1.4, R1.6)*
- O²⁻, oxide: a strong base that reacts completely with water, O²⁻ + H₂O → 2 OH⁻. So a metal oxide that dissolves gives a basic solution: CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq). *(CvTE R1.6)*
- CO₃²⁻, carbonate: a weak base that can take up two H⁺. With enough acid CO₂ escapes, so the mixture fizzes: CO₃²⁻ + 2 H₃O⁺ → CO₂ + 3 H₂O. *(CvTE R1.6)*
- HCO₃⁻, hydrogen carbonate: a weak base, HCO₃⁻ + H₃O⁺ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O, that can also act as an acid: HCO₃⁻ + OH⁻ → CO₃²⁻ + H₂O. A particle that can do both is an amphiprotic particle (*amfolyt*), like H₂O. *(CvTE R1.6)*
- In a salt the base is the negative ion: CO₃²⁻ in Na₂CO₃, HCO₃⁻ in NaHCO₃, O²⁻ in CaO, OH⁻ in NaOH. *(CvTE R1.6)*
- **Trap:** the OH group of an alcohol is not a hydroxide ion, so ethanol CH₃–CH₂–OH is not a base. And NH₃ is a base although it has no OH.

## B1.10 Salt formulas from ions

- A salt is neutral, so its ratio formula (*verhoudingsformule*) has as much positive as negative charge, with the ions in the smallest whole-number ratio. The positive ion comes first, and the numbers of ions are indices. *(CvTE M1.10)*
- Method: take the lowest common multiple of the two charges. For Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ that is 6 = 2 × 3 = 3 × 2, so 2 Al³⁺ and 3 SO₄²⁻ give Al₂(SO₄)₃. *(CvTE M1.10)*
- Put a composite ion in brackets when there is more than one of it: Ca(OH)₂, (NH₄)₂SO₄, Fe₂(SO₄)₃. A single composite ion needs no brackets: NaOH, NH₄Cl, KMnO₄. *(CvTE M1.10)*
- Name to formula: iron(III) sulfate is Fe³⁺ with SO₄²⁻, so Fe₂(SO₄)₃; tin(II) fluoride is Sn²⁺ with F⁻, so SnF₂. *(CvTE M1.10)*
- Formula to name: work out the metal's charge from the negative ions. In Pb(NO₃)₂ the two NO₃⁻ ions carry 2−, so the lead is Pb²⁺: lead(II) nitrate. In SnO₂ the two O²⁻ ions carry 4−, so it is tin(IV) oxide. *(CvTE M1.10)*
- Ions that a question gives work the same way: with chromate CrO₄²⁻ and dichromate Cr₂O₇²⁻, potassium chromate is K₂CrO₄ and potassium dichromate K₂Cr₂O₇. *(CvTE M1.10)*
- **Trap:** Ca(OH)₂ is one Ca²⁺ and two OH⁻; without the brackets, CaOH₂ would mean something else.
- **Trap:** simplify the ratio of the ions, never a composite ion itself. Pb⁴⁺ and O²⁻ give PbO₂, not Pb₂O₄, but Na⁺ and S₂O₃²⁻ give Na₂S₂O₃ with the S₂O₃ unchanged.

## B1.11 Crystal water

- A hydrate (*hydraat*) is a salt with water molecules built into its crystal lattice in a fixed ratio: the crystal water (*kristalwater*). The formula shows it after a dot: CuSO₄·5H₂O has five H₂O per CuSO₄. *(CvTE M1.11)*
- The name gives the number with a Greek prefix plus hydrate: copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate, in Dutch *koper(II)sulfaat-5-water*. The salt without crystal water is anhydrous (*watervrij*). *(CvTE M1.11)*
- Examples: CuSO₄·5H₂O is blue, while anhydrous CuSO₄ is white; CoCl₂·6H₂O is pink and anhydrous CoCl₂ blue; Na₂CO₃·10H₂O is washing soda (*soda*) and CaSO₄·2H₂O gypsum (*gips*). *(CvTE M1.11)*
- Heating drives the crystal water off: CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) → CuSO₄(s) + 5 H₂O(g). Adding water to the white powder turns it blue again, which is a test for water. *(CvTE M1.11)*
- When a hydrate dissolves, its crystal water simply joins the solvent: CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) + 5 H₂O(l). So 1 mol of the hydrate gives 1 mol Cu²⁺. *(CvTE M1.11)*
- The molar mass includes the crystal water: M(CuSO₄·5H₂O) = 159.6 + 5 × 18.02 = 249.7 g mol⁻¹. The mass percentage of water is 5 × 18.02 / 249.7 × 100% = 36.08%. *(CvTE M1.11, R&A1.2, R&A1.3)*
- Finding n from a measurement: suppose heating 2.50 g of a blue copper(II) sulfate hydrate leaves 1.60 g of white CuSO₄. Then n(H₂O) = 0.90 g / 18.02 g mol⁻¹ = 0.050 mol and n(CuSO₄) = 1.60 g / 159.6 g mol⁻¹ = 0.0100 mol, so there are 5.0 H₂O per CuSO₄: CuSO₄·5H₂O. Molar-mass calculations are in [C02](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C02-chemical-calculations/notes.md). *(CvTE M1.11, R&A1.2)*
- **Trap:** the dot doesn't multiply the formula; it adds water molecules. CuSO₄·5H₂O contains 9 O atoms and 10 H atoms, and forgetting the water in the molar mass is the usual mistake.

## B1.12 Lewis structures

- There are three ways to write a particle *(CvTE M1.12)*:
    - the molecular formula (*molecuulformule*) gives only the numbers of atoms: CH₄O;
    - the structural formula (*structuurformule*) shows which atoms are bonded, with a dash for each bonding pair: CH₃–OH;
    - the Lewis structure (*lewisstructuur*) is the structural formula plus all other valence electrons, so also the lone pairs (*niet-bindende elektronenparen*, *vrije elektronenparen*) and any formal charges (B1.13).
- A lone pair may be drawn as a dash or as two dots. *(CvTE Bijlage 3A)* Below, bonds are dashes and lone pairs dots: `··` above or below an atom, `:` beside it.
- Method *(CvTE M1.15)*:
    1. Add up the valence electrons of all atoms. Add one for each negative charge and take one off for each positive charge. Half of the total is the number of electron pairs.
    2. Put the atom with the highest covalence in the middle (C, N); H and the halogens go on the outside, with one bond each. Join the atoms with single bonds.
    3. Give the outer atoms lone pairs until they have an octet (H: 2 electrons), then put any pairs that are left on the central atom.
    4. If the central atom has no octet yet, turn a lone pair of a neighbour into a second or third bond.
    5. Check the total number of electrons, the octets (H: 2) and the formal charges.
- Starting from a structural formula you only add the lone pairs and charges. From a molecular formula, choose the skeleton with the covalences first (H 1, O 2, N 3, C 4). *(CvTE M1.15)*
- Electron counts for the examples *(CvTE M1.15, M1.17)*:

| Particle | Valence electrons | Bonding pairs | Lone pairs | Formal charges |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H₂O | 2 × 1 + 6 = 8 | 2 | 2 | none |
| NH₃ | 5 + 3 × 1 = 8 | 3 | 1 | none |
| CO₂ | 4 + 2 × 6 = 16 | 4 | 4 | none |
| N₂ | 2 × 5 = 10 | 3 | 2 | none |
| HCN | 1 + 4 + 5 = 10 | 4 | 1 | none |
| CH₃OH | 4 + 4 × 1 + 6 = 14 | 5 | 2 | none |
| NH₄⁺ | 5 + 4 × 1 − 1 = 8 | 4 | 0 | + on N |
| H₃O⁺ | 6 + 3 × 1 − 1 = 8 | 3 | 1 | + on O |
| OH⁻ | 6 + 1 + 1 = 8 | 1 | 3 | − on O |
| CO₃²⁻ | 4 + 3 × 6 + 2 = 24 | 4 | 8 | − on two O |
| NO₃⁻ | 5 + 3 × 6 + 1 = 24 | 4 | 8 | + on N, − on two O |
| CH₃COO⁻ | 2 × 4 + 3 × 1 + 2 × 6 + 1 = 24 | 7 | 5 | − on one O |
| O₃ | 3 × 6 = 18 | 3 | 6 | + on the middle O, − on an end O |

- Water, ammonia and carbon dioxide:

```text
    ··              ··          ··      ··
H – O – H       H – N – H       O = C = O
    ··              |           ··      ··
                    H
```

- Nitrogen, hydrogen cyanide and methanol:

```text
                                   H
                                   |   ··
:N ≡ N:       H – C ≡ N:       H – C – O – H
                                   |   ··
                                   H
```

- The ammonium ion, the oxonium ion and the hydroxide ion, with the charge on the atom that carries it (B1.13):

```text
    H
    |                ··            ··
H – N⁺ – H       H – O⁺ – H       :O⁻ – H
    |                |             ··
    H                H
```

- A radical (*radicaal*) has one single dot for its unpaired electron. The methyl radical CH₃• has three C–H bonds and a single electron on C; the chlorine radical Cl• has three lone pairs and a single electron, 7 valence electrons in both. *(CvTE M1.15)*

```text
    H
    |         ··
H – C•       :Cl•
    |         ··
    H
```

- A Lewis structure doesn't show the shape of the particle; the shape follows from VSEPR, in [B04](../B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md). *(CvTE M1.16)*

### Resonance (mesomeric) structures

- Sometimes the electrons of one particle can be placed in more than one correct way, with all atoms in the same positions. Each of these Lewis structures is a resonance (mesomeric) structure (*grensstructuur*), and the effect is called mesomerism (*mesomerie*). The structures are linked by a double-headed arrow. *(CvTE M1.15)*
- The real particle is in between the resonance structures: its electrons and charge are spread over several atoms, which makes it more stable. *(CvTE M1.15)*
- Carbonate CO₃²⁻ has three resonance structures. In reality the three C–O bonds are identical, between a single and a double bond, and each O carries on average two thirds of a negative charge. *(CvTE M1.15)*

```text
 ··       ··        ··       ··        ··      ··
:O⁻ – C = O    ↔   :O⁻ – C – O⁻:   ↔   O = C – O⁻:
 ··   |   ··        ··   ‖   ··        ··  |   ··
     :O⁻:               :O:               :O⁻:
      ··                                   ··
```

- Nitrate NO₃⁻ has the same three structures, with N⁺ in the middle (B1.13). *(CvTE M1.15)*
- The ethanoate ion (acetate) CH₃COO⁻ has two. Both C–O bonds are equal and the two O atoms share the negative charge. *(CvTE M1.15)*

```text
                          ··
     :O:                 :O⁻:
      ‖   ··              |   ··
CH₃ – C – O⁻:   ↔   CH₃ – C = O
          ··                  ··
```

- Benzene C₆H₆ has two, with the three double bonds in alternating places; each C also carries one H, not drawn here. The real molecule has six identical C–C bonds, so benzene is often drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside. *(CvTE M1.15)*

```text
  C = C           C – C
 /     \         //   \\
C       C   ↔   C       C
 \\   //         \     /
  C – C           C = C
```

- Resonance structures are also used to explain reactions, such as 1,4-addition ([C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md)). *(CvTE M1.15)*
- **Trap:** in resonance structures only electrons move, never atoms. Moving an H atom gives a different molecule, an isomer (B1.14). The double-headed arrow is not an equilibrium: the particle doesn't switch between the structures.
- **Trap:** include the charge in the electron count: CO₃²⁻ has 4 + 3 × 6 + 2 = 24 valence electrons, not 22.

## B1.13 Formal and partial charges

- The formal charge (*formele lading*) of an atom in a Lewis structure is its number of valence electrons as a free atom, minus the electrons in its lone pairs, minus its number of bonds (a double bond counts 2). The formal charges add up to the charge of the particle. *(CvTE M1.17)*
- Examples: in NH₄⁺ the N has 5 − 0 − 4 = +1, in H₃O⁺ the O has 6 − 2 − 3 = +1, and in OH⁻ the O has 6 − 6 − 1 = −1. *(CvTE M1.17)*
- In nitrate the N has 5 − 0 − 4 = +1, each single-bonded O 6 − 6 − 1 = −1 and the double-bonded O 6 − 4 − 2 = 0: in total +1 − 1 − 1 = −1, the charge of NO₃⁻. *(CvTE M1.17)*

```text
 ··        ··
:O⁻ – N⁺ = O
 ··   |    ··
     :O⁻:
      ··
```

- A neutral molecule can contain formal charges that cancel. In ozone O₃ the middle O has one lone pair and three bonds (6 − 2 − 3 = +1), and the end O with a single bond has three lone pairs (6 − 6 − 1 = −1). Ozone has two resonance structures. *(CvTE M1.15, M1.17)*

```text
··  ··   ··         ··   ··   ··
O = O⁺ – O⁻:   ↔   :O⁻ – O⁺ = O
··       ··         ··        ··
```

- Common cases *(CvTE M1.17)*:

| Atom | No formal charge | Formal charge +1 | Formal charge −1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 4 bonds | 3 bonds, no lone pair | 3 bonds, 1 lone pair |
| N | 3 bonds, 1 lone pair | 4 bonds | 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs |
| O | 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs | 3 bonds, 1 lone pair | 1 bond, 3 lone pairs |
| F, Cl, Br, I | 1 bond, 3 lone pairs | – | no bond, 4 lone pairs (the ion) |

- Put each formal charge on the atom that carries it. *(CvTE Bijlage 3A)*
- Partial charges (*partiële ladingen*), δ+ and δ−, show that a polar atomic bond (*polaire atoombinding*) shares its electron pair unequally. The atom that attracts the pair more strongly (higher electronegativity, *elektronegativiteit*) gets δ−, the other δ+: H<sup>δ+</sup>–O<sup>δ−</sup> in water and alcohols, H<sup>δ+</sup>–N<sup>δ−</sup> in ammonia and amines, C<sup>δ+</sup>=O<sup>δ−</sup> in a carbonyl group. They too are written on the atoms. *(CvTE M1.17, M3.1, Bijlage 3A)*
- Which bonds count as polar is in [B03](../B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md), and whether the whole molecule is a dipole in [B04](../B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md). *(CvTE M3.1, M3.5)*
- **Trap:** a formal charge is a whole charge from counting electrons; a partial charge is a fraction from unequal sharing and changes nothing in the Lewis structure. Formal charge is bookkeeping: H₃O⁺ has its + on the O, although O is the most electronegative atom.

## B1.14 Structural isomerism

- Isomers (*isomeren*) are different compounds with the same molecular formula. In structural isomers (*structuurisomeren*) the atoms are connected in a different order, so the structural formulas differ. *(CvTE M1.18)*
- **Chain isomers** have a different carbon skeleton: butane CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ and 2-methylpropane CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₃ are both C₄H₁₀. *(CvTE M1.18)*
- **Position isomers** have the same group in a different place: propan-1-ol CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–OH and propan-2-ol CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃ are both C₃H₈O. *(CvTE M1.18)*
- **Functional-group isomers** have different groups: ethanol CH₃–CH₂–OH and methoxymethane CH₃–O–CH₃ (both C₂H₆O); propanal CH₃–CH₂–CHO and propanone CH₃–CO–CH₃ (both C₃H₆O); propene CH₂=CH–CH₃ and cyclopropane (both C₃H₆). *(CvTE M1.18)*
- Isomers are different substances with different properties. Ethanol boils at about 78 °C and methoxymethane at about −24 °C, because only ethanol forms hydrogen bridges (hydrogen bonds, *waterstofbruggen*, [B03](../B03-bonds-and-properties/notes.md)). *(CvTE M1.18)*
- To find all isomers, vary the chain first, then the position of the group, then the kind of group. C₅H₁₂ has three: pentane, 2-methylbutane and 2,2-dimethylpropane. C₄H₁₀O has seven: four alkanols (butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol, 2-methylpropan-2-ol) and three ethers (1-methoxypropane, 2-methoxypropane, ethoxyethane). *(CvTE M1.18)*
- Stereo-isomerism (cis-trans isomers and mirror images) is in [E02](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md). *(CvTE M1.18)*
- **Trap:** the same molecule drawn the other way round, or with a bent chain, is not an isomer. A chain of five C atoms drawn with a bend is still pentane, and "1-methylbutane" is just pentane.

## B1.15 Functional groups

- A functional or characteristic group (*functionele groep, karakteristieke groep*) is a group of atoms that gives a class of compounds its typical reactions and properties *(CvTE M1.13)*:

| Group | Class | Example |
|---|---|---|
| C=C, double bond | alkenes (*alkenen*) | ethene CH₂=CH₂ (*etheen*) |
| C≡C, triple bond | alkynes (*alkynen*) | ethyne CH≡CH (*ethyn*) |
| –OH, hydroxyl | alkanols, alcohols (*alkanolen, alcoholen*) | ethanol CH₃–CH₂–OH (*ethanol*) |
| C=O at the end of the chain, –CHO | alkanals, aldehydes (*alkanalen, aldehyden*) | ethanal CH₃–CHO (*ethanal*) |
| C=O between two C atoms | alkanones, ketones (*alkanonen, ketonen*) | propanone CH₃–CO–CH₃ (*propanon*) |
| –COOH, carboxyl | alkanoic acids, carbon acids (*alkaanzuren, carbonzuren*) | ethanoic acid CH₃–COOH (*ethaanzuur*) |
| –NH₂, amino | amines (*amine*) | methanamine CH₃–NH₂ (*methaanamine*) |
| C–O–C, ether | ethers, alkoxy alkanes (*ethers*) | methoxymethane CH₃–O–CH₃ (*methoxymethaan*) |
| C–COO–C, ester | esters, alkoxy alkanoates (*esters*) | methyl ethanoate CH₃–COO–CH₃ (*methylethanoaat*) |
| C–CO–NH–C, amide or peptide | amides (*amiden*) | N-methylethanamide CH₃–CO–NH–CH₃ (*N-methylethaanamide*) |
| C–X, X = F, Cl, Br, I | halogen alkanes (*halogeenalkanen*) | chloromethane CH₃–Cl (*chloormethaan*) |

- In a structural formula, draw the C=O of a carbonyl group in full, write the H atoms of OH and NH₂ groups, and draw the bond from the O or N to the carbon skeleton. *(CvTE Bijlage 3A)*
- Esters and amides form in condensation reactions ([C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md)); the amide bond between amino acids in proteins is the peptide bond ([G01](../../G-society-chemistry-and-technology/G01-chemistry-of-life/notes.md)). *(CvTE M1.13)*
- **Trap:** read a group as a whole. COOH is one carboxyl group, not a carbonyl group plus a hydroxyl group. In an ester the O between the C=O and the next C is part of the ester group, not an ether. CONH is an amide, not a ketone plus an amine.
- **Trap:** an aldehyde has its C=O at the end of the chain, with an H on that C; a ketone has it between two C atoms. Write an aldehyde group as –CHO, never –COH, which looks like an alcohol.

## B1.16 Naming carbon compounds

### Rules

- The systematic IUPAC name (*systematische naam*) is built in five steps *(CvTE M1.14)*:
    1. Find the longest carbon chain that contains the functional group: the main chain (*hoofdketen*). Its length gives the stem: meth (1), eth (2), prop (3), but (4), pent (5), hex (6), hept (7), oct (8), non (9), dec (10).
    2. The class gives the ending: -ane, -ene, -yne, -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -amine (Dutch -aan, -een, -yn, -ol, -al, -on, -zuur, -amine).
    3. Number the chain from the end that gives the functional group the lowest number (locant, *plaatsnummer*). With no suffix group, give the side chains and halogen atoms the lowest numbers.
    4. Side chains (*zijketens*) such as methyl and ethyl, and the halogens as fluoro, chloro, bromo and iodo (Dutch *fluor, chloor, broom, jood*), go in front in alphabetical order, each with its locant. Di-, tri- and tetra- count identical groups and are ignored in the alphabetical order.
    5. Separate numbers by commas, and numbers from letters by hyphens: 2,2-dimethylpropane.
- Current IUPAC names put the locant just before the part it belongs to: but-2-ene, propan-2-ol, pentan-3-one (Dutch *but-2-een, propaan-2-ol, pentaan-3-on*). Exams use these names, but correct older names such as 2-butene and 2-propanol (*2-buteen, 2-propanol*) are accepted. *(CvTE Bijlage 3C)*
- No locant is needed where only one position is possible: ethene, propene, propanone, butanone. The -al and -oic acid groups are always at C1, as in propanal and butanoic acid, and their C counts in the stem. *(CvTE M1.14)*
- For a double or triple bond the locant is the lower number of its two C atoms: but-1-ene CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃. *(CvTE M1.14)*
- In a structural formula every C must clearly have four bonds. The C–H bonds may be drawn as dashes without the H symbols, or left out when the right number of H atoms is written at each C. *(CvTE Bijlage 3A)*

### Rings, benzene, ethers and esters

- Cycloalkanes (*cycloalkanen*) are rings, named with cyclo- and the alkane of the same number of C atoms. They are CₙH₂ₙ: cyclopropane C₃H₆, cyclohexane C₆H₁₂. With one side chain no locant is needed: methylcyclohexane C₇H₁₄. *(CvTE M1.14)*
- Benzene (*benzeen*) C₆H₆ is the parent of the benzene derivatives, which name the groups on the ring as prefixes: methylbenzene C₆H₅–CH₃ (toluene), chlorobenzene C₆H₅–Cl, and with two groups 1,4-dimethylbenzene. As a side chain the C₆H₅– group is phenyl (*fenyl*): phenylethene C₆H₅–CH=CH₂ (styrene). C₆H₅–OH keeps the name phenol (*fenol*). *(CvTE M1.14)*
- Ethers are named as alkoxy alkanes (CCVX's term): the shorter part with -oxy as a prefix, the longer part as the alkane. CH₃–O–CH₂–CH₃ is methoxyethane (*methoxyethaan*); from three C in the alkane part on, add a locant: 1-methoxypropane, 2-methoxypropane. *(CvTE M1.14)*
- Esters (CCVX: alkoxy alkanoates) are named alkyl alkanoate: first the alkyl group from the alcohol, then the acid part with -oate (Dutch *-oaat*, all in one word). CH₃–COO–CH₃ is methyl ethanoate (*methylethanoaat*), made from ethanoic acid and methanol: CH₃–COOH + HO–CH₃ ⇌ CH₃–COO–CH₃ + H₂O ([C01](../../C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/notes.md)). *(CvTE M1.14)*
- Trivial names (*triviale namen*) you will still meet: acetic acid (ethanoic acid), acetone (propanone), formaldehyde (methanal), acetylene (ethyne), toluene (methylbenzene), styrene (phenylethene), chloroform (trichloromethane). *(CvTE M1.3)*
- **Trap:** the longest chain may be drawn with a bend. CH₃–CH(CH₂–CH₃)–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ has a main chain of six C atoms, so it is 3-methylhexane, not 2-ethylpentane.
- **Trap:** number from the end nearest the functional group, not the side chain: CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₂–OH is 3-methylbutan-1-ol, not 2-methylbutan-4-ol.
- **Trap:** in an ester the alcohol part is named first but usually written last. H–COO–CH₂–CH₃ is ethyl methanoate and CH₃–COO–CH₃ is methyl ethanoate; they are isomers (C₃H₆O₂).

## CCVX versus the Dutch syllabus

- **In both:** everything above, with the same lists of non-metals, metals and ions, apart from the points below.
- **Numbering:** CCVX B1.8–B1.9 are CvTE R1.5–R1.6, and CCVX B1.12–B1.16 are M1.15 (with M1.12), M1.17, M1.18, M1.13 and M1.14; match by content, using the codes at each bullet. The Dutch M1 also holds VSEPR (M1.16) and stereo-isomerism (M1.18), which CCVX puts in [B04](../B04-bonds-structures-and-properties/notes.md) and [E02](../../E-innovation-and-chemical-research/E02-selectivity-and-specificity/notes.md).
- **Dutch syllabus only:** the places of the alkali and alkaline earth metals (M1.5); Lewis structures of radicals (M1.15); naming any binary molecular compound from its systematic name (M1.2), where CCVX lists CO₂, CO, HCl, NO₂, NO, SO₂ and SO₃ by name; and names with up to two kinds of functional group (M1.14, with at most one ester or ether group and amides not named), where CCVX allows one. *(CvTE M1.2, M1.5, M1.14, M1.15)*
- **CCVX only:** the shell names K, L and M with their maximum numbers of electrons (2, 8, 18). The CvTE replaced them with a "simple" electron configuration, the number of electrons per shell for periods 1–3, without s, p and d orbitals. CCVX also has its own terms "alkoxy alkanoates" for esters (named alkyl alkanoate, as in methyl ethanoate) and "alkanoic amines" for amines. *(CvTE M1.4)*
