# B3 — Bonds and properties: practice

Ten original questions on the B3 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Use Binas: the table of electronegativity and the tables of melting/boiling points.

### chem-b03-q01 | points 2 | outcome B3.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Name the lattice type and the particles each lattice is built from for: (1) iron, (2) sodium chloride, (3) ice, (4) diamond.
#### Answer
1. Metallic lattice (metal atoms in a sea of free electrons). 2. Ionic lattice (Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions). 3. Molecular lattice (H₂O molecules). 4. Atomic lattice (C atoms, each covalently bonded to four neighbours).
#### Solution
1. Metals: metal lattice with a metallic bond (lattice ions plus delocalised electrons).
2. Salts: ionic lattice with ion bonds between positive and negative ions.
3. Ice: H₂O molecules in a molecular lattice, held together by hydrogen bridges (a stronger kind of intermolecular bond).
4. Diamond: every C atom is covalently bonded to four others in one giant network — an atomic lattice, the reason for diamond's hardness.

### chem-b03-q02 | points 3 | outcome B3.3 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Describe how the ion bond in magnesium oxide (MgO) forms, starting from neutral Mg and O atoms, including the shell structure before and after.
#### Answer
Mg (2, 8, 2) gives its two outer electrons to O (2, 6): Mg²⁺ (2, 8) and O²⁻ (2, 8) form, and the charges of opposite sign attract — that attraction through the whole lattice is the ion bond.
#### Solution
1. Magnesium has two valence electrons; it reaches a noble gas configuration by giving them off: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2 e⁻.
2. Oxygen has six valence electrons; it reaches a noble gas configuration by taking up two: O + 2 e⁻ → O²⁻.
3. The positive and negative ions attract each other from all directions and stack into an ionic lattice; the ion bond is this strong attraction between opposite charges, repeated throughout the lattice — the reason for the high melting point of salts.

### chem-b03-q03 | points 3 | outcome B3.3 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Use the electronegativity values from Binas (H 2.1, O 3.5) to explain why the O–H atomic bond in water is a polar covalent bond, and where the partial charges δ+ and δ− sit.
#### Answer
Oxygen attracts the shared electron pair more strongly than hydrogen (3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4 difference), so the shared pair sits closer to O: the O end is partially negative (δ−) and the H end partially positive (δ+): a polar atomic bond.
#### Solution
1. In an atomic bond (covalent bond) the atoms share electron pairs.
2. Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts such a shared pair. With O (3.5) much higher than H (2.1), the pair leans to the O side.
3. The result is a polar atomic bond with partial charges: δ− on O and δ+ on each H. These δ charges explain why a water molecule is a dipole (with the bent shape, see B04) and why hydrogen bridges form between water molecules.

### chem-b03-q04 | points 3 | outcome B3.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Put the following substances in order of increasing boiling point and explain the order in terms of the bonds between the particles: methane (CH₄), butane (C₄H₁₀), methanol (CH₃OH), water (H₂O).
#### Answer
Methane (about −162 °C) < butane (about 0 °C) < methanol (65 °C) < water (100 °C).
#### Solution
1. Methane and butane are apolar, so only Van der Waals (London) forces act between the molecules; the larger the molecule (more electrons, larger surface), the stronger the Van der Waals attraction, so butane boils far above methane.
2. Methanol molecules form hydrogen bridges through their –OH group as well as Van der Waals bonds, so boiling costs more energy than with butane of comparable size: higher boiling point.
3. Water forms two hydrogen bridges per molecule (two lone pairs on O and two H atoms), much more strongly networked than methanol (one H it can donate): the highest boiling point.
4. Ordering principle: atomic bonds are strongest, then ion bonds, then hydrogen bridges, then dipole-dipole, then Van der Waals — but Van der Waals overtakes for large molecules.

### chem-b03-q05 | points 3 | outcome B3.1 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Explain with the lattice model why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity, while molten or dissolved sodium chloride does.
#### Answer
In the solid ionic lattice the ions are fixed in place: no freely moving charge carriers. When molten or dissolved, the ions move freely and carry the current.
#### Solution
1. Conduction needs mobile charge carriers.
2. In solid NaCl the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are locked in the ionic lattice — charged, but not mobile: no conduction.
3. Melting or dissolving (hydration, see C01) breaks the lattice positions loose; the ions are then free to move toward the electrodes, so the melt and the solution conduct.

### chem-b03-q06 | points 4 | outcome B3.5–B3.6 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Ethanol mixes with water in all proportions; hexane forms a separate layer on water. Explain this difference with the concepts hydrophilic, hydrophobic, hydrogen bridge and Van der Waals bond.
#### Answer
Ethanol has a polar –OH group that forms hydrogen bridges with water (hydrophilic), and its hydrocarbon part is short: so the favourable bonds with water outweigh the cost; Hexane is apolar: it can only make Van der Waals bonds with water, which do not compensate for the hydrogen bridges between water molecules that would have to break (hydrophobic), so the liquids separate.
#### Solution
1. Dissolving a molecular substance means: breaking the bonds between its own molecules and some bonds between water molecules, and forming new bonds between dissolved molecules and water.
2. Ethanol's –OH group forms hydrogen bridges with water (hydrophilic part); dissolving replaces H-bonds with H-bonds, so mixing costs little net energy and happens in all proportions.
3. Hexane has no such group: mixing would break many hydrogen bridges between water molecules and give only weak Van der Waals bonds in return — unfavourable, so hexane stays a separate, less dense, layer (hydrophobic).
4. A large molecule can have both parts: soaps and emulsifiers work just because of that combination (see B02).

### chem-b03-q07 | points 3 | outcome B3.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Graphite and diamond are both pure carbon with an atomic lattice, yet graphite conducts electricity while diamond does not. Use this example to explain what the "intermediate lattice types" of outcome B3.2 mean.
#### Answer
Graphite sits between an atomic lattice and a metallic lattice: each C atom bonds to three neighbours with atomic bonds, and each atom's fourth electron is delocalised over its layer, like metallic free electrons — so graphite conducts. Diamond uses all four electrons in atomic bonds and does not conduct.
#### Solution
1. In diamond every C atom forms four atomic bonds (covalent bonds) with its valence electrons fixed in shared pairs: no mobile charge carriers, so not conducting — the extreme case of an atomic lattice.
2. In graphite every C atom forms three atomic bonds; the fourth electron per atom moves freely through the layer — that part behaves like a metallic bond. Between the layers only Van der Waals bonds act.
3. Graphite therefore combines traits of two lattice types: an intermediate form. The same idea appears in alloys with polar bonds and in substances such as polar plastics: real materials often sit between the four textbook extremes.

### chem-b03-q08 | points 3 | outcome B3.7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Barium ions are toxic, yet patients swallow a "barium meal" of BaSO₄ for stomach X-rays. (1) Use the Binas table of salt solubilities to explain why this is safe. (2) Why could BaCl₂ never be used for this? (3) Give the precipitation equation with which BaSO₄ could also be detected in a solution of sulfate.
#### Answer
1. BaSO₄ is insoluble (Binas): it passes through the gut as solid particles and almost no Ba²⁺ ends up in solution. 2. BaCl₂ is soluble: it would release toxic Ba²⁺(aq) ions. 3. Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s)
#### Solution
1. The solubility table shows BaSO₄ is insoluble (s); safety here follows directly from the practical use of a salt's solubility: the X-ray contrast comes from the solid suspension, and the barium leaves the body unchanged.
2. BaCl₂ is soluble (o in the table): it would dissolve as free Ba²⁺ ions and poison the patient. Toxicity of an ion only matters when it is free in solution.
3. Testing sulfate with a barium salt solution: Ba²⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq) → BaSO₄(s) — a white precipitate that does not dissolve in acid (this distinguishes sulfate from carbonate, whose BaCO₃ precipitate does dissolve in acid).

### chem-b03-q09 | points 4 | outcome B3.4 | difficulty hard
#### Question
The boiling points of propane and of methanol are −42 °C and 65 °C respectively, although their molar masses differ by only about 14 u. Explain in detail why methanol boils so much higher than propane. In your answer, state which bonds exist between the molecules of each substance and which of those bonds have to break when the liquid boils.
#### Answer
Between propane molecules only Van der Waals bonds act; between methanol molecules both Van der Waals bonds and hydrogen bridges act (via the –OH group). The hydrogen bridge is much stronger than the Van der Waals bond, so boiling methanol — which breaks all intermolecular bonds — costs much more energy: a much higher boiling point.
#### Solution
1. Propane (C₃H₈) is apolar: the molecules attract each other only through Van der Waals (London) forces. Its boiling means breaking these weak bonds, so at −42 °C.
2. Methanol (CH₃OH) has a polar –OH group: besides Van der Waals bonds, methanol molecules form hydrogen bridges (from the lone pairs of O to the δ+ H of a neighbour's –OH).
3. Boiling breaks all bonds between the molecules (never the atomic bonds inside them). Methanol's extra, much stronger hydrogen bridges raise the energy cost far beyond what a similar-sized molecule without –OH would need, hence 65 °C.
4. The counter-example logic is the same as for HF vs the other hydrogen halides: check with Binas that HCl (−85 °C) is far below HF (20 °C), again a hydrogen-bridge effect.

### chem-b03-q10 | points 4 | outcome B3.3 | difficulty hard
#### Question
Use the electronegativity values from Binas (C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, H 2.1) to answer: (1) which of the bonds C–O, N–H, O–H and F–H is most polar, with the direction of the partial charges; (2) which hydrogen halide bond, H–F or H–Cl (Cl 3.2, or from your Binas), is more polar; (3) explain why a C–H bond is treated as apolar.
#### Answer
1. F–H is most polar (difference 1.9): δ− on F, δ+ on H. 2. H–F (difference 1.9) is more polar than H–Cl (difference about 1.1). 3. The C–H difference is only 0.4, so the partial charges are negligible.
#### Solution
1. Polarity grows with the difference in electronegativity: C–O 1.0, N–H 0.9, O–H 1.4, F–H 1.9 — largest for F–H. The more electronegative atom carries δ− (F), the less electronegative δ+ (H).
2. H–F (4.0 − 2.1 = 1.9) beats H–Cl (about 3.2 − 2.1 = 1.1): H–F is the more polar bond, which is also why HF molecules form hydrogen bridges and HCl molecules (weak dipole) boil far lower.
3. C (2.5) and H (2.1) differ by only 0.4: the shared pair is shared almost equally, so C–H bonds carry effectively no partial charge. This is why hydrocarbons are apolar and hydrophobic.
