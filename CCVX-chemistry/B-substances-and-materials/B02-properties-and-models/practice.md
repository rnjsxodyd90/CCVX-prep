# B2 — Properties and models: practice

Ten original questions on the B2 learning outcomes, in CCVX style. Substance data: Binas tables of melting/boiling points and density.

### chem-b02-q01 | points 2 | outcome B2.1 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A colourless liquid boils at 78 °C and has a density of 0.79 g mL⁻¹ at 20 °C. (1) With which table in Binas can you identify it, and which substance comes out? (2) Explain why macroscopic properties such as these are called "substance properties".
#### Answer
1. The table of (organic) substance properties (melting point, boiling point, density): ethanol. 2. Because every pure substance has its own fixed set of them, so they characterise (identify) the substance.
#### Solution
1. Binas lists melting point, boiling point and density for many substances; the combination 78 °C + 0.79 g mL⁻¹ matches ethanol.
2. Substance properties (*stofeigenschappen*) belong to the substance itself, not to the shape or amount of the sample: 1 mL and 1 L of ethanol boil at the same temperature. That is what makes them usable for identification.

### chem-b02-q02 | points 3 | outcome B2.2 | difficulty medium
#### Question
A technician tests a solder wire: it does not melt at one temperature but softens gradually between 183 °C and 190 °C. (1) What does this tell you about the solder? (2) Explain the difference between a melting point and a melting range at the micro level.
#### Answer
1. It is a mixture (an alloy, of tin and lead for example). 2. A pure substance has one kind of particle and one bond strength to break, so it melts at one temperature; in a mixture the different surroundings melt apart at different temperatures, giving a range.
#### Solution
1. Gradual softening over a range is the mark of a mixture; solder is a tin-lead (or tin-silver) alloy.
2. Micro level: a pure substance is a uniform lattice; melting breaks one kind of bond at one temperature. In a mixture the particles have different neighbours (different bond strengths), so parts melt at different temperatures: a melting range.

### chem-b02-q03 | points 2 | outcome B2.3 | difficulty easy
#### Question
Explain at the micro level why air is a mixture but distilled water is a pure substance, although both look uniform.
#### Answer
Air consists of different kinds of particles (N₂ and O₂ molecules, plus more) that are not bonded in a fixed ratio; distilled water consists of one kind of particle (H₂O molecules) only.
#### Solution
1. Micro-level criterion: one kind of particle = pure substance; two or more kinds of particles = mixture.
2. Air: N₂ and O₂ molecules (and Ar, CO₂, …) move independently through each other, and their ratio varies from place to place — a mixture.
3. Distilled water: only H₂O molecules — a pure substance. Looking uniform (homogeneous) does not prove purity; the particle picture does.

### chem-b02-q04 | points 3 | outcome B2.4 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Explain the difference between a decomposable and a non-decomposable substance at the micro level, using water and oxygen as your examples.
#### Answer
A decomposable substance (compound) consists of particles in which two or more kinds of atoms are bonded — H₂O molecules contain H and O atoms, so water can be decomposed into hydrogen and oxygen. A non-decomposable substance consists of one kind of atom — O₂ molecules contain only O atoms, so oxygen cannot be decomposed into other substances.
#### Solution
1. Water: electrolysis splits the H₂O molecules (breaking the atomic bonds) into H₂ and O₂ molecules — new substances: water is a compound (decomposable).
2. Oxygen: whatever you do chemically, O₂ contains only oxygen atoms; it can only give other substances by reacting, not by splitting: non-decomposable.
3. So the deciding question is "how many kinds of atom inside the particles?", not "how many atoms".

### chem-b02-q05 | points 3 | outcome B2.5 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Explain the difference between a molecular substance and a salt at the micro level, and use it to explain why a solution of sugar in water does not conduct electricity while a solution of sodium chloride does.
#### Answer
A molecular substance consists of neutral molecules; a salt consists of ions in a lattice. Conductivity needs mobile charge carriers: a sugar solution has only neutral particles; a salt solution has ions that move freely between the electrodes.
#### Solution
1. Sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) is molecular; when it dissolves, whole neutral molecules separate and are hydrated — the solution contains no charged particles that can move.
2. NaCl is a salt: an ionic lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻. On dissolving, the ions separate and are hydrated; in the solution the charged ions move freely.
3. Electric current through a liquid is transported by moving ions, so the salt solution conducts and the sugar solution does not — a classic micro-to-macro explanation (see B04 for conductivity in general).

### chem-b02-q06 | points 3 | outcome B2.6 | difficulty easy
#### Question
A student stirs sodium chloride into water until some stays on the bottom, however long she stirs. (1) What are the names of the solution at that moment and of the undissolved part? (2) Classify the mixture in the beaker as homogeneous or heterogeneous. (3) How could she dissolve the rest (two ways)?
#### Answer
1. The solution is saturated; the solid left over is the residue (the precipitate/sediment). 2. Liquid plus solid: heterogeneous. 3. Dilute it (add water) or raise the temperature (many salts dissolve better warm).
#### Solution
1. When no more salt can dissolve at that temperature, the solution is saturated; before that it is unsaturated.
2. A clear saturated salt solution is homogeneous, but the beaker as a whole (solution + solid on the bottom) is heterogeneous — two phases.
3. More solvent dissolves more salt; and the solubility of most salts rises with temperature (Binas may give solubility data), so heating then stirring dissolves the remainder. On cooling, the extra salt crystallises out again.

### chem-b02-q07 | points 3 | outcome B2.6 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Oil and water do not mix: they form two layers. With a little washing-up liquid, one milky liquid forms. (1) What is such a mixture called? (2) What role does the washing-up liquid play? (3) Explain at the micro level how the washing-up-liquid molecules achieve this.
#### Answer
1. An emulsion; 2. it is the emulsifier; 3. its molecules have a hydrophobic (apolar) tail that sits in the oil droplets and a hydrophilic (polar/ionic) head that sticks out into the water, so the droplets do not join and separate again.
#### Solution
1. Small droplets of one liquid in another: an emulsion (milk is the everyday example).
2. The added substance that keeps the droplets apart is the emulsifier.
3. An emulsifier molecule is two-ended: the apolar (hydrocarbon) part joins the oil phase with which it forms Van der Waals bonds; the polar or charged end forms hydrogen bridges with water. Sitting on the surface of each droplet, the molecules keep the oil droplets from merging, so the emulsion stays mixed.

### chem-b02-q08 | points 3 | outcome B2.6 | difficulty medium
#### Question
Pure copper is softer and more easily shaped than brass (copper with zinc). (1) To which class of mixtures does brass belong? (2) Explain at the micro level why the alloy is harder than the pure metal.
#### Answer
1. An alloy (a mixture of metals). 2. The differently sized zinc atoms disturb the regular copper lattice (lattice errors), so layers of atoms can no longer slide over each other as easily — the material becomes harder.
#### Solution
1. A melted-together mixture of metals: an alloy; brass = copper + zinc.
2. In pure copper all atoms are the same size in a regular lattice; layers slide past each other easily (malleability, see B04).
3. In brass, zinc atoms (a different size) take lattice positions and distort it (lattice errors); the sliding is blocked, so the alloy is harder and deforms less easily.

### chem-b02-q09 | points 3 | outcome B2.7 | difficulty medium
#### Question
In a described study, two white powders are both found to melt sharply at 159 °C. The researcher concludes: "They must be the same substance." Give (1) an argument for and (2) an argument against this conclusion, and (3) one further investigation you would do to decide.
#### Answer
1. For: a sharp melting point is the mark of a pure substance, and the same melting point agrees with being the same substance. 2. Against: different substances can share a melting point (a property alone never proves identity), and purity of each powder is not itself proof of sameness. 3. Compare a second, independent property — e.g., their infrared/mass spectra or their behaviour on a chromatogram; if those match too, the conclusion is strong.
#### Solution
1. Substance properties identify a substance only in combination; a match in one property is a clue, not proof.
2. Two different substances can coincide in one property (many substances melt near 159 °C).
3. An independent method (spectrum, chromatography, flame test, a second property like density or solubility) either confirms or rejects; this reasoning with evidence is exactly what outcome B2.7 asks.

### chem-b02-q10 | points 4 | outcome B2.5–B2.6 | difficulty hard
#### Question
A saturated solution of calcium hydroxide (limewater) contains only about 1.6 g of Ca(OH)₂ per litre. (1) Explain how a saturated solution can still be dilute. (2) Calcium hydroxide is a salt: what particles does the solution contain, and does it conduct electricity? (3) If you shake chalk (solid CaCO₃) with water you get a different kind of mixture: name it, and explain the difference from a solution.
#### Answer
1. Saturation only says "as much dissolved as possible"; a poorly soluble salt reaches that at a very low concentration — so saturated can be very dilute. 2. Ca²⁺(aq) and OH⁻(aq) ions; yes, it conducts (weakly). 3. A suspension: undissolved solid particles that settle or can be filtered, unlike a solution where the particles are dissolved at ion/molecule level.
#### Solution
1. "Saturated" and "concentrated" describe different things: saturated compares with the maximum (the solubility), concentrated compares with water content. A poorly soluble substance like Ca(OH)₂ has a low maximum, so its saturated solution is dilute.
2. A salt dissolves as separate hydrated ions: Ca²⁺(aq) + 2 OH⁻(aq); mobile ions carry current, so limewater conducts (with few ions, weakly).
3. Chalk is nearly insoluble (Binas, table of salt solubilities): shaking gives solid particles dispersed in water that settle out — a suspension. It is heterogeneous, can be filtered, and does not conduct appreciably; a solution is homogeneous with dissolved ions/molecules.
