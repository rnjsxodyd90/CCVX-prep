# CCVX Biology — Mock exam 1 (original)

**Time: 3 hours · 7 questions · 73 points**

This is an original mock exam in the style of the CCVX preliminary examination in biology: open questions in contexts, with the points per sub-question shown. All contexts, data and questions are invented for practice; nothing is copied from CCVX or CvTE exams.

**Allowed:** BINAS or Biodata (5th edition or later, without notes); a non-graphing calculator with logarithms; one paper dictionary. Where a question needs the genetic code or another BINAS table, a small excerpt is given.

**Grading:** the marking scheme at the end has one bullet per point. Grade yourself strictly: a bullet is scored only when that element is in your answer.

---

## Question 1 — Spider silk from bacteria (11 points)

Spider silk is extremely strong. Bacteria with a recombinant plasmid can produce a spider-silk protein. The gene used for this was made as cDNA from mRNA of the silk gland of an orb-weaver spider.

Part of the mRNA that is translated reads:

5'-AUG GGU GCA GGU GGU GCA UAG-3'

Genetic code (from the BINAS table): AUG Met, GGU Gly, GCA Ala, AGC Ser, UAG stop, UGA stop.

**1.** The researchers used cDNA, not the gene as it lies in the spider's chromosomes. Explain why bacteria can express the cDNA correctly but not the chromosomal gene. (2 points)

**2.** Give the amino-acid sequence that is translated from the mRNA fragment shown. (2 points)

**3.** Give the anticodon of the tRNA that delivers the second glycine of this fragment. (1 point)

**4.** The plasmid contains, besides the cDNA, two more DNA elements: a promoter and a marker gene for antibiotic resistance. Explain the function of each of these two elements. (2 points)

**5.** In a variant of the silk gene, a codon for glycine, GGU, has become AGC. Give the consequence for the amino-acid sequence and name this type of mutation. (2 points)

**6.** Explain why the bacteria produce silk protein with the same amino-acid sequence as the spider itself. (2 points)

## Question 2 — An isotonic sports drink (9 points)

During long efforts an athlete loses water and salts in sweat. A sports drink is "isotonic" when the total concentration of dissolved particles equals that of blood plasma, so the drink neither holds water back in the intestine nor draws water into it. Blood plasma has an osmotic value of about 300 mmol of dissolved particles per litre.

A sports drink contains, per litre, 50 mmol glucose and 100 mmol NaCl. Glucose dissolves as molecules; each NaCl gives two ions, Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

**1.** Show with a calculation whether this sports drink is isotonic with blood plasma. (2 points)

**2.** The drink reaches the small intestine, where the glucose concentration in the gut contents is often lower than in the cells of the intestinal wall. Yet glucose is absorbed completely. Name the type of transport by which glucose enters these cells, and explain what makes that transport possible. (2 points)

**3.** After the exercise the athlete drinks a large amount of plain water. The blood plasma briefly becomes more dilute. Explain the effect this has on the red blood cells. (3 points)

**4.** Some athletes swallow salt tablets during exercise. Explain why swallowing several salt tablets without water is unwise. (2 points)

## Question 3 — Training camp in the mountains (11 points)

A cycling team trains for three weeks at 2300 m altitude. The air pressure there is lower than at sea level, so each litre of air contains fewer O₂ molecules.

**1.** The chemical receptors in the aorta register the lower O₂ concentration of the arterial blood. Describe the regulation cycle that follows, from receptor to effect, and give the direct effect on breathing. (3 points)

**2.** During the weeks at altitude the number of red blood cells per litre of blood rises. Explain, naming the hormone and the organs involved, how this increase comes about, and explain its use for the athlete. (3 points)

**3.** The table gives the percentage saturation of haemoglobin with O₂ at different O₂ pressures (pO₂).

| pO₂ (kPa) | 4.0 | 8.0 | 10.7 | 13.3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hb saturation (%) | 75 | 92 | 95 | 98 |

At sea level the pO₂ in the alveoli is about 13.3 kPa; at the training camp it is about 10.7 kPa. In working muscle the pO₂ is about 4.0 kPa. Calculate for both altitudes how many percent of the haemoglobin saturation is delivered to the muscles, and use the result to explain why altitude air costs the athlete capacity. (3 points)

**4.** During a climb the heart rate of a cyclist is 176 beats per minute and the cardiac output is 22 L per minute. Calculate the stroke volume, in mL. (2 points)

## Question 4 — Norovirus on a ferry (11 points)

Several passengers on a ferry become ill with vomiting and diarrhoea caused by norovirus. Norovirus is a virus that enters the body through the mouth. A week later, most patients have recovered.

**1.** Noroviruses reach the stomach with food. Give two barrier defences of the body that can stop them before or just after entry, and explain briefly how each works. (2 points)

**2.** Macrophages in the intestinal wall take up virus particles. Describe what a macrophage then does with the antigens of the virus, and name the type of molecules with which it presents them. (3 points)

**3.** During a first infection, antiviral antibodies appear in the blood only after several days. During a second infection with the same virus, the antibody level rises much faster and to a higher value. Explain this difference, naming the cells involved. (3 points)

**4.** Blood from a crew member contains IgM antibodies against norovirus but almost no IgG antibodies. Another crew member has IgG antibodies and no IgM. Explain what each result says about the moment of infection. (2 points)

**5.** A passenger asks for antibiotics against norovirus. Explain why a doctor will not prescribe them. (1 point)

## Question 5 — Field beans without fertiliser (11 points)

A grower cultivates field beans without nitrogen fertiliser. The beans carry bacteria of the genus *Rhizobium* in root nodules. Measurements on one hectare give:

- nitrogen fixed via the root nodules and taken up by the plants: 180 kg N per year;
- nitrogen leaving the field in the harvested beans: 120 kg N per year;
- nitrogen in the plant residues (roots, stems, leaves) that are ploughed under: 60 kg N per year.

**1.** Name the process that the *Rhizobium* bacteria carry out, and give the form of nitrogen compound that the plant receives. (2 points)

**2.** Show with a calculation whether the total amount of nitrogen in the soil of this field rises or falls per year, if no other nitrogen sources or losses occur. (3 points)

**3.** After the residues are ploughed under, their nitrogen becomes available to the next crop as nitrate. Describe the steps and name the groups of bacteria involved, from residue protein to nitrate. (3 points)

**4.** In a wet winter, part of the nitrate is lost from the field. Give two processes that cause this loss, and explain the harm this loss can cause in a nearby ditch. (3 points)

## Question 6 — Bold foxes in the city (10 points)

Urban foxes are much less shy than foxes in the countryside. Researchers compare the behaviour of foxes in three areas. They measure the escape distance: the distance to which a person can approach before the fox flees.

| Area | Mean escape distance (m) | Number of foxes measured |
|---|---|---|
| City park | 4 | 26 |
| Edge of town | 11 | 18 |
| Forest | 28 | 15 |

To describe fox behaviour near humans, the researchers noted six behaviour elements, such as *freeze*, *approach*, *beg for food* and *flee*, in the order in which each fox showed them.

**1.** Give the name of such an ordered list of behaviour elements, and give one reason why researchers use it. (2 points)

**2.** Explain which of the three fox groups is the most strongly habituated to humans, using the table. (2 points)

**3.** Explain, in terms of chances of survival and reproduction, how bold behaviour can become more common in urban foxes within a few dozen generations. In your answer, use the concepts hereditary variation and selection pressure. (3 points)

**4.** A researcher claims: "Urban foxes have learned not to fear humans, so the trait is acquired, not hereditary." Argue that the trait can be both learned and hereditary. (3 points)

## Question 7 — A resistant worm (10 points)

Livestock farms use the medicine doramectin against a parasitic worm. The worm is diploid. In one gene, an allele d gives resistance; the allele D gives sensitivity. The resistance allele is recessive.

**1.** The resistance allele appeared in the worm population before the medicine was ever used. Explain why mutations such as this allele arise regardless of whether the medicine is present. (2 points)

**2.** The table shows the measured allele frequency of d in the worm population of one farm.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency of d | 0.05 | 0.08 | 0.15 | 0.26 | 0.41 |

Explain the change in the frequency of d, using the concept of selection pressure. (2 points)

**3.** Assume that in year 5 the population is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium for this gene. Calculate the percentage of worms that is heterozygous (Dd) in year 5. Give your answer as a whole percent. (3 points)

**4.** When the farm has not used doramectin for two years, the frequency of d slowly decreases. Give a possible explanation in terms of fitness. (2 points)

**5.** Some vets prescribe a combination of two medicines with different mechanisms instead of one medicine. Explain how this strategy lowers the chance that resistance develops. (1 point)

## Marking scheme

### Question 1 — Spider silk from bacteria (11 points)

**1.** The chromosomal gene contains introns, and bacteria cannot splice (cannot remove introns from pre-mRNA). cDNA is copied from mature mRNA, so it consists of exons only and is translated correctly.
• chromosomal gene has introns; bacteria cannot splice (1) • cDNA = exons only, from mature mRNA (1)

**2.** Met–Gly–Ala–Gly–Gly–Ala, then stop.
• codons read in the 5'→3' order with the table (1) • six amino acids correct, stop at UAG (1)

**3.** 3'-CCA-5' (anticodon complementary and antiparallel to codon 5'-GGU-3').
• 3'-CCA-5' (1)

**4.** Promoter: the DNA sequence to which the bacterial RNA polymerase binds; without it the cDNA is not transcribed. Marker gene: gives antibiotic resistance, so the researcher can select exactly those bacteria that took up the plasmid.
• promoter = binding/start signal for transcription in the bacterium (1) • marker = selection of transformed bacteria (1)

**5.** One glycine becomes a serine (Gly → Ser); this is a point mutation of the type substitution, with a missense effect.
• Gly replaced by Ser (1) • substitution / missense (1)

**6.** The genetic code is (almost) universal: every codon codes for the same amino acid in spider and bacterium, so translation in the bacterium gives the same sequence.
• universal code (1) • same codon–amino-acid assignment in both (1)

### Question 2 — An isotonic sports drink (9 points)

**1.** Dissolved particles: glucose 50 mmol (molecules) + NaCl 100 × 2 = 200 mmol (ions) → 250 mmol L⁻¹. That is less than 300 mmol L⁻¹, so the drink is not isotonic but slightly hypotonic.
• ×2 for the two ions (1) • 250 < 300 with conclusion (1)

**2.** Active transport (cotransport with Na⁺). The intestinal cell uses carrier proteins and energy (ATP, via the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) so glucose can be absorbed against its concentration gradient.
• active transport with carrier/transport protein (1) • energy (ATP) allows uptake against the gradient (1)

**3.** The diluted plasma becomes hypotonic relative to the red blood cells, so water flows into the cells by osmosis — down the osmotic gradient through the semipermeable membrane. Animal cells have no cell wall, so the cells swell and can burst (haemolysis).
• plasma hypotonic / higher water potential than the cells (1) • water enters by osmosis (1) • cells swell or burst, no cell wall (1)

**4.** The tablets raise the osmotic value of the blood plasma, so water is drawn out of the body cells and the thirst centre is stimulated; without extra water, the cell dehydration worsens even though the athlete still feels he "replaces salts".
• osmotic value of plasma rises (1) • cells lose water / worsened dehydration without extra water (1)

### Question 3 — Training camp in the mountains (11 points)

**1.** Chemical receptors in the aorta (and carotid arteries) detect the lower O₂ concentration and send impulses to the breathing centre in the medulla oblongata; the medulla increases the signals along nerves to the intercostal muscles and diaphragm, so the breathing rate and depth (ventilation) rise immediately.
• receptors detect lower O₂ and signal the medulla (1) • medulla drives respiratory muscles (1) • effect: faster and/or deeper breathing (1)

**2.** The kidneys detect the lower O₂ and release more EPO (erythropoietin); EPO stimulates the bone marrow to produce more red blood cells. More red blood cells means more haemoglobin per litre of blood, so more O₂ can be transported per litre, which benefits endurance.
• EPO from the kidneys (1) • more red blood cells from bone marrow (1) • more haemoglobin → more O₂ transport (1)

**3.** Sea level: 98 − 75 = 23 percentage points delivered. Camp: 95 − 75 = 20 points. The delivery per heartbeat is thus smaller at altitude (about 13% less per litre of blood), because the alveolar pO₂ is lower, so the blood loads less O₂ in the lungs while the muscle unloads as usual; the athlete must raise heart rate or breathe more for the same power.
• both differences computed (23 and 20) (1) • cause: lower alveolar pO₂ → lower saturation after the lungs (1) • consequence explained (1)

**4.** Stroke volume = cardiac output ÷ heart rate = 22 000 mL ÷ 176 = **125 mL**.
• output ÷ rate (1) • 125 mL (1)

### Question 4 — Norovirus on a ferry (11 points)

**1.** Any two of: stomach acid (low pH kills many pathogens); mucus layer of the airways and intestine (traps particles; cilia move it out); intact skin (mechanical barrier at the hands/mouth); lysozyme in saliva and tears (breaks down bacterial/viral envelopes).
• two barriers named (1) • working explained for both (1)

**2.** The macrophage engulfs the virus particles (phagocytosis), digests them, and presents the antigens on its surface with MHC II molecules, so that helper T cells that fit the antigen are activated.
• phagocytosis (1) • antigens presented (1) • MHC II / to helper T cells (1)

**3.** In the first infection, fitting B cells are activated, divide and form plasma cells and memory cells; the antibody level rises slowly. In a second infection the memory cells respond at once: they divide quickly and many plasma cells form, so the antibody level rises faster and higher.
• first infection: activation of B cells → plasma cells (1) • memory cells remain (1) • faster and stronger response via memory cells (1)

**4.** IgM appears early in an infection and disappears again: this crew member has a recent/current infection. IgG appears later and remains: the other crew member had the infection earlier (or was vaccinated) and is no longer in the early phase.
• IgM = recent infection (1) • IgG = earlier infection / after-phase (1)

**5.** Antibiotics work only against bacteria (they attack bacterial cell structures or ribosomes); a virus has no such targets, so antibiotics do nothing against norovirus.
• virus, not a bacterium; no target for antibiotics (1)

### Question 5 — Field beans without fertiliser (11 points)

**1.** Nitrogen fixation: N₂ from the air is bound (reduced) to ammonium (NH₄⁺), which the plant can use.
• nitrogen fixation named (1) • ammonium as product for the plant (1)

**2.** Input: 180 kg N fixed per year. Export: 120 kg N in beans. Balance: +180 − 120 = +60 kg N per ha per year (the 60 kg in residues stays on the field), so the soil nitrogen rises.
• input and export identified (1) • 180 − 120 (1) • +60 kg ha⁻¹: soil N rises (1)

**3.** Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down the proteins of the residues and release ammonium: ammonification. Nitrifying bacteria then oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrate in two steps: nitrification (nitrite-forming, then nitrate-forming bacteria).
• ammonification by decomposers (1) • nitrification to nitrate (1) • two bacterial steps (ammonium → nitrite → nitrate) (1)

**4.** Leaching: nitrate dissolves and is washed down below the roots and out of the field. Denitrification: in wet, oxygen-poor soil, denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate to N₂ (and N₂O). In the ditch the nitrate (and phosphate) can cause eutrophication: algal growth, then decomposition and oxygen depletion, harming the water life.
• leaching (1) • denitrification (1) • eutrophication explained (1)

### Question 6 — Bold foxes in the city (10 points)

**1.** An ethogram. It makes behaviour observation objective and comparable between observers and between groups.
• ethogram (1) • systematic/objective recording or comparability (1)

**2.** The city-park foxes: their escape distance is smallest (4 m against 11 and 28 m), so they tolerate people at the shortest distance — the strongest habituation. (The large numbers measured make the comparison reliable.)
• smallest escape distance = strongest habituation (1) • correct use of the table values (1)

**3.** Within the fox population there is hereditary variation in boldness. In the city the selection pressure favours bold animals: they reach food near humans more often, so they survive and reproduce better, passing the alleles for boldness on more often; over generations the frequency of those alleles — and of bold behaviour — rises.
• hereditary variation present (1) • selection pressure in the city favours bold foxes (1) • more reproduction → allele frequency rises over generations (1)

**4.** Learning: each fox can habituate during its life to humans and may copy bold behaviour from its mother or group members. Heredity: the ease with which this habituation happens, and the basic level of boldness, are influenced by genes, as the population change over generations shows. So the observable behaviour is learned in part, while its potential and starting level are hereditary.
• habituation/learning during life (1) • hereditary component in boldness (1) • both argued consistently (1)

### Question 7 — A resistant worm (10 points)

**1.** Mutations arise as chance errors in DNA replication (or through mutagenic factors), without direction: a mutation does not arise "because" the medicine would be useful. The allele was already present at low frequency before use of the drug.
• mutations are chance events (1) • arise without purpose/regardless of later use (1)

**2.** While doramectin is used, worms with allele d (homozygous dd survive, Dd partly) survive the treatment more often than sensitive worms and reproduce more: the medicine is the selection pressure, so the frequency of d rises year after year.
• survival advantage for worms with d under treatment (1) • medicine = selection pressure → d rises (1)

**3.** freq(d) = q = 0.41, so p = 0.59. Heterozygotes: 2pq = 2 × 0.59 × 0.41 = 0.4838 ≈ **48%**.
• p = 1 − 0.41 = 0.59 (1) • 2pq computed (1) • 48% (1)

**4.** Without the medicine the resistance allele no longer gives an advantage; if dd worms have a slightly lower fitness (grow or reproduce a little slower) than DD worms, selection works against d and its frequency falls back. Alleles also drift, but a consistent decline points to a fitness cost of resistance.
• no advantage without treatment, possible fitness cost (1) • selection now against d (1)

**5.** A worm must then carry resistance to both medicines at once to survive; such double mutations are far rarer than single ones, so hardly any worm passes the combination and resistance hardly builds up.
• simultaneous resistance to both is much rarer (1)
