### bio-mock2-q01 | points 2 | outcome E2.3 | difficulty easy

#### Question

At a dermatology research centre, technicians culture keratinocytes (skin cells) from a small skin biopsy. In a well-fed culture the cells divide regularly. A mole removed from the same patient turns out to contain a tumour whose cells divide much faster and without the normal restraints.

One keratinocyte in the culture is about to divide. Describe the phases of the cell cycle this cell passes through from one division to the next, and give for each phase the most important event.

#### Answer

The cell passes through interphase (G1: growth and normal work; S: DNA replication; G2: preparation for division) and then the M phase: mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) followed by division of the cytoplasm into two daughter cells.

#### Solution

From one division to the next the cell cycle runs: G1 — the cell grows and performs its function; S — the DNA is replicated, so each chromosome then consists of two chromatids; G2 — the cell prepares for division (organelles and proteins duplicated); M — mitosis, in which the sister chromatids are separated by the spindle (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase), followed by cytokinesis giving two genetically identical daughter cells.
*Marking:* • interphase with S/DNA replication as main event (and growth in G1/G2) (1) • M phase = mitosis ending in two daughter cells (1)

### bio-mock2-q02 | points 2 | outcome E2.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

At a dermatology research centre, technicians culture keratinocytes (skin cells) from a small skin biopsy. In a well-fed culture the cells divide regularly. A mole removed from the same patient turns out to contain a tumour whose cells divide much faster and without the normal restraints.

For a comparison study the researchers also look at cells in the patient's testis that form sperm cells. Explain in which phase the division that forms sperm cells differs from the division in the skin culture, and relate this difference to the function of each type of division.

#### Answer

In the first division of meiosis (meiosis I) the homologous chromosomes — not the sister chromatids — are separated, so the chromosome number is halved (diploid → haploid); in mitosis sister chromatids separate and the number stays the same.

#### Solution

In mitosis (skin culture) sister chromatids separate in anaphase, giving two diploid, genetically identical daughter cells — fitting for growth and repair of skin. In meiosis, homologous chromosome pairs separate in anaphase I and the chromatids only in meiosis II, giving haploid cells that are genetically different — fitting for gamete formation: fertilisation restores the diploid number and mixing of paternal and maternal material creates variation.
*Marking:* • homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I vs sister chromatids in mitosis → halving of chromosome number (1) • linked to function: mitosis = identical cells for growth/repair, meiosis = haploid gametes (1)

### bio-mock2-q03 | points 2 | outcome E2.5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

At a dermatology research centre, technicians culture keratinocytes (skin cells) from a small skin biopsy. In a well-fed culture the cells divide regularly. A mole removed from the same patient turns out to contain a tumour whose cells divide much faster and without the normal restraints.

In the tumour cells, the checkpoints at which the cell cycle is halted when the DNA is damaged no longer work. Describe how the cell cycle is normally regulated, and explain what can go wrong with that regulation when a tumour develops.

#### Answer

The cell cycle is regulated by internal checkpoints (e.g. the cell pauses at the end of G1 and G2 to check the DNA and the conditions) and by signals from adjacent cells; if the genes for these control proteins are damaged by mutations, the cell skips the checkpoints and divides uncontrolled.

#### Solution

Normally the cycle is driven and braked by regulatory proteins: checkpoints (G1, G2, spindle checkpoint in M) verify DNA integrity, cell size and correct attachment of chromosomes, and adjacent/crowded cells inhibit further division (contact inhibition); a damaged cell can also be removed by apoptosis. If mutations disable the genes for these brake/repair proteins (or keep accelerator genes switched on), the cell divides despite errors and crowding: a tumour arises. In the tumour cells described, the damaged-DNA checkpoints no longer work, so the cycle runs on uncontrolled.
*Marking:* • regulation via checkpoints/control signals (incl. from adjacent cells) described (1) • failure by mutations in those control genes → division despite damaged DNA / no contact inhibition (1)

### bio-mock2-q04 | points 2 | outcome E2.8 | difficulty easy

#### Question

At a dermatology research centre, technicians culture keratinocytes (skin cells) from a small skin biopsy. In a well-fed culture the cells divide regularly. A mole removed from the same patient turns out to contain a tumour whose cells divide much faster and without the normal restraints.

The pathologist must decide whether the tumour is benign or malignant. Describe the difference between a benign and a malignant tumour, and explain why a malignant tumour is more dangerous.

#### Answer

A benign tumour stays encapsulated at its site of origin and does not invade other tissue; a malignant tumour grows invasively into surrounding tissue and its cells can spread via blood or lymph to form metastases — which is why it is more dangerous.

#### Solution

Benign: slow or demarcated growth, cells stay together, no spread; usually removable and less dangerous. Malignant: invasive growth into neighbouring tissue, cells detach and travel via blood or lymph vessels, founding secondary tumours (metastases) elsewhere in the body; these damage vital organs and are harder to treat.
*Marking:* • benign = demarcated, no spread vs malignant = invasive growth (1) • metastasis via blood/lymph explained as the danger (1)

### bio-mock2-q05 | points 2 | outcome E2.11 | difficulty hard

#### Question

At a dermatology research centre, technicians culture keratinocytes (skin cells) from a small skin biopsy. In a well-fed culture the cells divide regularly. A mole removed from the same patient turns out to contain a tumour whose cells divide much faster and without the normal restraints.

The cultured normal skin cells stop dividing after about fifty divisions and die; the cultured tumour cells keep dividing indefinitely. Explain this difference, using the role of telomeres in cell ageing, and explain why errors in DNA remain uncorrected more often where there is uncontrolled cell division.

#### Answer

In normal cells telomeres shorten at every division until they are so short that the cell stops dividing (the Hayflick limit, about fifty divisions) and ages and dies; tumour cells keep their telomeres long (telomerase stays active), so they can divide indefinitely — and because their cycle runs on despite DNA errors, errors are copied instead of corrected and accumulate.

#### Solution

Telomeres are the protective ends of chromosomes; because DNA replication cannot copy the very end, they shorten at each division. After roughly fifty divisions they are too short: the cell enters senescence and dies by apoptosis. Tumour cells reactivate telomerase, which rebuilds the telomeres, so there is no division limit. Moreover, in uncontrolled division the checkpoints and apoptosis fail, so cells with DNA errors are not halted, repaired or removed; the errors are replicated with the DNA and pile up over the many divisions, producing ever more abnormal cells.
*Marking:* • telomere shortening limits normal cells to ~50 divisions; no limit in tumour cells via telomerase (1) • with uncontrolled division, errors survive uncorrected and accumulate (1)

### bio-mock2-q06 | points 2 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A breeder keeps the Helder burrow-rat, a small burrowing mammal. Three traits are studied:

- coat colour: brown (B) is dominant over cream (b); autosomal gene;
- coat pattern: striped (S) is dominant over plain (s); autosomal gene on a different chromosome;
- tail marking: ringed tail (X^R) is dominant over uniformly coloured tail (X^r); gene on the X chromosome.

A family of burrow-rats is followed over three generations. Phenotypes:

- generation I: male 1 (brown) and female 2 (brown);
- their offspring, generation II: male 3 (brown), female 4 (cream), female 5 (brown);
- female 5 mates with male 6 (cream); their pups, generation III: three brown and two cream.

Give the genotypes of female 2, female 4 and female 5 for the coat-colour gene, and explain your deduction.

#### Answer

Female 2 is Bb and female 4 is bb; female 5 is Bb.

#### Solution

Female 4 is cream, the recessive phenotype, so her genotype is bb; she received one b allele from each brown parent, so both parents (male 1 and female 2) are heterozygous: female 2 is Bb. Female 5 is brown, so B_; from the cross with male 6 (bb) cream pups (bb) appear, which needed a b allele from female 5, so female 5 is Bb. (With Bb × bb, brown : cream ≈ 1 : 1, consistent with three brown and two cream pups.)
*Marking:* • female 4 = bb ⇒ female 2 = Bb (1) • female 5 = Bb from the cream pups in generation III (1)

### bio-mock2-q08 | points 3 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A breeder keeps the Helder burrow-rat, a small burrowing mammal. Three traits are studied:

- coat colour: brown (B) is dominant over cream (b); autosomal gene;
- coat pattern: striped (S) is dominant over plain (s); autosomal gene on a different chromosome;

Two animals with the genotype BbSs are mated. Calculate the expected percentage of pups that are cream with a plain pattern. Show your reasoning with the expected phenotypic ratio for this cross.

#### Answer

For two unlinked genes, BbSs × BbSs gives the phenotypic ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, so cream with plain pattern (bbss) is 1/16 = 6.25%.

### bio-mock2-q09 | points 3 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty hard

#### Question

A breeder keeps the Helder burrow-rat, a small burrowing mammal. Three traits are studied:

- coat colour: brown (B) is dominant over cream (b); autosomal gene;
- coat pattern: striped (S) is dominant over plain (s); autosomal gene on a different chromosome;
- tail marking: ringed tail (X^R) is dominant over uniformly coloured tail (X^r); gene on the X chromosome.

A ringed-tailed male is mated with a ringed-tailed female whose own father had a uniformly coloured tail. Calculate the probability that their first pup is a son with a uniformly coloured tail. Show your reasoning.

#### Answer

The female is X^R X^r, the male is X^R Y; the probability of a son with a uniformly coloured tail is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 (25%).

#### Solution

The female is ringed-tailed but her father had a uniform tail (genotype X^r Y), so she must have received X^r from him: she is heterozygous X^R X^r. The male is ringed: X^R Y. Cross: daughters get X^R from the father and are always ringed; sons get Y from the father and the X from the mother — half X^R (ringed), half X^r (uniform). P(son) = 1/2; P(son inherits X^r) = 1/2; combined probability = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 25%.
*Marking:* • female X^R X^r and male X^R Y correctly deduced (1) • sons inherit X only from the mother: 1/2 of sons uniform (1) • 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 25% (1)

### bio-mock2-q10 | points 2 | outcome B3.4.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A student eats a breakfast of scrambled eggs fried in butter and a glass of full-cream milk. Researchers follow the digestion of the egg protein and the butter fat through her digestive tract.

The egg protein first comes into contact with digestive enzymes in the stomach. Describe where and by which enzymes the protein is broken down further along the tract, and name the end products that are absorbed.

#### Answer

In the stomach, pepsin (in gastric juice, acid pH) splits the protein into shorter chains; in the small intestine, trypsin (and other peptidases) from the pancreas and the intestinal wall complete the breakdown to amino acids, the end products that are absorbed.

#### Solution

Protein digestion begins in the stomach, where pepsin — which works best at the low pH of gastric juice — cuts the protein into peptide fragments. In the duodenum and small intestine the pancreas secretes trypsin (and other proteases), and peptidases of the intestinal wall break the fragments down further, in the near-neutral pH maintained there. The end products, amino acids, are absorbed through the intestinal wall into the blood.
*Marking:* • pepsin in stomach (acid) → polypeptides (1) • trypsin/peptidases in small intestine → amino acids (1)

### bio-mock2-q11 | points 2 | outcome B3.4.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A student eats a breakfast of scrambled eggs fried in butter and a glass of full-cream milk. Researchers follow the digestion of the egg protein and the butter fat through her digestive tract.

The butter fat arrives as large droplets in the duodenum. Explain the role of bile in the digestion of this fat, making clear whether bile itself is an enzyme or not.

#### Answer

Bile (made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder) is not an enzyme: it emulsifies the fat droplets into tiny droplets, enlarging the surface on which the enzyme lipase can work.

#### Solution

Bile is produced by the liver and released via the gall bladder into the duodenum. Bile salts do not catalyse a chemical breakdown — bile contains no digestive enzymes — but emulsify the large fat droplets into a fine suspension. This greatly increases the surface area where fat and water meet, so pancreatic lipase can hydrolyse the fat to fatty acids and glycerol (and mono-glycerides) much faster.
*Marking:* • bile emulsifies fat into small droplets / larger surface (1) • bile contains no enzyme; lipase does the chemical breakdown (1)

### bio-mock2-q12 | points 2 | outcome B3.4.2 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A student eats a breakfast of scrambled eggs fried in butter and a glass of full-cream milk. Researchers follow the digestion of the egg protein and the butter fat through her digestive tract.

The products of digestion are absorbed in the small intestine. Explain how the structure of the small intestine is adapted to this absorption; involve the intestinal villi in your answer, and state into which fluid the breakdown products of fat first enter.

#### Answer

The small intestine is long and its wall is folded into villi (with microvilli), giving a huge surface with a thin epithelium; each villus has a dense capillary network that takes up amino acids (and sugars) into the blood, while the breakdown products of fat first enter the lymph via the central lymph vessel.

#### Solution

Adaptations to absorption: great length, folds and villi (and microvilli on the epithelial cells) enlarge the uptake surface enormously; the wall is only one cell layer thick, so the diffusion distance is short; each villus contains blood capillaries and a lymph vessel (lacteal). Amino acids (and monosaccharides) pass into the blood capillaries; resynthesised fat droplets are too large and enter the lymph of the lacteal first, reaching the blood only later via the thoracic duct.
*Marking:* • villi/microvilli → large surface, thin wall (1) • fat products into the lymph (lacteal), other products into the blood (1)

### bio-mock2-q13 | points 3 | outcome B3.5.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The absorbed nutrients pass through the student's kidneys. Measurements on her kidneys give:

- blood plasma flow through both kidneys: 660 mL per minute;
- fraction of the plasma that passes the glomerular membrane into the renal capsules: 0.20;

Calculate the volume of primary urine (glomerular filtrate) formed per day, and calculate the percentage of that volume that is reabsorbed. Show your working.

### bio-mock2-q14 | points 1 | outcome B3.5.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

The absorbed nutrients pass through the student's kidneys. On a hot afternoon she sweats a lot and drinks little; her blood plasma becomes more concentrated (higher osmotic value).

Describe how the hormone ADH acts in this situation, and name where ADH exerts its effect in the kidney.

#### Answer

The release of ADH rises; ADH makes the walls of the collecting ducts (and distal tubules) more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed from the filtrate and less, more concentrated urine is produced.

#### Solution

The hypothalamus detects the higher osmotic value of the blood and the pituitary releases more ADH. ADH acts on the collecting ducts of the nephrons: their walls become more permeable to water, which follows the osmotic gradient (via aquaporins) back into the tissue and blood. Result: a small volume of concentrated urine, and the plasma is diluted back towards normal.
*Marking:* • more ADH → more water reabsorption in the collecting ducts → less, concentrated urine (1)

### bio-mock2-q15 | points 2 | outcome B4.3.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A sprinter stands in the starting blocks. At the shot of the starting pistol she drives away explosively. A biomechanics team studies what happens between the shot and the first movement.

Describe the chain of events from the stimulus (the sound of the shot) to the contraction of the leg muscles, naming the receptor, the types of neuron, the central nervous system and the effector.

#### Answer

Sound → receptor cells (hair cells in the cochlea of the ear) → sensory neuron → relay neurons in the central nervous system (brain and spinal cord) → motor neuron → effector (the skeletal/leg muscles), which contract.

#### Solution

The sound waves of the shot stimulate the auditory receptors — the hair cells in the cochlea — which convert the stimulus into electrical impulses. These travel along sensory neurons to the central nervous system; there, relay neurons pass the signal via the brain to the motor centres, and motor neurons carry the command down the spinal cord to the effectors, the leg muscles, which contract. At the synapses between the neurons the signal is passed on chemically by neurotransmitters.
*Marking:* • receptor (hair cells/cochlea) → sensory neuron → CNS (relay) (1) • → motor neuron → effector (leg muscles) (1)

### bio-mock2-q17 | points 1 | outcome B6.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A sprinter stands in the starting blocks. At the shot of the starting pistol she drives away explosively. A biomechanics team studies what happens between the shot and the first movement.

During the push-off, the leg muscles are stretched and loaded heavily. State the function of the muscle spindle and of the tendon (organ) in this situation.

#### Answer

The muscle spindle detects the stretch (length change) of the muscle and triggers a reflex that counteracts overstretching; the tendon (organ) detects the tension/pulling force and, via a reflex, inhibits contraction (or moderates it) to protect muscle and tendon.

#### Solution

Muscle spindles are stretch receptors inside the muscle: they register how fast and how far the muscle is stretched and drive (via a spinal reflex arc) a contraction that limits the stretch, as in the stretch reflex. *Marking:* • spindle = stretch receptor driving the stretch reflex AND tendon organ = tension receptor that inhibits/moderates contraction (1) (both elements required for the point)

### bio-mock2-q18 | points 2 | outcome B4.3.1 | difficulty hard

#### Question

In athletics, a start is declared a false start when a runner moves within 0.100 s of the shot. Physiologists argue that no runner can react to the sound in less time. Use these data from a study of sprinters:

- total length of the impulse path from the auditory nerve down the spinal cord to the calf muscles: 1.8 m;
- mean speed of impulse conduction in the myelinated neurons concerned: 80 m s⁻¹;
- processing in the cochlea, the brain centres and across the synapses: at least 0.060 s;
- activation of the muscle filaments after the motor end plate is reached: 0.015 s.

Show with a calculation that the 0.100 s limit is consistent with these data, and argue why a runner who moves 0.080 s after the shot cannot have reacted to the sound.

#### Answer

Conduction time: 1.8 m / 80 m s⁻¹ = 0.0225 s; total minimum = 0.0225 + 0.060 + 0.015 = 0.0975 s ≈ 0.10 s, so a true reaction cannot be faster than about 0.100 s; a runner moving at 0.080 s started before the signal could have arrived — an anticipation, not a reaction.

#### Solution

The nerve impulses must cover 1.8 m at 80 m s⁻¹: t = distance / speed = 1.8 / 80 = 0.0225 s. Adding the irreducible delays — sensory and central processing with synaptic delays 0.060 s, and excitation–contraction delay in the muscle 0.015 s — gives a minimum reaction time of 0.0225 + 0.060 + 0.015 = 0.0975 s, i.e. about 0.10 s. Whoever moves 0.080 s after the shot has a "reaction" shorter than the physiological minimum: the movement must have begun before the signal was processed, so the start was anticipated — exactly what the 0.100 s false-start rule is meant to catch.
*Marking:* • t = 1.8 / 80 = 0.0225 s and summed with the other delays to ≈ 0.10 s (1) • argued: 0.080 s is below the physiological minimum → anticipation, not reaction (1)

### bio-mock2-q19 | points 2 | outcome B6.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A sprinter stands in the starting blocks. At the shot of the starting pistol she drives away explosively. A biomechanics team studies what happens between the shot and the first movement.

Describe how a skeletal muscle fibre shortens during the push-off. Involve the proteins actin and myosin and the role of ATP in your answer.

#### Answer

In each sarcomere, the myosin heads attach to the actin filaments, pull them towards the centre (the filaments slide past each other) and detach again; ATP provides the energy for this cycle of attach–pull–detach, so the sarcomeres — and the whole muscle — shorten.

#### Solution

When the motor impulse arrives, Ca²⁺ is released in the muscle fibre, exposing binding sites on the thin actin filaments. The heads of the thick myosin filaments bind to actin, make a power stroke that slides the actin filaments inwards, then bind ATP, release, and re-cock. Repeated cross-bridge cycling shortens every sarcomere, so the whole muscle shortens. ATP is needed to detach and re-activate the myosin heads (and to pump the Ca²⁺ back during relaxation); without ATP the heads stay bound (rigor).
*Marking:* • myosin heads attach to actin and pull/slide the filaments past each other → shortening (1) • ATP supplies the energy for (detaching) the myosin heads / the cycle (1)

### bio-mock2-q22 | points 2 | outcome D2.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A field of spring barley is attacked by the grey-spot fungus, a parasitic fungus whose hyphae push through the leaf surface. Barley cells carry a receptor protein in their cell membrane that exactly fits a protein from the fungal wall. When the fungal protein binds, the barley cell starts to produce defence proteins that strengthen the cell wall and enzymes that attack the fungus.

Only a few fungal protein molecules reach a barley cell, yet the cell produces large quantities of defence proteins within minutes. Explain how the signal cascade inside the cell makes this amplification possible.

#### Answer

At every step of the cascade one active protein activates many copies of the next component (enzymes catalyse repeatedly; second messengers are produced in large numbers), so a few bound receptors are amplified step by step into a large output.

This is why the response is fast and strong despite few signal molecules.

### bio-mock2-q23 | points 2 | outcome D1.1.5 | difficulty hard

#### Question

A field of spring barley is attacked by the grey-spot fungus, a parasitic fungus whose hyphae push through the leaf surface. Barley cells carry a receptor protein in their cell membrane that exactly fits a protein from the fungal wall. When the fungal protein binds, the barley cell starts to produce defence proteins that strengthen the cell wall and enzymes that attack the fungus.

A chemist designs a fungicide molecule that resembles the fungal wall protein and blocks the barley receptor. She expects the crop to be protected. Assess her expectation: explain what the effect of this fungicide on the barley's defence will be, and predict what that means for the crop in this attack.

#### Answer

Her expectation is wrong: blocking the barley receptor prevents signal transduction, so the defence genes are not switched on and no defence proteins are made; the fungicide therefore makes the crop more vulnerable to the fungus, not protected. (A useful blocker would have to hit a fungal target, not the plant receptor.)

#### Solution

The defence is triggered through the receptor: fungal protein → receptor → cascade → gene expression → defence proteins. A molecule that blocks the receptor abolishes the first step of this chain; the cascade is never started and the genes are not expressed, so the cell wall is not strengthened and no antifungal enzymes appear. Blocking the plant's own detection thus disturbs the regulation of the defence and will increase the damage the fungus does — the design expectation fails. Only a substance that blocks the fungus (its enzymes, receptors or regulator proteins) would protect the crop.
*Marking:* • no receptor activation → no cascade/gene expression → no defence proteins (1) • conclusion: crop not protected, rather more vulnerable (or: blocker must act on the fungus, not the plant) (1)

The grey-spot fungus breaks down the waxy cuticle of the barley leaf with enzymes. The genes for these cuticle-degrading enzymes lie together in one operon in the fungal DNA. Regulation:

The promoter is the binding site for RNA polymerase; the operator is the switch site where the repressor sits. Calculate the concentration factor of nerolin between the pond water and the grey heron, and explain why the concentration rises so strongly along this food chain.

The Vassen moor is a region of heathland with a shallow pond. In 1985 the surrounding farmers stopped grazing sheep there; ecologists have followed the area for forty years. Pro heathland: (a) the open heathland holds specialised, rare species (plants, insects, birds) that cannot live in woodland — restoring it conserves biodiversity at species level; (b) a landscape with both heathland, pond and woodland patches has more ecosystem types and ecotones, supporting more species in total — biodiversity at ecosystem level.
