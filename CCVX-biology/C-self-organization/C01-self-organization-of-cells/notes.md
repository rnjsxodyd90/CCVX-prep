# C1 — Self-Organization of Cells: notes

These are English explanations of every learning outcome in [README.md](README.md), and every topic is *drafted*: written from the CCVX syllabus, the Dutch CvTE syllabus and standard VWO biology, not yet checked against an open source text or exam question.

## C1.1 Gene Expression

### 1. Non-coding DNA and introns

- Most DNA in eukaryotes is non-coding DNA (*niet-coderend DNA*): it doesn't code for the amino acids (*aminozuren*) of any protein (*eiwit*). *(CvTE M3.1 specs 1–2)*
- The human genome (*genoom*) has about 3.2·10⁹ base pairs per set of 23 chromosomes (*chromosomen*) but only about 20,000 protein-coding genes (*genen*); the parts that code for protein make up only about 1–2% of it, some 30–60 million base pairs.
- The rest includes introns, regulatory sequences such as the promoter (*promotor*) of each gene, genes for non-coding RNA (*niet-coderend RNA*), repetitive DNA such as telomeres and transposons ([B01](../../B-self-regulation/B01-protein-synthesis/README.md)), and DNA whose function is still unknown.
- Bacteria are the opposite: about 88% of the chromosome of E. coli codes for protein, and bacterial genes normally have no introns.
- A eukaryotic gene is a mosaic: its exons end up in the mature mRNA, while the introns between them are transcribed and then cut out (outcome 2).
- In most genes the introns are far longer than the exons: the dystrophin gene spans more than 2 million base pairs, but its mRNA is only about 14,000 nucleotides long, so more than 99% of the gene is intron.
- cDNA shows this: it is copied from mature mRNA by reverse transcriptase ([B01](../../B-self-regulation/B01-protein-synthesis/README.md)), so it lacks the introns, and comparing it with the gene shows where they were.
- Non-coding RNA is RNA that is never translated, such as tRNA, rRNA, microRNA that silences other genes ([D01](../../D-interaction/D01-molecular-interaction/README.md)) and Xist RNA, which switches off one of the two X chromosomes in the cells of women; its genes are non-coding DNA, but they do work.
- **Trap:** non-coding doesn't mean useless: non-coding DNA includes promoters, the genes for tRNA, rRNA and microRNA, and the telomeres.

### 2. Gene expression up to protein synthesis

- Gene expression (*genexpressie*) is using the information in a gene to make its product: transcription (*transcriptie*), processing of the RNA and, for a protein-coding gene, translation (*translatie*). *(CvTE M3.1 spec 3)*
- A chromosome (*chromosoom*) is one long DNA molecule with proteins, carrying hundreds to thousands of genes; a gene is the stretch of DNA that codes for one RNA, usually an mRNA for a protein.
- Step 1, access: the nucleosomes (*nucleosomen*), DNA wound around a core of eight histone proteins, loosen around the gene so that its DNA can be read ([B01](../../B-self-regulation/B01-protein-synthesis/README.md)). *(CvTE M4.1 spec 4)*
- Step 2, transcription: transcription factors (*transcriptiefactoren*, outcome 3) bind near the promoter, and RNA polymerase (*RNA-polymerase*) copies the template strand (*template/matrijsstreng*) of the gene into pre-mRNA, which still contains exons and introns.
- Step 3, splicing: a complex of RNA and proteins cuts the introns out of the pre-mRNA and joins the exons into mature mRNA.
- Alternative splicing joins different combinations of exons, so one gene can give several proteins: the same human gene gives the hormone calcitonin in the thyroid and a different peptide, CGRP, that nerve cells (*zenuwcellen*) use as a signal substance (*signaalstof*).
- Step 4, translation: the mRNA leaves the nucleus through the nuclear pores, and a ribosome translates it from the start codon (*startcodon*) AUG to a stop codon (*stopcodon*) UAA, UAG or UGA, following the genetic code (*genetische code*) shown in the Binas table of the genetic code ([B01](../../B-self-regulation/B01-protein-synthesis/README.md)).
- Step 5: the protein folds and, if it is meant for export or for a membrane, is finished in the RER and Golgi apparatus (*golgi-systeem*).
- A cell can regulate expression at every step: packing, transcription, splicing, breakdown of the mRNA, translation and the activity of the protein ([D01](../../D-interaction/D01-molecular-interaction/README.md)).
- **Trap:** splicing removes the introns from the RNA, not from the DNA: the gene in the chromosome keeps its introns.

### 3. Genes are expressed depending on the circumstances

- Most genes aren't active all the time: cells switch them on or off in response to signals such as hormones, nutrients, temperature, light and stage of development. *(CvTE M3.1 spec 4)*
- A transcription factor (*transcriptiefactor*) is a protein that binds to a specific DNA sequence in or near the promoter and helps or blocks the binding of RNA polymerase; without the right transcription factors a eukaryotic gene isn't transcribed. *(CvTE M4.1 spec 3)*
- An activator is a transcription factor that increases transcription, often by binding to an enhancer sequence that can lie thousands of base pairs from the gene; the DNA loops so that the activator reaches the proteins at the promoter.
- Signals usually act by activating transcription factors: the steroid hormone (*steroïdhormoon*) cortisol binds a receptor in the cytoplasm (*cytoplasma*), and the hormone–receptor complex acts as a transcription factor in the nucleus ([D02](../../D-interaction/D02-cellular-interaction/README.md)).
- Nutrients: the bacterium E. coli only makes the enzymes for breaking down lactose when lactose is present (the lac operon, [D01](../../D-interaction/D01-molecular-interaction/README.md)).
- Temperature: heated cells switch on genes for heat-shock proteins, which help other proteins keep or regain their shape.
- Development: before birth, red blood cell precursors express a fetal globin gene, and around birth they switch to the adult β-globin gene; fetal haemoglobin binds O₂ more strongly, which helps the fetus take O₂ from the mother's blood.
- Light: light switches on plant genes for the photosynthesis machinery, which is why potato tubers turn green when they lie in the light.
- **Trap:** a signal doesn't add genes or change the base sequence (*basenvolgorde*); it only changes which of the existing genes are read, and how much.

### 4. Different cell types make different proteins

- All body cells contain the same genes, but each cell type (*celtype*) expresses its own selection of them, so it makes the set of proteins that fits its function. *(CvTE M3.1 spec 5)*
- Some genes, such as those for RNA polymerase, ribosomal proteins and the enzymes of glycolysis, are active in almost every cell; others are active only in particular cell types.
- The CvTE groups proteins by function, and each group has typical producers:

| Protein type | Example | Typical producer |
|---|---|---|
| enzyme (*enzym*) | salivary amylase | salivary gland cells |
| receptor | insulin receptor | especially liver, muscle and fat cells |
| membrane protein (*membraaneiwit*) | aquaporin, a water channel | kidney collecting duct cells |
| transport protein (*transporteiwit*) | haemoglobin | red blood cell precursors |
| protein hormone (*eiwithormoon*) | insulin | β-cells of the pancreatic islets |
| structural protein (*structuureiwit*) | collagen; keratin | fibroblasts; skin cells |
| antibody (*antistof*) | antibodies against one antigen | plasma cells |
| motor protein (*motoreiwit*) | myosin | muscle cells |

- Example: a β-cell and a red blood cell precursor both contain the insulin gene and the β-globin gene, but of these two the β-cell transcribes only the insulin gene and the precursor only the globin gene.
- **Trap:** cell types differ in which genes they express, not in which genes they have; specialisation comes from gene regulation, not from losing DNA.

### 5. Gene expression and the functioning of the organism

- The phenotype (*fenotype*), the observable traits of an organism, depends on which proteins its cells make, how much and when, together with the environment. *(CvTE M3.1 spec 6)*
- A knockout gene (*knock-out-gen*) is a gene that researchers have deliberately disabled in an organism, for example with CRISPR-Cas9, so that the changed phenotype shows what the gene normally does.
- Example: mice with a knocked-out myostatin gene grow much bigger muscles, so myostatin normally limits muscle growth; Belgian Blue cattle are double-muscled because of a natural mutation (*mutatie*) in the same gene.
- Example: in phenylketonuria (PKU) the liver lacks a working enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine, which then builds up and damages the developing brain. The Dutch heel-prick test (*hielprik*) detects PKU in newborns, and a low-phenylalanine diet prevents the damage.
- Example: in most mammals the lactase gene switches off after weaning, but many Europeans have a variant in a regulatory DNA sequence that keeps it on, so they can digest milk as adults.
- The link works both ways: hormones from organs change gene expression in their target cells ([D02](../../D-interaction/D02-cellular-interaction/README.md)), and the proteins those cells then make change how the organs work.
- **Trap:** a knockout shows what a gene is needed for, not everything it does: if another gene can take over its task, knocking it out may show little or no effect.

## C1.2 Cell Differentiation

### 1. (Almost) all cells have the same genome

- CCVX's “multicellular organization” probably means a multicellular organism (*meercellig organisme*): virtually all its cells have the same genome, which the CvTE phrases as the same genotype (*genotype*). *(CvTE M3.2 spec 1)*
- The genome is all the DNA of a cell or organism: in eukaryotes the nuclear DNA plus the DNA in mitochondria and, in plants, chloroplasts.
- All body cells come from one zygote by mitosis (*mitose*), which gives both daughter cells an exact copy of every chromosome ([E01](../../E-reproduction/E01-dna-replication/README.md), [E02](../../E-reproduction/E02-cell-life-cycle/README.md)).
- Evidence: Dolly the sheep (1996) developed from an egg cell whose own nucleus had been replaced by the nucleus of an udder cell, so a specialised cell still contains all the genes needed for a whole animal.
- Exceptions: mature red blood cells have no nucleus, gametes (*gameten*) are haploid (*haploïd*) and genetically unique, B and T lymphocytes cut and rejoin parts of their antibody and receptor genes, and somatic mutations make small differences, which can lead to cancer (*kanker*, outcome 5).
- **Trap:** Dolly's nuclear DNA came from the udder-cell donor, but her mitochondrial DNA came from the egg donor, so a clone isn't completely identical to the nucleus donor.

### 2. Differentiation into cells with different forms and functions

- Cell differentiation (*celdifferentiatie*) is the development of unspecialised cells into specialised cells with their own form and function (specialisation, *specialisatie*). *(CvTE M3.2 spec 2)*
- A human develops from one zygote into more than 200 cell types by repeated cell division followed by differentiation.
- Form fits function: red blood cells lose their nucleus and fill up with haemoglobin, neurons grow long axons, sperm cells get a flagellum and many mitochondria, and xylem vessel cells in plants build thick, lignified walls and then die, leaving open tubes.
- Many specialised animal cells divide rarely or never again: most neurons and heart muscle cells stay in the G0 phase ([E02](../../E-reproduction/E02-cell-life-cycle/README.md)).
- The extracellular matrix (*tussencelstof*) is the material that cells secrete around themselves, such as collagen fibres and gel-forming molecules in connective tissue, or the calcium phosphate-hardened matrix of bone.
- The matrix holds cells together and gives strength; cells attach to it with membrane proteins, and signals from the matrix help steer differentiation, which is why tissue engineers grow stem cells (*stamcellen*, outcome 4) on matrix-like scaffolds.
- **Trap:** division and differentiation are different processes: division makes more cells, differentiation makes them different, and a differentiated cell keeps the genome of the cell it came from.

### 3. Differentiation by switching genes on and off

- Cells differentiate because different combinations of genes are switched on and off in them, not because they lose genes. *(CvTE M3.2 spec 3)*
- The switching is triggered from outside the cell: by signal substances (*signaalstoffen*) from neighbouring cells, by the cell's position in the embryo and by contact with the extracellular matrix.
- Key transcription factors switch on whole sets of genes: MyoD switches on muscle genes, and adding the MyoD gene to cultured fibroblasts turns them into muscle-like cells.
- Once set, the pattern is kept and passed on at every mitosis by epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation (*methylering*) and nucleosome packing, so a liver cell divides into liver cells ([D01](../../D-interaction/D01-molecular-interaction/README.md)).
- Plant example: in the amylopectin potato the gene for the enzyme that makes amylose (granule-bound starch synthase) is switched off or broken, so the tuber cells store only amylopectin (see specimen contexts).
- Plant cells stay more flexible than most animal cells: a single carrot cell can grow into a whole plant, which tissue culture uses to clone crops ([E02](../../E-reproduction/E02-cell-life-cycle/README.md)).
- **Trap:** switching a gene off doesn't remove it, and it can sometimes be switched on again, as when body cells are reprogrammed into iPS cells (outcome 4).

### 4. Stem cells

- A stem cell (*stamcel*) is an unspecialised cell that can keep dividing (self-renewal) and whose daughter cells can differentiate into specialised cells. *(CvTE M3.2 specs 4–5)*
- At a division one daughter cell often stays a stem cell while the other starts to differentiate, so the supply of stem cells doesn't run out.
- Types of stem cells (*typen stamcellen*) differ in potency, the range of cell types they can form:
    - totipotent: every cell type including the placenta, so a whole individual; these are the zygote and the cells of the first few divisions;
    - pluripotent: every cell type of the body but not the placenta; these are embryonic stem cells (*embryonale stamcellen*) from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst, about 5 days after fertilisation;
    - multipotent: several cell types of one tissue; these are adult stem cells (*adulte stamcellen*), such as the blood stem cells in red bone marrow that form all types of blood cells, and stem cells in the skin and gut lining.
- Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells) are body cells, such as skin fibroblasts, that have been reprogrammed into pluripotent cells by adding the genes for four transcription factors (Yamanaka, 2006; Nobel Prize 2012).
- Stem cell transplantation (*stamceltransplantatie*): bone marrow or blood stem cell transplants have treated leukaemia for decades, skin grown from stem cells helps burn patients, and cells grown from stem cells to replace damaged retina, heart muscle or insulin-producing cells are being tested in trials.
- Stem cell research (*stamcelonderzoek*): stem cells show how differentiation works, and cells or organoids (small organ-like structures) grown from a patient's stem cells let researchers study a disease and test drugs outside the patient.
- Embryonic stem cells are the most versatile, but harvesting them destroys an embryo and the donor cells can be rejected; adult and iPS cells avoid the embryo, and a patient's own iPS cells aren't rejected.
- A risk of all pluripotent cells: undifferentiated cells left in a transplant can grow into tumours.
- **Trap:** embryonic stem cells are pluripotent, not totipotent: they can't form a placenta, so on their own they can't develop into a complete individual.

### 5. Apoptosis

- Apoptosis (*apoptose*) is programmed cell death: a cell destroys itself in an orderly way when it is no longer needed or is damaged beyond repair. *(CvTE M3.2 specs 6–7)*
- Enzymes inside the cell cut up its proteins and DNA, the cell shrinks and falls apart into small membrane-wrapped packages, and phagocytes such as macrophages engulf them.
- The packages are digested in the lysosomes (*lysosomen*) of the phagocyte, organelles full of digestive enzymes that work at a low pH; lysosomal enzymes can also help break down the dying cell itself.
- Because nothing leaks out, apoptosis causes no inflammation, unlike necrosis (*necrose*), death by injury, in which the cell swells and bursts.
- Triggers are internal, such as DNA damage that can't be repaired (detected by the protein p53), or external, such as a lack of growth factors or a death signal from a cytotoxic T cell ([B05](../../B-self-regulation/B05-defence-in-organisms/README.md)).
- In the human embryo apoptosis removes the webbing between the fingers and toes; in ducks this apoptosis is largely blocked, so their feet stay webbed.
- A tadpole loses its tail by apoptosis, triggered by thyroid hormone; many young neurons that fail to make good connections die by apoptosis; and T cells that would attack the body's own cells are removed in the thymus.
- In the worm C. elegans exactly 131 of the 1090 cells formed during the development of a hermaphrodite die by apoptosis, leaving 959; the genes for apoptosis were first found in this worm ([D02](../../D-interaction/D02-cellular-interaction/README.md)).
- Cancer (*kanker*): when apoptosis fails, damaged cells survive and can grow into a tumour. About half of all human cancers have a mutated p53, and radiotherapy and many chemotherapy drugs work partly by triggering apoptosis ([E02](../../E-reproduction/E02-cell-life-cycle/README.md), [D01](../../D-interaction/D01-molecular-interaction/README.md)).
- **Trap:** apoptosis isn't an accident or a failure: it is an active, ATP-requiring programme that normal development and health depend on.

## Specimen contexts

- Stem cells at a transplant centre (health): stem cells from bone marrow, embryos or iPS cells are grown and steered with signal substances and a suitable matrix to differentiate into, for example, heart muscle or insulin-producing cells. The open problems are rejection (avoided with the patient's own iPS cells), the risk of tumours and the ethics of embryonic stem cells. This uses C1.2 outcomes 2–4 and C1.1 outcomes 3–4.
- Amylopectin potatoes (food production): normal potato starch is about 20–25% amylose, an almost unbranched chain, and 75–80% branched amylopectin; amylose makes starch pastes set into a gel, so industry has to treat or separate the starch. Switching off the gene for granule-bound starch synthase, by genetic modification or by breeding with a natural mutation, gives potatoes that store practically only amylopectin, which simplifies processing for paper, glue and textiles. This uses C1.1 outcomes 3 and 5, C1.2 outcome 3 and [D01](../../D-interaction/D01-molecular-interaction/README.md).

## CCVX versus the Dutch syllabus

- **In both:** non-coding DNA, introns, exons and cDNA, gene expression up to protein synthesis (RNA polymerase, splicing, start and stop codons), condition-dependent expression, different proteins in different cell types, knockout genes and phenotype, the same genome in all cells, differentiation by switching genes, stem cells, apoptosis and lysosomes.
- **Dutch syllabus only:** the list of protein types (enzyme, receptor, membrane protein, transport protein, protein hormone, structural protein, antibody, motor protein) in M3.1 spec 5, specialisation in M3.2 spec 2, stem cell research and stem cell transplantation in M3.2 spec 5, and cancer in M3.2 spec 6; all are covered above.
- **CCVX only:** transcription factor and activator (the CvTE has them in M4.1 spec 3), nucleosomes (M4.1 spec 4), non-coding RNA, the extracellular matrix (the CvTE only lists *tussencelstof* as an assimilation product in M2.3 spec 7), and the names totipotent and pluripotent (the CvTE only says *typen stamcellen*).
