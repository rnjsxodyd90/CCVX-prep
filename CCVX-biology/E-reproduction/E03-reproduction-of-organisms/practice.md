### bio-e03-q01 | points 2 | outcome E3.1.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A grower propagates spider plants (*Chlorophytum comosum*) from the plantlets that form at the ends of stolons (runners). Explain why these plantlets are genetically identical to the parent plant (mutations apart), and name the type of cell division involved.

#### Answer

The plantlets arise by mitosis from one parent, without fusion of gametes, so they carry a copy of the parent's genome: they are clones.

#### Solution

The plantlets form by mitotic division of the parent's body cells, so each cell gets a complete copy of the parent's genome. No meiosis or fertilization takes place, so no new allele combinations arise (a rare mutation aside). Offspring that are genetically identical to a single parent are called clones.
*Marking:* • division by mitosis / only one parent, no fusion of gametes (1) • copy of the genome, so a clone (1)

### bio-e03-q02 | points 3 | outcome E3.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

At an algae test farm, the green alga *Teractis lacustris* (2n = 18) multiplies by mitotic cell division. For a breeding programme, researchers want cultures with new combinations of alleles, so they induce sexual reproduction: haploid cells (n = 9) then behave as gametes and fuse into a zygote. Explain, using chromosome behaviour in meiosis, why such a zygote has allele combinations that neither parent cell had, and calculate how many different chromosome combinations one haploid cell of this alga can produce by independent assortment alone (without crossing-over).

#### Answer

In meiosis the nine chromosome pairs orient independently, so each gamete gets a random mix of the parent's chromosomes; crossing-over and subsequent fertilization mix them further. Number: 2⁹ = 512.

#### Solution

In meiosis I the homologous chromosome pairs orient independently, so one gamete can form 2⁹ = 512 different chromosome combinations. Crossing-over in prophase I exchanges parts of homologous chromosomes and increases the variation further. At fertilization two such gametes fuse, so the zygote combines two of these mixes: allele combinations that neither haploid parent cell had.
*Marking:* • independent assortment of the homologous pairs in meiosis I (1) • crossing-over and/or fertilization as the extra mixing step, giving new allele combinations (1) • 2⁹ = 512 (1)

### bio-e03-q03 | points 2 | outcome E3.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In a person's large intestine, an *E. coli* cell transfers a copy of a plasmid with a resistance gene to a neighbouring *E. coli* cell through a pilus (conjugation). A student calls this "sexual reproduction of bacteria". Take a position on this statement: give one argument why conjugation does increase genetic variation, and one argument why it is nevertheless not reproduction. Also calculate the following: an *E. coli* culture of 400 cells divides once every 20 minutes. Concentrations are chosen so that all cells conjugate at t = 0; the transfer itself takes 35 minutes. How many cells does the culture contain after those 35 minutes?

#### Answer

The statement is wrong. Variation: the recipient gains new DNA from another cell. Not reproduction: no extra cell is formed. Cell count: 800.

#### Solution

Variation: the recipient takes up genetic material (a plasmid) from another cell, which changes its genome (horizontal gene transfer). Reproduction: conjugation produces no extra individual — the number of cells stays the same; only cell division increases the number. Calculation: one division per 20 minutes, so in 35 minutes each cell can divide only once (35 min is less than two division times): 400 → 800 cells.
*Marking:* • new DNA from another cell increases variation, but no extra cell is formed so it is not reproduction (1) • 800 cells, via at most one doubling in 35 minutes (1)

### bio-e03-q04 | points 2 | outcome E3.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A couple has their prize mare cloned by nuclear transfer: the nucleus of a body cell of the mare is placed into an egg cell of another horse whose own nucleus has been removed, and a surrogate mare carries the embryo. The foal is called a clone of the prize mare. Give two reasons why the foal is nevertheless not completely genetically identical to the prize mare.

#### Answer

Her mitochondrial DNA comes from the egg cell, not from the prize mare, and her epigenetic marks can differ because the egg cell reprogrammes them only partly.

#### Solution

Only the cell nucleus comes from the prize mare; the mitochondrial DNA in the cytoplasm comes from the enucleated egg cell of the other horse. In addition, epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation are only partly erased and reset in the cloned embryo, so the pattern of gene activity can differ. (The environment also differs, but that changes the phenotype, not the genotype.)
*Marking:* • mitochondrial DNA from the egg cell (1) • epigenetic marks can differ (1)

### bio-e03-q05 | points 3 | outcome E3.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A fruit company sells a seedless watermelon variety. The plant is triploid: its body cells contain three sets of chromosomes (3n = 33) instead of two. Explain why a triploid plant forms hardly any usable gametes, and therefore no seeds, and explain how seed producers obtain new triploid plants every year.

#### Answer

In meiosis the three homologous sets cannot be distributed evenly over the gametes, so the gametes end up with unbalanced chromosome numbers and fail. New triploid plants are obtained by crossing a diploid plant (n = 11) with a tetraploid plant (gametes with 22).

#### Solution

In meiosis, homologous chromosomes pair and are shared out in twos; with three sets, pairing and separation cannot come out evenly, so gametes receive unbalanced numbers of chromosomes (a missing or extra copy of one or more chromosomes) and are not viable: no seeds. A triploid (33) is therefore made freshly each time from a diploid gamete (11) and a tetraploid gamete (22): 11 + 22 = 33.
*Marking:* • three homologous sets cannot be distributed evenly in meiosis (1) • unbalanced gametes are not viable, so no seeds (1) • made by crossing diploid × tetraploid (11 + 22 = 33) (1)

### bio-e03-q06 | points 2 | outcome E3.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Breeders treat dividing cells of a diploid melon plant (2n = 22) with colchicine, a substance that blocks the spindle, so that the chromosome number is doubled. Calculate the chromosome numbers in: a body cell of the treated plant; a gamete of the treated plant; and a zygote formed by crossing the treated plant with an untreated diploid plant.

#### Answer

Treated plant: 44; its gamete: 22; zygote: 22 + 11 = 33.

#### Solution

Doubling 2n = 22 gives a tetraploid cell: 4n = 44. In meiosis this number is halved, so the treated plant's gametes have 22 chromosomes. An untreated plant makes gametes with n = 11, so the zygote has 22 + 11 = 33 chromosomes (triploid).
*Marking:* • body cell 44 and gamete 22 (1) • zygote 33 from 22 + 11 (1)

### bio-e03-q07 | points 3 | outcome E3.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In humans, one primary spermatocyte yields four working sperm cells, but one primary oocyte yields only one egg cell. Name the small cells that are formed besides the egg cell, and explain — from the way the cytoplasm is divided — why the outcome differs, including the advantage of this difference for the embryo.

#### Answer

The small cells are polar bodies. In oogenesis the cytoplasm divides very unequally, so almost all cytoplasm ends up in one large egg cell; in spermatogenesis both divisions are equal, giving four equal cells. Advantage: the large egg cell keeps the nutrients and organelles for the first cell divisions of the embryo.

#### Solution

Besides the egg, polar bodies form; they are small, haploid and die off. In oogenesis, meiosis I and II split the chromosome sets equally but the cytoplasm very unequally, producing one large cell; in spermatogenesis both divisions are equal, producing four equal spermatids that mature into four sperm cells. The advantage: the single egg retains almost all cytoplasm, with the nutrients and organelles needed until the embryo can take up food itself.
*Marking:* • polar bodies named (1) • unequal cytoplasm division in oogenesis versus equal divisions in spermatogenesis (1) • advantage: one large egg keeps nutrients/organelles for early development (1)

### bio-e03-q09 | points 3 | outcome E3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A woman with a regular 28-day cycle measures her hormone levels for one cycle (arbitrary units):

| day | oestrogen | LH | progesterone |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 10 | 6 | 1.0 |
| 6 | 26 | 8 | 1.1 |

Determine on which day ovulation most likely took place, and support your answer with two observations from the table.

### bio-e03-q10 | points 2 | outcome E3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In the second half of the cycle the corpus luteum produces progesterone. Explain why the progesterone level falls at the end of the cycle if the woman is not pregnant, explain what then happens in the uterus, and explain the role hCG would play in a pregnancy.

#### Answer

Without a pregnancy no hCG is made, so the corpus luteum breaks down after about two weeks; progesterone falls, the endometrium is no longer maintained and is shed (menstruation). In a pregnancy, hCG from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum producing progesterone.

#### Solution

The corpus luteum survives only with hormonal support. Without fertilization there is no embryo and hence no hCG, so the corpus luteum degenerates and its progesterone production stops. The low progesterone (with low oestrogen) can no longer maintain the thickened endometrium, which breaks down and is shed: menstruation. In a pregnancy, hCG from the embryo's outer layer (and a pregnancy test detects it) keeps the corpus luteum active until the placenta takes over.
*Marking:* • corpus luteum breaks down without hCG, so progesterone falls (1) • endometrium shed; hCG from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum going in a pregnancy (1)

### bio-e03-q11 | points 3 | outcome E3.1.3 | difficulty hard

#### Question

The combined contraceptive pill contains a synthetic oestrogen and a progestogen, taken daily in the same dose. Explain, using the feedback between ovaries and pituitary, why no ovulation occurs during use: include which two pituitary hormones stay low, why no follicle matures, and why the normal trigger for ovulation does not occur.

#### Answer

The pill hormones exert continuous negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary, so FSH and LH stay low. Without FSH no follicle matures enough to produce a high oestrogen level, so the positive-feedback LH peak never occurs and there is no ovulation.

#### Solution

In a normal cycle, the low hormone levels at menstruation let FSH rise; follicles grow and make oestrogen, and a sustained high oestrogen level switches to positive feedback: an LH peak and ovulation. The pill keeps the oestrogen/progestogen level constantly raised, which inhibits the release of FSH and LH (negative feedback). Follicles hardly grow, the ovary's oestrogen stays low, the LH peak is never triggered, and ovulation does not occur.
*Marking:* • negative feedback of the pill hormones on hypothalamus/pituitary (1) • FSH and LH low, so no follicle maturation (1) • no high oestrogen, so no LH peak and no ovulation (1)

### bio-e03-q12 | points 2 | outcome E3.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A couple cannot conceive naturally because the woman's oviducts are blocked after an infection. They choose in vitro fertilization (IVF). Describe the successive steps of an IVF treatment, from hormone treatment to pregnancy test.

#### Answer

Hormone stimulation of the ovaries → collection of oocytes (follicle puncture) → fertilization with sperm cells in a dish → a few days of culturing → transfer of one embryo into the uterus (surplus embryos frozen) → pregnancy test (hCG).

#### Solution

(1) The woman is treated with hormones so that several follicles mature at once. (2) The oocytes are sucked out of the follicles with a fine needle (follicle puncture). (3) In the laboratory the oocytes are brought together with sperm cells (or one sperm cell is injected: ICSI). (4) The zygotes divide in the dish for a few days (cleavage). (5) One, sometimes two, embryos are placed into the uterus; surplus embryos can be frozen. (6) About two weeks later a pregnancy test shows whether hCG is present.
*Marking:* • hormone stimulation and collection of oocytes (1) • fertilization outside the body, culturing, and transfer into the uterus (1)

### bio-e03-q14 | points 2 | outcome E3.2.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A breeder takes 40 cuttings of one hedge plant (so all cuttings are genetically identical). He grows half of them in full sun and half in shade. The sun plants develop dark-red leaf edges; the shade plants stay fully green. Explain what this experiment shows about the way the phenotype arises, and explain why this difference in leaf colour cannot be passed on to offspring via seeds.

#### Answer

Same genotype, different environment, different phenotype: the phenotype develops from the genotype in interaction with environmental factors — here light is needed to form the red pigment. The difference is not in the DNA, so it is not inherited.

#### Solution

Because all 40 plants share one genotype, the colour difference must be caused by the environment (light); this shows that the phenotype develops from the genotype together with environmental factors. Acquired traits such as this change no allele in the DNA of the gametes, so they cannot be passed on to offspring.
*Marking:* • same genotype plus different environment, so the environment co-determines the phenotype (1) • not heritable because the DNA (of the gametes) is unchanged (1)

### bio-e03-q16 | points 2 | outcome E3.2.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Give two structural differences between the X and the Y chromosome. Explain the role of the *SRY* gene in the development of the embryo's gonads, and use it to explain the sex of a person with 45,X chromosomes (Turner syndrome).

#### Answer

The X chromosome is large and carries many hundreds of genes; the Y is small with few genes, among them *SRY*. *SRY* makes the embryo's undifferentiated gonads develop into testes, whose testosterone then drives male development. A 45,X person has no Y and no *SRY*, so ovaries form and the child develops as a girl.

#### Solution

The X chromosome is large and rich in genes, most of them unrelated to sex; the Y chromosome is small and gene-poor, but carries *SRY*. Early embryos of both sexes have undifferentiated gonads; *SRY* directs them to become testes, after which testosterone drives male development. Without any Y chromosome (as in 45,X) there is no *SRY*, the gonads become ovaries, and development proceeds as a girl: it is the presence of *SRY*, not the number of X chromosomes, that is decisive.
*Marking:* • two structural differences (X large/many genes, Y small/few genes) (1) • *SRY* → testes → testosterone → male development; without it (45,X) female development (1)

### bio-e03-q18 | points 2 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The breeder has one purple-stemmed marshbell plant of unknown genotype. Describe a cross that shows whether the plant is PP or Pp, give the two possible outcomes with their ratios, and name this type of cross.

#### Answer

Cross it with a homozygous green plant (pp): a test cross. All offspring purple → the plant is PP; about 1/2 purple : 1/2 green → the plant is Pp.

### bio-e03-q19 | points 3 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In the sand-pouncer, a desert mammal, short fur (K) is dominant over long fur (k), and a white belly (W) is dominant over a grey belly (w). The two genes are on different chromosomes. Two animals with genotype KkWw are crossed and produce a large litter series of 256 young in total. (a) Give the gametes of a KkWw parent with their probabilities. (b) Calculate the expected number of young in each of the four phenotype classes.

#### Answer

Gametes KW, Kw, kW and kw, each with probability 1/4. Expected phenotype ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1, so of 256 young: 144 short fur/white belly, 48 short fur/grey belly, 48 long fur/white belly and 16 long fur/grey belly.

#### Solution

The genes assort independently, so a KkWw parent makes four gamete types, KW, Kw, kW and kw, each with probability 1/4. Per gene: P(short) = 3/4, P(long) = 1/4, P(white) = 3/4, P(grey) = 1/4. (Total 144 + 48 + 48 + 16 = 256.)
*Marking:* • four gamete types, each 1/4 (independent assortment) (1) • phenotype ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 via product rule or 16-box diagram (1) • numbers 144, 48, 48, 16 (1)

### bio-e03-q21 | points 4 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty hard

#### Question

In the plum moth, grey body (G) is dominant over black body (g) and normal antennae (A) over feathery antennae (a). The two genes lie on the same chromosome. A heterozygous moth is test-crossed with a black, feathery moth; the offspring are:

| phenotype | number |
|---|---|
| grey body, normal antennae | 438 |
| black body, feathery antennae | 442 |
| grey body, feathery antennae | 57 |
| black body, normal antennae | 63 |

(a) Which two classes are the parental classes, and what does the heterozygous parent's pair of homologous chromosomes look like (which alleles lie together)? (b) Calculate the recombination percentage between the two genes.

Parental: grey + normal (438) and black + feathery (442), so the heterozygous parent has G with A on one chromosome and g with a on the homologue (GN/gn). Recombinants: 57 + 63 = 120 of 1000 offspring → 12%.

### bio-e03-q22 | points 2 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty hard

#### Question

In the sand-pouncer test cross above (KkWw × kkww, unlinked genes) the four offspring classes are equally frequent; in the plum-moth test cross above (GN/gn × gn/gn, linked genes) two classes are large and two are small. Explain this difference from the way gametes form in meiosis, and explain why a recombination percentage can never rise above 50%.

#### Answer

Unlinked genes assort independently, so all four gamete types are equally likely. Linked genes lie on the same chromosome: without crossing-over only the two parental gametes form, and crossing-over between the loci (only in some cells) adds a smaller number of recombinant gametes. Recombination can never exceed 50%, because at 50% the four gamete types are already equal — exactly what independent, unlinked genes give.

#### Solution

Genes on different chromosomes: independent assortment in meiosis I gives 1/4 of each gamete type, hence four equal offspring classes in a test cross. Genes on the same chromosome: the allele combinations on each chromosome travel together, so parental gametes predominate and recombinant gametes appear only where crossing-over occurred between the two loci — in only part of the meiocytes — giving two small recombinant classes. The recombinant classes together can make up at most half of the gametes, because 50% is the value at which the combinations behave as fully random (as for unlinked genes).
*Marking:* • independent assortment gives four equal gamete classes, linkage a majority of parental gametes (1) • recombinants only via crossing-over in part of the cells; above 50% impossible because 50% equals the unlinked case (1)

### bio-e03-q25 | points 3 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In the steppe hamster, the allele T gives a tufted ear and is dominant; the recessive allele t gives a smooth ear. However, TT embryos die before birth. A breeder crosses two tufted hamsters and counts 180 born young. (a) Explain with a cross diagram why every born tufted hamster is heterozygous. ### bio-e03-q29 | points 2 | outcome E3.2.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In a pedigree for a rare mitochondrial muscle disease, an affected mother appears in generation I. In the pedigree, all six of her children are affected, and so are all children of her affected daughters, while her affected sons have children who are all healthy. Explain this pattern of inheritance, and explain why it does not follow Mendel's laws.

#### Answer

Mitochondrial DNA is inherited only through the mother: every child of an affected mother receives her mitochondria via the cytoplasm of the egg cell, while an affected father passes on no mitochondria. There are no allele pairs segregating in meiosis, so Mendelian ratios and dominance/recessivity do not apply.

#### Solution

At fertilization, the zygote's cytoplasm — with its mitochondria (and mtDNA) — comes almost entirely from the egg cell; the sperm cell's mitochondria do not contribute. Therefore an affected mother transmits the trait to all her children (both sexes), and a father to none. Mendelian inheritance concerns pairs of nuclear alleles that segregate and assort in meiosis, giving ratios like 3 : 1 or 1 : 1; mtDNA is a single maternally inherited molecule with no pairing or segregation, so no such ratios can arise.
*Marking:* • mitochondria/mtDNA only via the egg cell (maternal inheritance) (1) • no allele pairs and no segregation in meiosis, so no Mendelian ratios (1)

### bio-e03-q30 | points 2 | outcome E3.2.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

People conceived during a famine can show changed DNA methylation of certain genes decades later, with measurable differences in phenotype. Give two differences between this epigenetic inheritance and classical (Mendelian) inheritance, and explain why an epigenetic change is not a mutation.

#### Answer

Differences: epigenetic marks can be set by the environment (diet, stress) and can be erased/reversed (often during gamete formation), whereas classical alleles are fixed DNA variants inherited unchanged. Not a mutation: the base sequence of the DNA does not change.

#### Solution

Classical inheritance passes stable alleles — specific DNA sequences — from parent to child through the gametes, and the environment does not overwrite them. Epigenetic inheritance passes activity patterns (such as methylation) that can be placed by environmental influences (famine, stress, toxins), can be removed again, and are often erased when gametes form — though some marks survive. A mutation is a change in the base sequence; methylation adds only a –CH₃ group to a base, so the sequence itself is untouched.
*Marking:* • environment-sensitive and reversible/erased versus fixed alleles (1) • the base sequence is unchanged, so it is no mutation (1)
