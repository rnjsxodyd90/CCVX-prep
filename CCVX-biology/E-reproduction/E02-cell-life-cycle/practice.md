### bio-e02-q01 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

Researchers culture the unicellular alga *Chlorella* as a raw material for biofuel. In the culture vessel every algal cell divides into two cells; in this way the whole culture grows.

Describe the importance of cell division in each of the following cases: the growth of the algal culture; the development of a human embryo; the closing of a cut in human skin.

#### Answer

For the unicellular algae, division is reproduction: each division turns one individual into two. In the embryo and the skin, mitotic division serves growth (more cells from one zygote) and repair (neighbouring cells divide to replace damaged and lost cells).

#### Solution

For a unicellular organism such as *Chlorella*, cell division by mitosis is the way the organism reproduces: the number of individuals doubles each round. A multicellular organism such as a human grows from one cell (the zygote) to trillions of cells by mitosis, and damaged tissue such as a cut is repaired when the cells next to the wound divide to close the gap.
*Marking:* • division of the algae is reproduction of a unicellular organism (1) • in humans: growth from one zygote and repair/replacement of damaged cells, both by mitosis (1)

### bio-e02-q02 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

An adult human body consists of about 4 × 10¹³ cells, all descending from one single cell, the fertilised egg.

Show with a calculation that in theory roughly 45 rounds of cell division are enough to produce this number of cells, and explain why in a real embryo the total number of cell divisions is actually much higher than this calculation suggests.

#### Answer

After n rounds of division one cell gives 2ⁿ descendants; 2⁴⁵ ≈ 3.5 × 10¹³ and 2⁴⁶ ≈ 7.0 × 10¹³, so about 45–46 rounds reach 4 × 10¹³ cells. In reality far more divisions occur, because cells die, not every cell divides in every round, and many cells stop dividing when they differentiate.

#### Solution

Each round of division doubles the number of cells: after n rounds one cell has 2ⁿ descendants. 2⁴⁵ ≈ 3.5 × 10¹³ (and 2⁴⁶ ≈ 7.0 × 10¹³), which is the right order of magnitude for 4 × 10¹³ cells, so roughly 45 rounds suffice. In a real embryo many daughter cells differentiate and leave the cell cycle, cells die along the way (apoptosis), and tissues keep replacing cells throughout life, so the true total number of divisions is much higher than this theoretical minimum.
*Marking:* • doubling per round, 2ⁿ (1) • 2⁴⁵ ≈ 3.5 × 10¹³ (or 2⁴⁶ ≈ 7 × 10¹³) with the conclusion ≈ 45 rounds (1) • real number higher because cells die or stop dividing (differentiate) / not all cells divide every round (1)

### bio-e02-q03 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.2 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A researcher grows human skin cells in a culture dish until they form a single closed layer. She scrapes a strip of cells away with a small spatula. Within a day the cells along the bare strip divide and migrate into it; division stops once the layer is closed again. Cells in the middle of the untouched layer do not divide.

Explain, involving the influence of adjacent cells, why only the cells next to the scraped strip divide.

#### Answer

Cells that are surrounded by neighbours on all sides stop dividing (contact inhibition). The cells along the bare strip lack contact on one side, so this brake is lifted and — stimulated by growth factors — they divide until the layer is closed and contact on all sides is restored.

#### Solution

Normal cells stop dividing when they touch neighbouring cells on all sides; this is contact inhibition. In the closed layer every cell touches neighbours on all sides, so they do not divide. The cells next to the scraped strip have free space on one side, contact inhibition no longer applies to them, and growth factors in the medium (like those from blood platelets at a real wound) switch on their cell cycle. Once the strip is closed, all cells touch neighbours again and division stops.
*Marking:* • cells fully surrounded by neighbours stop dividing (contact inhibition) (1) • cells at the edge lack contact on one side and divide (under the influence of growth factors) until contact is restored (1)

### bio-e02-q04 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

At a wound, blood platelets release platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF). In an experiment, connective-tissue cells (fibroblasts) were cultured in a medium without or with PDGF. Without PDGF almost no cells entered the phase of DNA replication; with PDGF many cells did so within hours.

Explain how a signal protein like PDGF can move a cell from G1 into S phase, and predict what happens when cancer cells, which no longer need growth factors, are cultured in the medium without PDGF.

#### Answer

PDGF binds a receptor protein in the cell membrane; this starts a signal cascade inside the cell that switches on the genes needed to pass from G1 into S phase (DNA replication). The cancer cells will keep dividing even without PDGF.

#### Solution

PDGF binds a specific receptor protein of the fibroblast. The activated receptor passes the signal on inside the cell via a series of relay proteins (a signal cascade). At the end of the cascade the genes are switched on whose products take the cell through the G1 checkpoint into S phase, so DNA replication starts. Cancer cells produce their stimulating signals themselves or have a permanently active signal cascade, so they divide without growth factors: in the PDGF-free medium they keep dividing.
*Marking:* • PDGF binds a receptor protein (1) • signal cascade switches on genes for the G1→S transition (1) • prediction: cancer cells keep dividing without PDGF (1)

### bio-e02-q05 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.3 | difficulty easy

#### Question

The following phases of the cell cycle are in random order:

cytokinesis – anaphase – G1 – prophase – S – G2

Give the phases in the correct order, and name one process that takes place during S phase.

#### Answer

G1 → S → G2 → prophase → (metaphase →) anaphase → (telophase →) cytokinesis. In S phase the DNA is replicated: each chromosome is copied into two sister chromatids.

#### Solution

The cycle runs: G1 (growth) → S (DNA replication) → G2 (further growth and check) → mitosis, which starts with prophase (and runs via metaphase and anaphase to telophase) → cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm). During S phase DNA polymerase copies the DNA, so that afterwards every chromosome consists of two identical sister chromatids held together at the centromere.
*Marking:* • correct order of the phases (1) • in S phase: DNA replication / each chromosome copied into two sister chromatids (1)

### bio-e02-q06 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Body cells of an insect species with 2n = 6 are dividing by mitosis. For one cell the numbers are counted at several stages. The row for G1 is already filled in.

| Stage | Chromosomes per cell | Chromosomes consisting of two chromatids | DNA molecules |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 6 | 0 | 6 |
| metaphase | … | … | … |
| anaphase | … | … | … |
| daughter cell just after cytokinesis | … | … | … |

Copy the table and complete it.

#### Answer

Metaphase: 6 chromosomes, 6 chromosomes of two chromatids, 12 DNA molecules. Anaphase: 12 and 0 and 12. Daughter cell: 6 and 0 and 6.

#### Solution

In metaphase the DNA has already been replicated (in S phase): the 6 chromosomes each consist of 2 sister chromatids, giving 12 DNA molecules in the cell. In anaphase the centromeres split and the sister chromatids are pulled apart; from then on each chromatid counts as a chromosome, so the cell (both poles together) contains 12 chromosomes of one chromatid each, still 12 DNA molecules. After cytokinesis each daughter cell has 6 chromosomes of one chromatid: 6 DNA molecules.
*Marking:* • metaphase row: 6 – 6 – 12 (1) • anaphase row: 12 – 0 – 12 (1) • daughter cell row: 6 – 0 – 6 (1)

### bio-e02-q07 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In a root tip of garlic the cells divide about once every 15.6 hours. A student counts the cells in a microscope preparation of the growth region:

| Phase | Number of cells |
|---|---|
| interphase | 1680 |
| prophase | 150 |
| metaphase | 80 |
| anaphase | 55 |
| telophase | 35 |

The fraction of cells found in a phase equals the fraction of the total cycle time that a cell spends in that phase.

Show with a calculation that mitosis (the four phases of division together) takes about 2.5 hours, and calculate how many minutes prophase takes.

#### Answer

Cells in mitosis: 150 + 80 + 55 + 35 = 320 of the 2000 cells, so mitosis takes (320 / 2000) × 15.6 h ≈ 2.5 h. Prophase: (150 / 2000) × 15.6 h = 1.17 h ≈ 70 minutes.

#### Solution

Total number of cells: 1680 + 150 + 80 + 55 + 35 = 2000. Cells in mitosis: 320. Fraction in mitosis: 320 / 2000 = 0.16. Duration of mitosis: 0.16 × 15.6 h = 2.496 h ≈ 2.5 h. Fraction in prophase: 150 / 2000 = 0.075, so prophase takes 0.075 × 15.6 h = 1.17 h, which is 1.17 × 60 ≈ 70 minutes.
*Marking:* • total in mitosis = 320 of 2000, fraction 0.16 (1) • 0.16 × 15.6 ≈ 2.5 h (1) • prophase: 0.075 × 15.6 h = 1.17 h ≈ 70 min (1)

### bio-e02-q08 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The substance colchicine prevents the microtubules of the spindle apparatus from forming. Root-tip cells are treated with colchicine and studied under the microscope after a few hours.

Explain in which recognisable state of mitosis the treated cells accumulate, and why.

#### Answer

Without a spindle the chromosomes cannot attach to spindle fibres and cannot be pulled apart, so the cells arrest with condensed chromosomes at the transition to metaphase (the spindle checkpoint is never passed); metaphase-like cells accumulate.

#### Solution

The spindle apparatus of microtubules attaches to the centromeres of the chromosomes, lines them up in the middle and pulls the sister chromatids apart. If colchicine prevents microtubule formation, the chromosomes condense (prophase) but can never line up and separate properly. The spindle checkpoint then blocks the start of anaphase, so treated cells pile up with condensed, unattached chromosomes — recognisable as cells stuck in (pro)metaphase.
*Marking:* • without microtubules no attachment/transport of chromosomes (1) • cells arrest before anaphase (at the spindle checkpoint), so condensed/metaphase cells accumulate (1)

### bio-e02-q09 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.3 | difficulty easy

#### Question

Mature nerve cells of a human virtually never divide. Liver cells, on the other hand, normally do not divide either, but they start dividing when part of the liver is removed.

Describe in which phase of the cell cycle these cells stay, and explain the difference between the two cell types.

#### Answer

Both cell types rest in the G0 phase, outside the cycle. Nerve cells stay in G0 almost permanently; liver cells rest in G0 temporarily and return to G1 when growth factors signal after liver damage.

#### Solution

Cells that do not divide leave the cycle from G1 and enter the G0 phase, in which they only do their normal work. Mature nerve cells remain in G0 almost permanently, so they are hardly ever replaced. Liver cells are also in G0 as long as the organ is intact, but when part of the liver is lost, growth factors bring them back into G1 and they pass through the cycle again, so the liver regains its size.
*Marking:* • the cells are in G0, outside the cycle (1) • nerve cells stay in G0; liver cells can re-enter G1 on a signal (1)

### bio-e02-q10 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In mitosis, the sister chromatids separate in anaphase and the chromosome number stays the same. Meiosis (reduction division) differs from mitosis in exactly one phase-pair: prophase/metaphase and anaphase of meiosis I.

Explain in which phase the decisive difference occurs, and how that difference relates to the function of meiosis. Use a cell with 2n = 6 in your answer, and give the chromosome number of each cell after meiosis I.

#### Answer

The decisive difference is anaphase I (prepared by the pairing in prophase I/metaphase I): the homologous chromosomes separate, each still consisting of two chromatids, so the 6 chromosomes are divided as 3 and 3 — the number is halved. This fits the function: gametes become haploid (n = 3), so that fertilisation (3 + 3) restores 2n = 6.

#### Solution

Homologous chromosomes pair up in prophase I and the pairs line up in metaphase I. In anaphase I the two homologues of each pair move to opposite poles, each homologue still consisting of two sister chromatids. A cell with 2n = 6 thus yields two cells with n = 3 chromosomes (each of two chromatids): the chromosome number is halved — this is the actual reduction. In mitosis it is the sister chromatids that separate, so the number stays at 2n. The function follows directly: gametes must be haploid so that fertilisation restores the diploid number, while mitosis must keep the chromosome number constant for growth and repair.
*Marking:* • in anaphase I homologous chromosomes separate (not sister chromatids) (1) • 2n = 6 → n = 3 per cell after meiosis I, chromosome number halved (1) • relation to function: gametes haploid, fertilisation restores 2n; mitosis keeps 2n for growth/repair (1)

### bio-e02-q11 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.4 | difficulty easy

#### Question

Human somatic cells are diploid; the gametes are haploid.

Give the number of chromosomes in a human somatic cell and in a human sperm cell, and describe what a pair of homologous chromosomes is.

#### Answer

A somatic cell has 46 chromosomes (2n), a sperm cell 23 (n). A homologous pair consists of two chromosomes — one from the father and one from the mother — that carry the same genes in the same order, possibly with different alleles.

#### Solution

In humans 2n = 46 and n = 23. Every somatic cell contains 23 pairs of homologous chromosomes: in each pair one chromosome comes from the father and one from the mother, and they carry the same genes in the same order, though the alleles (variants) of those genes may differ. The gametes contain only one chromosome of each pair.
*Marking:* • 46 in a somatic cell, 23 in a sperm cell (1) • homologous pair: same genes in the same order, one from each parent (1)

### bio-e02-q12 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A crop plant has 2n = 8. In metaphase I of meiosis each homologous pair is shared out over the two cells independently of the other pairs. A seed producer crosses two plants of this species.

Calculate the number of different chromosome combinations that one plant can produce in its pollen grains, and the number of different combinations possible in a zygote from this cross. Name one process in meiosis that increases the variation even further.

#### Answer

Pollen grains: n = 4, so 2⁴ = 16 combinations. Zygote: 16 × 16 = 256 combinations. Crossing-over in prophase I adds further variation.

#### Solution

The haploid number is n = 8 / 2 = 4. Each of the 4 homologous pairs can contribute either of its two chromosomes, giving 2⁴ = 16 different chromosome combinations per pollen grain (and equally per egg cell). In a zygote the combinations of both parents come together: 16 × 16 = 256 possibilities. On top of that, crossing-over between non-sister chromatids in prophase I creates new combinations of alleles within each chromosome, increasing the variation far beyond 256.
*Marking:* • 2⁴ = 16 combinations in pollen (1) • 16 × 16 = 256 combinations in the zygote (1) • crossing-over (prophase I) adds variation (1)

### bio-e02-q13 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.5 | difficulty easy

#### Question

The cell cycle is controlled at several checkpoints, moments at which the cell only continues if the conditions are right.

Name two checkpoints of the cell cycle and give for each what is checked at that point.

#### Answer

Any two of: the G1 checkpoint (enough growth factors, nutrients and cell size; DNA undamaged); the G2 checkpoint (all DNA completely and correctly replicated); the spindle (metaphase) checkpoint (all chromosomes attached to spindle fibres from both poles).

At the end of G1 the cell checks whether conditions for division are right: presence of growth factors and nutrients, sufficient size and undamaged DNA. At the end of G2 it checks whether the DNA has been replicated completely and without errors. In metaphase the spindle checkpoint verifies that every chromosome is attached to spindle fibres from both poles; only then may anaphase begin.

### bio-e02-q14 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.5 | difficulty hard

#### Question

The gene SRC codes for a protein that stimulates cell division; it is a proto-oncogene. The gene TP53 codes for the protein p53, which stops the cell cycle when DNA is damaged; it is a tumour suppressor gene.

Cell K has one allele of SRC mutated into a constantly active form; its other SRC allele is normal. Cell L has one allele of TP53 destroyed by a mutation; its other TP53 allele is intact.

Predict in which of the two cells the control of cell division is disturbed, and explain your prediction for both cells.

#### Answer

Only in cell K: one mutated proto-oncogene allele (an oncogene) is already enough for constant stimulation of division, because stimulation works dominantly. In cell L the remaining intact TP53 allele still produces working p53, so the brake still works — for a tumour suppressor gene usually both alleles must fail.

#### Solution

A proto-oncogene codes for a protein that stimulates division. If one allele mutates into a constantly active oncogene, the cell receives a permanent "divide" signal even though the other allele is normal: stimulation does not need both alleles. Cell K therefore divides without control. A tumour suppressor gene codes for a brake on division. As long as one intact allele of TP53 remains, enough p53 is made to stop the cycle when DNA is damaged; the brake only fails when both alleles are lost. So in cell L the control is (still) intact, although the cell has less reserve.
*Marking:* • one mutated SRC allele suffices: oncogene gives a constant "divide" signal (1) • in L one intact TP53 allele still makes working p53, so the brake works (1) • correct conclusion: control disturbed only in K (1)

### bio-e02-q15 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In a tumour cell line the gene for a spindle-checkpoint protein is mutated, so this checkpoint no longer works.

Describe what can go wrong during the division of these cells, and explain the consequence for the chromosome numbers of the daughter cells.

#### Answer

The cells start anaphase even when not all chromosomes are attached to the spindle, so chromatids or whole chromosomes are shared out unevenly; the daughter cells receive a wrong chromosome number (too many or too few: aneuploidy).

#### Solution

The spindle checkpoint normally postpones anaphase until every chromosome is attached to spindle fibres from both poles. With the checkpoint broken, anaphase can start with unattached or wrongly attached chromosomes. Those chromosomes or chromatids then end up at random at one of the poles instead of being shared evenly, so daughter cells arise with one or more chromosomes too many or too few. This unequal sharing makes the tumour cells genetically unstable and speeds up further abnormalities.
*Marking:* • anaphase can start with unattached/wrongly attached chromosomes (1) • uneven distribution → daughter cells with abnormal chromosome numbers (aneuploidy) (1)

### bio-e02-q16 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Researchers treat cultured tumour cells with short interfering RNA that is complementary to the mRNA of cyclin D, a protein that takes cells from G1 into S phase. They then measure what percentage of the cells is in S phase:

| Treatment | Cells in S phase |
|---|---|
| untreated | 34% |
| interfering RNA against cyclin D | 12% |

Explain how the interfering RNA lowers the amount of cyclin D, and explain what the results show about the division of the treated cells.

#### Answer

The interfering RNA binds, in a protein complex, by base pairing to the cyclin-D mRNA, which is then broken down (or no longer translated) — the gene is silenced after transcription, so less cyclin D is made. With less cyclin D fewer cells pass from G1 into S: division slows down (12% instead of 34% in S phase).

#### Solution

In RNA interference a short RNA molecule in a protein complex binds to an mRNA with a complementary sequence; the mRNA is then degraded or blocked, so the gene is switched off after transcription and little of the protein is produced. Cyclin D is needed to pass the G1 checkpoint into S phase. If little cyclin D is made, far fewer cells enter S phase, as the table shows (12% instead of 34%): the cells divide more slowly. Researchers use interfering RNA this way to study the role of cell-cycle genes.
*Marking:* • complementary binding to the mRNA → mRNA broken down/blocked, less protein (1) • conclusion: fewer cells enter S phase, division is slowed (1)

### bio-e02-q17 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.6 | difficulty easy

#### Question

Immediately after division, a daughter cell is much smaller than its mother cell was.

Describe how such a daughter cell grows and develops before it can divide again, and name one kind of organelle that is not formed anew by mitosis but divides by itself.

#### Answer

During G1 the daughter cell grows: it makes new proteins, membranes and organelles until it reaches normal size, after which it can replicate its DNA in S phase (or differentiate instead). Mitochondria (and in plants chloroplasts) grow and divide by themselves and are shared out over the daughter cells at division.

#### Solution

After cytokinesis the daughter cell is about half the size of the mother cell. In G1 it synthesises proteins, membranes and new organelles and does its normal work until it is big enough for the next cycle; then it can pass the G1 checkpoint and replicate its DNA in S phase. Alternatively it leaves the cycle (G0) and differentiates. Mitochondria and chloroplasts are semi-autonomous: they grow and divide by themselves and are distributed over the two daughter cells at cytokinesis.
*Marking:* • growth by synthesis of proteins/membranes/organelles in G1 (1) • mitochondria (or chloroplasts) divide by themselves and are shared over the daughters (1)

### bio-e02-q18 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.6 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A stem cell in the bone marrow divides. One daughter cell remains a stem cell; the other daughter cell develops into a red blood cell.

Describe this pattern of division and development, and explain how a daughter cell can become a red blood cell without losing genes from its genome — and which change of form in that development actually fits the function of a red blood cell.

#### Answer

This is unequal (asymmetrical) division: one daughter renews the stem cell, the other differentiates. Differentiation means genes are switched on and off — the genome stays complete (stem and blood cell have the same DNA). Fitting change of form: the maturing red blood cell loses its nucleus, making room for haemoglobin, which transports O₂.

#### Solution

Stem cells often divide unequally: division maintains the stem-cell stock (self-renewal) while the other daughter cell differentiates. Differentiation is not gene loss but gene regulation: in the future red blood cell the genes for haemoglobin are strongly switched on and other genes are switched off, while virtually the full genome is retained — that is why a specialised cell can in principle still be reprogrammed. One fitting change of form is the loss of the nucleus (an exception to "no loss of genetic material"): without a nucleus there is more room for haemoglobin, which increases the oxygen-transporting capacity of the cell.
*Marking:* • unequal division: one daughter stays a stem cell, the other differentiates (1) • differentiation = switching genes on and off; genome retained (1) • loss of the nucleus → more room for haemoglobin → better O₂ transport (1)

### bio-e02-q19 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.7 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In a screening programme in Limburg, 30 000 people aged 55–60 in whom a small benign polyp had been found in the large intestine were followed for ten years. Results after ten years:

| Outcome | Share of the participants |
|---|---|

#### Answer

0.7% of 30 000 = 0.007 × 30 000 = 210 participants developed a malignant tumour. Because a benign polyp can slowly turn malignant (percentage rising with time), removing detected polyps is worthwhile: it prevents these 210 cases of cancer.

0.7% of 30 000 equals 210 people. ### bio-e02-q20 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.7 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Many chemotherapy drugs damage DNA during replication or block the spindle apparatus, and in this way kill cells that are in the middle of dividing. Patients treated with chemotherapy often lose their hair and develop too few blood cells.

Explain these side effects.

#### Answer

The drugs do not distinguish tumour cells from normal cells: they hit every cell that divides often. Hair-follicle cells and blood-forming stem cells in the bone marrow are among the fastest-dividing normal cells, so follicles are damaged (hair loss) and blood-cell production falls (low blood counts).

#### Solution

Chemotherapy interferes with processes that every dividing cell needs: replicating DNA or building the spindle. Tumour cells divide fast, which is why they are hit, but the drugs also hit fast-dividing healthy cells. The cells in the hair follicles that produce hair divide almost continuously and are damaged — the hair falls out. The stem cells in the bone marrow divide fast to replace red and white blood cells; when their division is blocked, the number of new blood cells drops. Less fast-dividing tissues suffer less, which is why these side effects are typical for fast-dividing tissues.
*Marking:* • chemotherapy hits all (fast-)dividing cells, not only tumour cells (1) • hair follicles and bone-marrow stem cells divide fast → hair loss and low blood counts (1)

### bio-e02-q22 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.8 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A patient with breast cancer develops a second tumour, this time in a bone.

Explain why this bone tumour consists of breast cells and not of bone cells, and explain why the presence of metastases makes the treatment of cancer more difficult.

#### Answer

The tumour in the bone is a metastasis: cells that broke loose from the breast tumour and travelled through blood or lymph, so they are descendants of the breast tumour — breast cells, not bone cells. Because the cancer is now present at several places in the body, it can no longer be removed with one local operation or irradiation, while every metastasis can keep growing.

#### Solution

Metastasis means that cells detach from the primary (malignant) tumour, are carried by the blood or lymph and start dividing somewhere else. The new tumour is built from descendants of those breast-tumour cells: a metastasis in bone is still breast cancer, not bone cancer. Surgery or radiotherapy can remove or destroy a local tumour, but metastases are spread over different organs, often too many or too small to find and remove all of them; every remaining focus forms a new source of cancer cells. That is why early detection, before metastasis, improves the outlook so much.
*Marking:* • broken-loose breast-tumour cells spread via blood/lymph → metastasis consists of breast cells (1) • treatment harder: tumour present at several sites, not removable locally (1)

### bio-e02-q23 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.9 | difficulty hard

#### Question

In a normal cell, copying errors and other DNA damage are usually repaired, or the cell is stopped from dividing. In a cell line that divides in an uncontrolled way, errors in the DNA often remain uncorrected.

Argue how DNA errors can remain uncorrected in an uncontrolled dividing cell line, and what the long-term consequence is for the cells of that line.

#### Answer

Normally the checkpoints (repair proteins such as p53) stop the cycle until the DNA is repaired, or start apoptosis. In uncontrolled division the checkpoint and repair genes are themselves often damaged, so the cell replicates and divides with the damage still present; the error then becomes a permanent mutation in all daughter cells, and with every further round of division new errors are added — the line becomes ever more abnormal.

#### Solution

A normal cell with DNA damage is halted at the G1 or G2 checkpoint: repair enzymes correct the damage, or — if repair fails — the cell kills itself by apoptosis, so errors are rarely passed on. In uncontrolled division the very genes for these brakes (checkpoint proteins, repair enzymes, apoptosis proteins) are usually mutated. The cell then replicates damaged DNA without pausing, so the error is fixed in the DNA of every daughter cell. Each fast round of division adds new errors — and with a broken spindle checkpoint even whole chromosomes can be misdistributed — so mutations accumulate: the cell line becomes genetically unstable and produces ever more aggressive variants.
*Marking:* • normal: checkpoints stop the cycle for repair, or apoptosis removes the cell (1) • in uncontrolled division these checkpoints/repair genes are broken, so division proceeds with damaged DNA (1) • errors are copied into all descendants and accumulate over rounds (genetic instability) (1)

### bio-e02-q24 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.10 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In 1961 Leonard Hayflick showed that human fibroblasts in culture can divide only about fifty times. Then the cells stop dividing for good; this limit is named after him.

Show with a calculation that the descendants of one such cell could in theory number about 1.1 × 10¹⁵ cells, and give one reason why a real culture never reaches that number.

#### Answer

After 50 rounds of division one cell would give 2⁵⁰ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵ descendants. A real culture never reaches this because cells die along the way, not every cell divides in every round, and nutrients and space run out.

#### Solution

Every round of division doubles the cell number, so 50 rounds give 2⁵⁰ cells. Working it out: 2¹⁰ ≈ 1.024 × 10³, so 2⁵⁰ = (2¹⁰)⁵ ≈ (1.024 × 10³)⁵ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵. This is more than all the cells in a human body (~10¹³–10¹⁴): the Hayflick limit does not normally restrict growth. In practice the theoretical number is never reached: part of the cells dies in every round, not all cells divide simultaneously, and in the culture dish nutrients and space become limiting.
*Marking:* • doubling per round: number = 2ⁿ (1) • 2⁵⁰ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵ (1) • realistic reason: cell death / not all cells divide every round / nutrients or space limit (1)

### bio-e02-q25 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.11 | difficulty hard

#### Question

In cultured human skin fibroblasts the telomeres shorten by an average of 180 base pairs (bp) per division. At the start of the culture the telomeres measure 10.4 kilobase pairs (kb). The cells stop dividing when their telomeres have shrunk to 3.2 kb.

Calculate the telomere length after 25 divisions, and calculate after how many divisions these cells stop dividing. Germ cells and many stem cells keep dividing far beyond this number. Name the enzyme involved and describe its action.

#### Answer

After 25 divisions: 10.4 − 25 × 0.18 = 10.4 − 4.5 = 5.9 kb. Stop after (10.4 − 3.2) / 0.18 = 7.2 / 0.18 = 40 divisions. The enzyme is telomerase: it adds new telomere repeats to the chromosome ends, so the telomeres do not shorten.

#### Solution

Loss per division is 180 bp = 0.18 kb. After 25 divisions the telomeres measure 10.4 − 25 × 0.18 = 5.9 kb. Shortening may proceed until 3.2 kb remains, so the total allowed loss is 10.4 − 3.2 = 7.2 kb; at 0.18 kb per division that is 7.2 / 0.18 = 40 divisions. In germ cells, embryonic cells and many stem cells the enzyme telomerase is active: using its own RNA template it adds new repeats to the ends (telomeres) of the chromosomes, compensating for the shortening that inevitably occurs at each DNA replication. Most somatic cells lack telomerase, which is what sets their limit of about 50 divisions.
*Marking:* • 5.9 kb after 25 divisions (10.4 − 4.5) (1) • (10.4 − 3.2) / 0.18 = 40 divisions (1) • telomerase adds new telomere repeats to the chromosome ends (1)

### bio-e02-q26 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.11 | difficulty medium

#### Question

HeLa cells originate from a cervical tumour that was removed from a patient in 1951. The cells still divide in laboratories today, many thousands of divisions later, and show no sign of ageing.

Explain which property of the HeLa cells allows them to escape the Hayflick limit, and describe the role of telomeres in a normal body cell.

#### Answer

In HeLa cells (as in most cancer cells) telomerase is switched on, so the telomeres are continually lengthened and never give the "stop" signal — the cells are immortal. In a normal body cell the telomeres form a protective cap on the chromosome ends and shorten with every division; when they become too short, the cell stops dividing (ageing).

#### Solution

Normal somatic cells lack telomerase, so each replication removes a piece of telomere; after about fifty divisions the telomeres are too short, the cell reads the ends as DNA damage and stops dividing (the Hayflick limit). HeLa cells have telomerase switched back on, so their chromosome ends are rebuilt after every division — the division counter never runs out and the cells divide indefinitely. In normal cells the telomere cap also hides the chromosome ends from the DNA-repair system, so that healthy ends are not treated as breaks.
*Marking:* • telomerase reactivated in the cancer cells → telomeres stay long → unlimited division (1) • telomeres = protective caps that shorten per division and stop the cell when too short (1)

### bio-e02-q27 | points 3 | outcome E2.1.12 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A seed producer multiplies a valuable plant variety with plant tissue culture: small pieces of shoot tip grow on a gel with plant hormones into thousands of plantlets, which are sold to growers.

Explain why all plantlets grown from one shoot tip are genetically identical, and give one advantage and one risk of this uniformity for the grower.

#### Answer

The tissue multiplies only by mitosis — without meiosis and without fertilisation — so every plantlet is an exact copy (a clone) of the donor plant. Advantage: all plants have the same desirable properties (and a shoot-tip culture is often virus-free). Risk: all plants are equally susceptible to the same disease or pest, so an infection can wipe out the whole crop.

#### Solution

Plant tissue culture is asexual reproduction: the pieces of tissue grow and divide by mitosis only. No reduction division and no fusion of gametes take place, so no new combinations of alleles arise — all plantlets have exactly the genotype of the original plant. The grower benefits because every plant shows the same desired traits (yield, quality, harvest time), and cultures from shoot meristems are usually virus-free. The downside is that the uniform genotype means uniform vulnerability: one fungus or virus to which this variety is susceptible can destroy the entire harvest.
*Marking:* • multiplication by mitosis only (no meiosis/fertilisation) → clones, identical genotype (1) • advantage: uniform quality / virus-free (1) • risk: all equally vulnerable to the same disease (1)

### bio-e02-q29 | points 2 | outcome E2.1.12 | difficulty medium

#### Question

At a cancer research centre, substances are tested for carcinogenic properties with cultures of normal human cells. After exposure to a substance, the cells are examined for signs of change into tumour cells.

Give two properties of the cultured cells that point to a change towards tumour cells, and give one reason why cell cultures are a suitable first test for this purpose.

#### Answer

Any two of: the cells no longer show contact inhibition (they pile up in layers); they keep dividing without growth factors; they divide without attachment to a surface; they divide indefinitely (no Hayflick limit); they show abnormal chromosome numbers. Cell cultures are suitable because results are obtained quickly and cheaply, under controlled conditions, and with fewer animal experiments.

#### Solution

Normal human cells in culture stop dividing when they touch each other (contact inhibition), need growth factors and a surface, and stop after about fifty divisions. If after exposure to a substance the cells lose exactly these properties — they pile up in layers, divide without growth factors or anchorage, or become "immortal" — they behave like tumour cells, which is a warning that the substance may be carcinogenic. Such a culture test is a quick, cheap, well-controlled first screening that does not use experimental animals; positive results still have to be confirmed in further tests (for example with animals or epidemiology), because a dish lacks organs, a blood supply and an immune system.
*Marking:* • two correct signs: loss of contact inhibition (piling up), division without growth factors/attachment, unlimited division, abnormal chromosomes (1) • reason: fast/cheap/controlled test with fewer animal experiments (1)
