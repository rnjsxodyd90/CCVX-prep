# B1 — Protein Synthesis: practice questions

Ten original practice questions in CCVX style: open questions set in a context, with points per part and partial credit. All contexts, data and questions are original work; nothing is copied from CCVX or CvTE exams. In the real exam you have BINAS (5th edition or later); where a question needs the genetic code, a small excerpt of the BINAS genetic-code table is given. Worked answers with marking points are at the end.

Covered: B1.1 (DNA) and B1.2 (protein synthesis), all outcomes.

## Questions

### Question 1 — DNA from a hot-spring bacterium (B1.1, outcome 1)

Biologists isolate DNA from a bacterium that lives in hot springs at about 75 °C. Analysis shows that 37% of its bases are cytosine.

**1a.** Give the percentages of the other three bases in this DNA. Explain your answers. (3 points)

**1b.** A second bacterial species, from pond water at 20 °C, has DNA of which 26% of the bases are cytosine. Explain which of the two DNA samples will separate into single strands at the lower temperature when heated. (2 points)

**1c.** A fragment of the hot-spring DNA of exactly 1000 base pairs has the same base composition as the whole DNA of this bacterium. Calculate the total number of hydrogen bonds in this fragment. (2 points)

**1d.** Does the rule "%A = %T and %G = %C" also hold for the mRNA of this bacterium? Explain. (1 point)

### Question 2 — Human insulin from bacteria (B1.1, outcome 2; B1.2, outcome 2)

Since the 1980s, human insulin has been produced by bacteria such as *E. coli*. The bacteria are not given the human insulin gene as it occurs in the chromosome, but a cDNA copy of it, made from mRNA that was isolated from β-cells of the pancreatic islets.

**2a.** Explain why the bacteria are given a cDNA copy made from mRNA instead of the gene as it occurs in the human chromosome. (2 points)

**2b.** Name the enzyme that is used to make cDNA from mRNA, and name the group of viruses this enzyme originally comes from. (2 points)

**2c.** The cDNA is built into a plasmid before it is brought into the bacteria. Give two properties that a plasmid must have to be usable for this. (2 points)

**2d.** The bacterial cells translate the human mRNA code into exactly the same amino-acid sequence as human cells do. Explain how that is possible. (1 point)

### Question 3 — PCR at the forensic laboratory (B1.1, outcome 3)

A forensic laboratory finds a tiny blood stain. For the DNA profile, one region of 400 base pairs is multiplied with the polymerase chain reaction (PCR). At the start there is exactly one double-stranded DNA molecule of the region, plus an excess of primers, free DNA nucleotides and Taq polymerase.

**3a.** PCR repeats one cycle of three temperature steps. Name these three steps and describe what happens to the DNA or the primers in each step. (3 points)

**3b.** Calculate the number of double-stranded copies of the 400-bp region that is present in theory after 25 cycles, at 100% efficiency. Give your answer in the form 4.2 × 10⁷ (one decimal × 10 to a power). (2 points)

**3c.** Two different primers are needed for each PCR. Explain why one primer is not enough, and explain why the polymerase can only extend a primer at its 3' end. (3 points)

**3d.** A PCR primer is about 20 nucleotides long. Show with a calculation that a random sequence of 20 bases is expected to occur at most once by chance in the human genome of 3.2 × 10⁹ base pairs. (2 points)

### Question 4 — Locating an inserted gene (B1.1, outcome 3)

To check a genetically modified plant, biologists map the DNA around an inserted gene. A linear DNA fragment from the plant has a total length of 10.0 kilobase pairs (kb). The fragment contains two sites for the restriction enzyme HindIII, at 2.5 kb and at 7.5 kb from the left end, and one site for the restriction enzyme EcoRI at 4.0 kb from the left end.

**4a.** The fragment is cut with HindIII only. Give the number of fragments and the length of each. (2 points)

**4b.** The fragment is cut with HindIII and EcoRI together. Give the number of fragments and the length of each. (2 points)

**4c.** EcoRI cuts between the G and the A of the sequence 5'-GAATTC-3' on both strands, so it leaves short single-stranded overhangs. Explain what these "sticky ends" are and why they are useful when a DNA fragment is built into a plasmid. (2 points)

**4d.** After cutting, the fragments are separated with gel electrophoresis. Explain why DNA fragments move towards the positive pole and why shorter fragments move faster through the gel than longer fragments. (2 points)

### Question 5 — From DNA to mRNA (B1.2, outcomes 1 and 2)

A research group studies a small human gene. Part of the template (matrix) strand of this gene reads:

3'-TAC AGC TTA ATC-5'

**5a.** Give the sequence of the mRNA that is transcribed from this template strand, written with the 5' and 3' ends. (2 points)

**5b.** Give the sequence of the coding strand of this part of the gene, written with the 5' and 3' ends. (1 point)

**5c.** Name the DNA sequence to which RNA polymerase binds to start transcription of a gene. (1 point)

**5d.** In eukaryotic cells, transcription takes place in the nucleus and translation on ribosomes in the cytoplasm. Explain how the mRNA gets from the nucleus to the ribosomes, and name one advantage of this arrangement for the cell. (2 points)

### Question 6 — An antifreeze peptide (B1.2, outcomes 1 and 2)

Fish in polar seas produce small antifreeze proteins. Part of the mRNA for one of these proteins reads:

5'-AUG UGU GGU CCA AAG UAA-3'

Genetic code (from the BINAS table): AUG Met, UGU Cys, GGU Gly, CCA Pro, AAG Lys, AGG Arg, UAA stop.

**6a.** Give the amino-acid sequence that is translated from this mRNA. (2 points)

**6b.** Give the anticodon of the tRNA that delivers lysine, written with the 3' and 5' ends, and explain what this anticodon does during translation. (2 points)

**6c.** Explain why translation stops at the codon UAA and not at another codon. (2 points)

**6d.** A mutation changes the codon AAG into AGG. Give the consequence for the amino-acid sequence, and give the name of this type of point mutation. (2 points)

### Question 7 — Mutations in a pigmentation gene (B1.2, outcomes 1 and 3)

Researchers study mutations in a human gene for an enzyme involved in pigment formation. Part of the coding strand of the healthy allele reads:

5'-ATG GGC TTT CAG TAA-3'

Genetic code (from the BINAS table): AUG Met, GGC Gly, GGT Gly, UUU Phe, UUG Leu, CAG Gln, UAG stop, UAA stop.

**7a.** In one patient, the sixth base of this fragment, C, is replaced by T, so the second codon reads GGT instead of GGC. Give the consequence for the amino-acid sequence and explain your answer using the table. (2 points)

**7b.** In a second patient, the codon CAG has become TAG. Give the consequence for the length of the protein chain and explain why. (2 points)

**7c.** In a third patient, a single nucleotide in the second codon has been deleted. Explain why this mutation usually has much more serious consequences for the protein than the substitution in question 7a, and explain why a deletion of exactly three nucleotides in this region is usually less serious than a deletion of one. (3 points)

### Question 8 — Folding a protein (B1.2, outcome 3)

Lysozyme is an enzyme in tears and saliva that breaks down bacterial cell walls. It is a single polypeptide chain of 130 amino acids with four disulfide bridges.

**8a.** Name the four levels of protein structure, and for each level name the bond(s) that maintain it. (4 points)

**8b.** A student claims: "When lysozyme is boiled, its peptide bonds break and the protein falls apart into loose amino acids." Assess this claim. (2 points)

**8c.** Antibodies consist of four polypeptide chains that stay together in the bloodstream for weeks. Explain which level of protein structure is involved here, and explain the role of disulfide bridges. (2 points)

**8d.** In sickle-cell anaemia, the sixth amino acid of the β-chain of haemoglobin, glutamic acid, is replaced by valine. Glutamic acid has a charged side group; valine has a hydrophobic side group. Explain how this single substitution can make haemoglobin molecules stick together into fibres when the oxygen concentration is low. (3 points)

### Question 9 — Exon skipping in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (B1.2, outcomes 1, 3 and 4)

In Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), a deletion in the dystrophin gene shifts the reading frame of the mRNA downstream of the deletion, so that a stop codon appears far too early and no working dystrophin is made. In the milder Becker form, the deletion leaves the reading frame intact and a shorter but partly functional dystrophin is produced. Medicines called antisense oligonucleotides (AONs) are short synthetic RNA-like molecules. They bind to one chosen exon in the pre-mRNA, so that the splicing machinery skips that exon as well.

*In this simplified example, a patient has a deletion of exons 49 and 50; after splicing, exon 48 is joined to exon 51 in a shifted reading frame.*

**9a.** Explain what is meant by "shifted reading frame" and why such a shift usually leads to an early stop codon. (3 points)

**9b.** The patient is treated with an AON against exon 51. Explain how this treatment can restore the reading frame downstream of exon 48. (3 points)

**9c.** Explain why the treated patient still does not produce completely normal dystrophin, and why the effect can nevertheless resemble the milder Becker form. (2 points)

### Question 10 — Assessing an insect-resistant crop (B1.1, outcome 3; B1.2, outcomes 1, 2 and 4)

Maize with a gene from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt maize) produces a protein, Bt toxin, that kills certain caterpillars. A Dutch assessment bureau evaluates an application to grow Bt maize.

**10a.** The Bt gene comes from a bacterium but is correctly translated in the cells of the maize plant. Explain how that is possible. (2 points)

**10b.** Describe the steps needed to construct a plasmid that makes maize cells produce Bt toxin, from isolating the Bt gene to the production of the protein in the plant cell. Name the enzymes involved and explain why the Bt gene alone is not enough: a second DNA element must be placed in front of it. (4 points)

**10c.** The bureau wants to know the exact base sequence at the site where the new DNA is inserted in the maize DNA. Name the technique that determines this sequence, and name the technique used first to multiply the small amount of DNA at that site. (2 points)

**10d.** Bt toxin is eaten by humans in maize products. Explain what normally happens to proteins such as Bt toxin during digestion, and explain why the bureau nevertheless compares the amino-acid sequence of Bt toxin with the sequences of known allergens. (3 points)

## Answers

**1a.** Guanine pairs with cytosine, so %G = %C = 37%. The remaining bases: 100 − (37 + 37) = 26%, so %A = %T = 13%.
*Marking:* • G = 37% with reference to base pairing (1) • A + T = 26% (1) • A = T = 13% (1)

**1b.** The pond-water DNA separates at the lower temperature. It has fewer G–C pairs (52%) than the hot-spring DNA (74%); G–C pairs are held by three hydrogen bonds and A–T pairs by two, so DNA richer in G–C needs more heat (energy) to separate.
*Marking:* • correct choice, pond-water DNA (1) • G–C has three hydrogen bonds, A–T two, so more energy is needed with more G–C (1)

**1c.** The fragment has 1000 base pairs = 2000 bases. Composition: 37% C and 37% G → 740 G–C pairs; 13% A and 13% T → 260 A–T pairs. Hydrogen bonds: 740 × 3 + 260 × 2 = 2220 + 520 = **2740**.
*Marking:* • correct numbers of G–C and A–T pairs (740 and 260) (1) • correct calculation and answer 2740 (1)

**1d.** No. mRNA is single-stranded, so there is no complementary strand to enforce pairing; %A need not equal %U and %G need not equal %C.
*Marking:* • no, with reference to the single strand (1)

**2a.** The chromosomal insulin gene contains introns, and *E. coli* cannot remove introns (no splicing). cDNA is copied from mature mRNA, so it contains only the exons and can be expressed correctly in bacteria.
*Marking:* • human gene contains introns / bacterial expression needs exons only (1) • cDNA is made from mature (spliced) mRNA (1)

**2b.** Enzyme: reverse transcriptase. Origin: retroviruses.
*Marking:* • reverse transcriptase (1) • retroviruses (1)

**2c.** Any two of: • an origin of replication, so the bacterium copies the plasmid (1) • one or more restriction sites, to insert the cDNA (1) • a marker gene, for example for antibiotic resistance, to select bacteria that took up the plasmid (1) • a promoter that works in the host cell (1)

**2d.** The genetic code is (almost) universal: each codon codes for the same amino acid in bacteria and in humans.
*Marking:* • universal genetic code (1)

**3a.** Denaturation, about 95 °C: the strands separate (hydrogen bonds break). Annealing, about 50–65 °C: the primers pair with their complementary sequences on both strands. Extension, about 72 °C: Taq polymerase builds the new strands from the primers with free nucleotides.
*Marking:* • denaturation with strand separation at high temperature (1) • annealing = primer binding at lowered temperature (1) • extension = synthesis by (Taq) polymerase (1)

**3b.** Each cycle doubles the number of molecules: after 25 cycles, 2²⁵ = 33,554,432 ≈ **3.4 × 10⁷ copies**.
*Marking:* • use of 2²⁵ (1) • answer in correct form, 3.4 × 10⁷ (1)

**3c.** The two strands are antiparallel, and the target is copied on both strands in opposite directions, so each strand needs its own primer at the other end of the target. DNA polymerase builds a new strand only 5' → 3' and needs a free 3'-OH end to attach the next nucleotide, so it extends the primer's 3' end.
*Marking:* • two strands copied in opposite directions / both ends of the target must be defined (1) • synthesis only 5'→3' (1) • extension from a free 3' end (1)

**3d.** With 4 possible bases per position there are 4²⁰ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹² different sequences of 20 bases. The genome offers only ~3.2 × 10⁹ starting positions; chance of a given 20-mer occurring once is ≈ 3.2 × 10⁹ / 1.1 × 10¹² ≈ 3 × 10⁻³, far below 1.
*Marking:* • 4²⁰ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹² (1) • comparison with 3.2 × 10⁹ and conclusion (1)

**4a.** Three fragments: 2.5 kb, 5.0 kb and 2.5 kb.
*Marking:* • three fragments (1) • lengths 2.5 – 5.0 – 2.5 kb (1)

**4b.** Four fragments: 2.5 kb (0–2.5), 1.5 kb (2.5–4.0), 3.5 kb (4.0–7.5) and 2.5 kb (7.5–10.0).
*Marking:* • four fragments (1) • lengths 2.5 – 1.5 – 3.5 – 2.5 kb (1)

**4c.** A sticky end is the short single-stranded overhang left after the staggered cut (here AATT). Two molecules cut with the same enzyme have complementary overhangs that base-pair, so the fragment fits into the plasmid in a fixed position and DNA ligase can seal the backbones.
*Marking:* • single-stranded overhang from the staggered cut (1) • complementary pairing and sealing by ligase (1)

**4d.** The phosphate groups give DNA a negative charge, so fragments migrate to the positive pole in the electric field. The gel acts as a sieve: shorter fragments pass through the pores more easily and therefore move faster (travel further in the same time).
*Marking:* • negative charge (phosphate) and movement to the positive pole (1) • sieving effect: shorter fragments move faster (1)

**5a.** 5'-AUG UCG AAU UAG-3'. (Template A→U, T→A, C→G, G→C; the mRNA is complementary and antiparallel to the template strand.)
*Marking:* • all four triplets correct in the right order (1) • correct 5' and 3' ends (1)

**5b.** 5'-ATG TCG AAT TAG-3' (same as the mRNA, with T instead of U).
*Marking:* • sequence correct with T (1)

**5c.** The promoter.
*Marking:* • promoter (1)

**5d.** The mRNA leaves the nucleus through the nuclear pores into the cytoplasm, where ribosomes are. Advantage (any one): the mRNA can first be spliced/processed and quality-checked; the DNA itself stays protected in the nucleus; the cell can regulate which mRNAs are exported or broken down.
*Marking:* • through nuclear pores (1) • a correct advantage, e.g. processing/quality control or protection of DNA (1)

**6a.** Met–Cys–Gly–Pro–Lys (translation stops at UAA).
*Marking:* • five amino acids correct and in order (2, or 1 with one mistake); stop at UAA required for full marks

**6b.** Anticodon 3'-UUC-5'. The anticodon pairs complementarily (and antiparallel) with the codon AAG in the mRNA, so the tRNA delivers its amino acid (lysine) at exactly the right position.
*Marking:* • anticodon 3'-UUC-5' (1) • complementary pairing with the codon places the amino acid correctly (1)

**6c.** UAA is a stop codon: no tRNA has the matching anticodon, so no amino acid is delivered; instead a release factor binds and the finished chain is released. Other codons each have a matching tRNA.
*Marking:* • no tRNA fits a stop codon (1) • release factor / chain released (1)

**6d.** AAG (Lys) becomes AGG (Arg): one amino acid is replaced by another; this is a missense mutation (a substitution).
*Marking:* • Lys replaced by Arg (1) • missense/substitution (1)

**7a.** No consequence: GGC and GGT both code for glycine, so the amino-acid sequence is unchanged (silent mutation). The genetic code is degenerate.
*Marking:* • no change in the sequence (1) • both codons code for Gly / code is degenerate (1)

**7b.** The protein becomes shorter by at least one amino acid (the chain ends after Met–Gly–Phe). Coding TAG gives mRNA UAG, a stop codon, so translation stops at the fourth codon instead of after Gln.
*Marking:* • new stop codon UAG (1) • premature end of translation, shorter chain (1)

**7c.** Deleting one nucleotide shifts the reading frame for the whole rest of the mRNA: every downstream codon is read differently, so most or all following amino acids are wrong and a stop codon usually appears early. Deleting exactly three nucleotides removes one complete codon: the frame is kept and only one amino acid is missing.
*Marking:* • frameshift changes all downstream codons (1) • many wrong amino acids / often early stop codon (1) • 3-nucleotide deletion removes one codon but keeps the frame (1)

**8a.** Primary structure: the amino-acid sequence, held by peptide bonds. Secondary structure: α-helix/β-sheet of the backbone, held by hydrogen bonds. Tertiary structure: overall folding of one chain, held by bonds between side groups (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges, hydrophobic interactions). Quaternary structure: several chains together, held by bonds between the chains (non-covalent bonds and sometimes disulfide bridges).
*Marking:* • primary with peptide bonds (1) • secondary with hydrogen bonds (1) • tertiary with side-group bonds (1) • quaternary with several chains (1)

**8b.** The claim is wrong. Boiling breaks the weak bonds (such as hydrogen bonds) of the secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure: the protein denatures and loses its shape, but the peptide bonds of the primary structure stay intact; they are covalent and are broken only by hydrolysis.
*Marking:* • claim rejected (1) • denaturation concerns folding, peptide bonds need hydrolysis (1)

**8c.** Quaternary structure (four polypeptide chains). Disulfide bridges are covalent S–S bonds between cysteines of chains; they are much stronger than hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions, so the four chains stay firmly connected.
*Marking:* • quaternary (1) • disulfide bridges are covalent and strong, holding the chains together (1)

**8d.** Valine is hydrophobic where glutamic acid was charged. The hydrophobic valine sits on the outside of the chain and sticks to hydrophobic parts of neighbouring haemoglobin molecules; at low oxygen concentration the molecules aggregate into fibres (and the red blood cells deform).
*Marking:* • valine is hydrophobic, glutamic acid charged (1) • hydrophobic attraction to neighbouring molecules (1) • aggregation into fibres at low O₂ (1)

**9a.** The reading frame is the grouping of the mRNA into triplets (codons), fixed by the start codon. After a shift, three bases are grouped differently, so all downstream codons change. Three of the 64 codons are stop codons, so in a shifted frame a stop codon appears by chance within about 21 codons on average, far sooner than the real end of the gene.
*Marking:* • frame = grouping into triplets (1) • downstream codons all read differently (1) • high chance of an early stop codon (1)

**9b.** The AON binds exon 51 in the pre-mRNA and hides it from the splicing machinery, so exon 51 is skipped too. Splicing then joins exon 48 directly to exon 52 (in this example), which restores the original grouping into codons downstream of the junction.
*Marking:* • exon 51 is skipped through binding of the AON (1) • exon 48 joined to exon 52 (1) • original reading frame restored downstream (1)

**9c.** The protein still lacks the amino acids that were encoded by exons 49–51, so it is not completely normal. But the reading frame is intact and the rest of the protein is normal, so it is a shortened, partly functional protein — comparable to a Becker-type dystrophin, which also has an internal deletion with an intact frame.
*Marking:* • amino acids of the deleted exons are missing → shorter protein (1) • intact frame gives a partly functional protein, like Becker (1)

**10a.** The genetic code is (almost) universal: every codon means the same amino acid in the bacterium and in maize, so plant ribosomes translate the bacterial gene into the same protein.
*Marking:* • universal code (1) • plant translation uses the same codon–amino-acid pairs (1)

**10b.** Steps: cut the Bt gene out of the bacterial DNA with a restriction enzyme; open a plasmid with the same restriction enzyme; join gene and plasmid with DNA ligase (sticky ends pair); place a promoter in front of the gene that is active in plant cells, because the bacterial promoter is not (or badly) recognised by plant RNA polymerase; multiply the plasmid in bacteria and/or transfer it into plant cells, where the gene is transcribed (RNA polymerase) and translated on ribosomes.
*Marking:* • restriction enzyme for cutting gene and plasmid (1) • ligase for joining (1) • promoter active in plant cells must be added (1) • gene then transcribed and translated in the plant (1)

**10c.** Determination of the sequence: (DNA) sequencing. Multiplying the small amount first: PCR.
*Marking:* • sequencing (1) • PCR (1)

**10d.** Dietary proteins are normally hydrolysed into single amino acids and short peptides by proteases in the stomach and small intestine, so an intact active protein is not taken up. But some proteins resist digestion, and resistant fragments can trigger allergy; a sequence resembling a known allergen is a warning sign, so the comparison is a precaution.
*Marking:* • hydrolysis by digestive (protease) enzymes (1) • intact protein normally not absorbed (1) • some proteins resist digestion, hence the allergen comparison (1)
