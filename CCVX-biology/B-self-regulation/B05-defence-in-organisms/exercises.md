# B5 — Defence in Organisms: practice questions

Ten original practice questions in CCVX style: open questions set in a context, with points per part and partial credit. All contexts, data and questions are original work; nothing is copied from CCVX or CvTE exams. In the real exam you have BINAS (5th edition or later); no BINAS table excerpt is needed here beyond the data given within the questions themselves. Worked answers with marking points are at the end.

Covered: B5.1 (Defence), outcomes 1, 2 and 3.

## Questions

### Question 1 — A rusty nail through a shoe (B5.1, outcome 1)

At a building site, a worker steps on a nail that goes through his shoe into his foot. The nail is dirty and rusty. His doctor gives him a tetanus booster shot and cleans the wound thoroughly. Tetanus bacteria live in soil and had been sitting on the nail.

**1a.** The worker's skin was the first barrier the bacteria had to pass. Give the name of the other barrier type in the human body that fulfils the same first-line function, and name one location where it is found. (2 points)

**1b.** Give two chemical defences, each with its location, that could kill bacteria without any wound being present. (2 points)

**1c.** Within hours the puncture wound becomes red, warm, swollen and painful. Explain how histamine and other mediators cause these four signs. (3 points)

**1d.** The doctor judges that a tetanus booster is needed. A colleague claims it is pointless because the worker already received a full tetanus vaccination as a child. Assess this claim. (1 point)

### Question 2 — Swollen glands in the neck (B5.1, outcome 1)

A 19-year-old student has a severe throat infection. Her doctor finds swollen, tender "glands" on both sides of her neck. These swellings are lymph nodes, and they hurt most when the infection is worst.

**2a.** Explain why lymph nodes in the neck swell during a throat infection. (2 points)

**2b.** Which two organs are involved in the formation of the lymphocytes that fill these lymph nodes? For each organ, state which type of lymphocyte (B cell or T cell) matures there. (2 points)

**2c.** A spleen and lymph nodes have a comparable function in defence. Describe the one essential difference between them in terms of which body fluid they filter. (1 point)

**2d.** Some white blood cells from the student's blood have entered her throat tissue and engulf bacteria there. Name the process by which these cells leave the blood and the name of the digestion process they use on the bacteria. (1 point)

### Question 3 — Four kinds of immunity (B5.1, outcome 2)

4. A tourist bitten by a venomous snake is immediately injected with antibodies (serum) against the venom.

**3a.** Copy the grid below and fill in the numbers 1–4 in the correct cells. (4 points)

| | Natural | Artificial |
|---|---|---|
| Active | ? | ? |
| Passive | ? | ? |

**3b.** Describe the difference between active and passive immunity in terms of who or what made the antibodies. (2 points)

**3c.** Explain why Situation 4 helps immediately but gives no long-term protection, while Situation 3 took weeks to build up but still protects after ten years. (2 points)

### Question 4 — Control of an experimental antiseptic (B5.1, outcome 1)

A research team tests a new wound antiseptic on human skin in the laboratory. They apply a bacteria suspension to skin samples, rinse with antiseptic, and dilute the remaining bacteria to count them. One control sample (no antiseptic) must be diluted 1:100 to give a countable plate. The volume of the undiluted sample is 2.24 mL, and the final volume after dilution must be exactly 224 mL.

**4a.** Calculate the volume of sample and the volume of dilution fluid needed to make this 1:100 dilution. (2 points)

**4b.** The actual plate shows 136 colonies after plating 0.10 mL of the dilution. Calculate the concentration of bacteria per mL in the undiluted control sample. (2 points)

**4c.** A treated sample gives only 4 colonies. The team concludes the antiseptic "kills 97% of bacteria". Show this percentage with a calculation. (2 points)

**4d.** The control sample is taken from the outer dead layer of the skin. Explain why bacteria on intact skin are normally not a threat, even in such large numbers. (2 points)

### Question 5 — Antibody titre after a flu vaccination (B5.1, outcomes 1 and 2)

Public-health researchers follow the immune response after an influenza vaccination. They measure the concentration of antibodies against haemagglutinin (the main surface protein of influenza virus) in the blood of one volunteer:

| Day | Antibody concentration (arbitrary units) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 5 |
| 7 | 15 |
| 14 | 60 |
| 21 | 120 |
| 28 | 95 |
| 90 | 20 |

One year later the volunteer is vaccinated again with the same vaccine.

**5a.** Describe how the second response to this repeated vaccination will differ from the first in speed, height and duration. (3 points)

**5b.** Explain which cells are responsible for the difference you described in question 5a, and where they came from. (2 points)

**5c.** The volunteer's antibodies disappear almost completely between day 90 and next year. Explain why a follow-up vaccination is nevertheless useful. (2 points)

**5d.** The vaccine contains inactivated influenza virus. Name the type of immunity it gives (choose from: natural/artificial × active/passive) and explain both classifications. (2 points)

### Question 6 — A macrophage's day (B5.1, outcome 2)

| The cytotoxic T cell recognizes the antigen on MHC-II. | ? |
| The B cell is activated by cytokines from the T helper cell. | ? |
| The macrophage itself produces antibodies against the bacterium. | ? |

**6a.** Copy the table and fill in "yes" or "no" for each statement. (6 points)

**6b.** Explain why the macrophage's MHC-II molecules show fragments of the streptococcus rather than fragments of the macrophage's own proteins. (2 points)

**6c.** Which of the three neighbouring cells does NOT participate in the direct humoral response that follows, and why not? (1 point)

### Question 7 — Assessing a quoted transplant figure (B5.1, outcome 2)

A newspaper reports on kidney transplantation: "The chance that two unrelated Dutch people have exactly the same MHC (HLA) type is less than 1%." For this question, assume that one particular MHC gene has four equally common alleles in the population, on each of the two chromosome copies, so 4 × 4 = 16 allele combinations per gene are possible in a person. Assume for simplicity that all 16 combinations are equally common.

**7a.** With these assumptions, what is the probability that two randomly chosen people have exactly the same combination of alleles (an "MHC match")? Show your calculation. (2 points)

**7b.** The newspaper's "less than 1%" is more precise than your answer to 7a. Give one reason why a calculation with more MHC genes would give a lower probability than your answer. (1 point)

**7c.** A recipient's T cells recognize the donor's MHC molecules as non-self and attack the transplant. Describe the two strategies that doctors use to limit this rejection, apart from choosing a well-matched donor. (2 points)

### Question 8 — An emergency blood transfusion (B5.1, outcome 2)

**8a.** Copy or complete the table below and give for the patient's own group A: which antigens are on his red blood cells and which antibodies are in his plasma. (2 points)

| Blood group | Antigens on red cells | Antibodies in plasma |
|---|---|---|
| A | ? | ? |

**8b.** Which of the three available concentrates can the patient receive without danger of clumping (agglutination) of the donor cells? Explain for each of the other two why it is dangerous. (3 points)

**8c.** The emergency doctor chooses group O rhesus-negative even though the patient is rhesus-positive. Explain why this is safe for the recipient, and why the reverse (giving rhesus-positive donor cells to a rhesus-negative, never-sensitized recipient) is also usually tolerated the first time but must never be repeated without a reason. ### Question 9 — A practical joke with a peanut (B5.1, outcome 2)

**9a.** Assess the first student's claim. (2 points)

**9b.** Assess the second student's claim. (1 point)

**9c.** Assess the third student's claim. (2 points)

### Question 10 — Greenhouse rose growing (B5.1, outcomes 1, 2 and 3)

A grower cultivates roses in greenhouses for cut flowers. He wants to protect them against fungi and aphids without spraying more pesticide than necessary. A colleague suggests he should choose a thornless rose variety "because thorns are useless against aphids". The grower also considers planting border strips of lavender, which produces scents, to repel insects, and he knows some rose varieties are officially "mildew-resistant".

**10a.** Name the two types of external defence CCVX distinguishes in plants, and give for each type one example mentioned in this context or that you know. (2 points)

**10b.** Explain why thorns protect roses against some attackers but not against aphids, and classify thorns into one of the two defence types from question 10a. (2 points)

**10c.** Lavender's scent is a chemical defence that does not come from inside the leaves but from oil glands on the surface. Cyanide in clover is one that *is* stored inside cells. Describe the difference in when these two defences act. (1 point)

**10d.** "Mildew-resistant" rose varieties recognize specific mildew strains with receptors in their cells and switch on their defences fast. Compare this "recognition" in plants with the recognition of non-self in human defence, and give the most important difference between the two immune systems. (3 points)

**10e.** The grower wants a reasoned decision about spraying. Argue one possible problem of frequent spraying with a pesticide that kills aphids, and argue one advantage of the resistant variety. (3 points)

## Answers

**1a.** Mucous membranes. Found for example in the airways (trachea and bronchi), the digestive tract or the urogenital tract.
*Marking:* • mucous membranes named (1) • one correct location (1)

**1b.** Any two of: lysozyme in tears and saliva (breaks down bacterial cell walls); strongly acidic gastric juice in the stomach (kills swallowed microorganisms); sebum and sweat make the skin surface slightly acidic.
*Marking:* • one chemical defence with its location (1) • a second, different chemical defence with its location (1)

**1c.** Histamine widens the arterioles: more blood flows to the wound, causing redness and warmth. It makes the capillaries leaky: plasma enters the tissue, causing the swelling. The swelling and the mediators stimulate pain receptors, causing pain.
*Marking:* • vasodilation → more blood → red and warm (1) • leaky capillaries → plasma into tissue → swelling (1) • stimulation of pain receptors (1)

**1d.** The claim is wrong. Protection from a childhood vaccination can fade; a booster dose makes the memory cells give a fast, strong secondary response, so protection is refreshed.
*Marking:* • claim rejected with reference to the booster renewing memory-based protection (1)

**2a.** Pathogens and their antigens from the throat are carried with the lymph to the neck lymph nodes. There the specific response starts: matching lymphocytes are activated and divide, and macrophages accumulate, so the node fills with cells and swells.
*Marking:* • antigens reach the lymph node via the lymph (1) • lymphocytes activated and multiply, so the node grows (1)

**2b.** Red bone marrow: B cells mature there (and all blood cells are made there). Thymus: T cells mature there.
*Marking:* • bone marrow with B cells (1) • thymus with T cells (1)

**2c.** Lymph nodes filter the lymph; the spleen filters the blood.
*Marking:* • lymph versus blood (1)

**2d.** They squeeze out through the capillary wall (diapedesis) and engulf the bacteria by phagocytosis.
*Marking:* • leaving the capillary named or described, and phagocytosis named (1)

**3a.** Natural-active: 1; artificial-active: 3; natural-passive: 2; artificial-passive: 4.
*Marking:* • 1 in natural-active (1) • 3 in artificial-active (1) • 2 in natural-passive (1) • 4 in artificial-passive (1)

**3b.** In active immunity the person's own B cells have made the antibodies and memory cells have formed. In passive immunity the person has received ready-made antibodies from outside.
*Marking:* • active = own antibodies and memory cells (1) • passive = received antibodies (1)

**3c.** Situation 4 is passive: the injected antibodies act immediately, but they are broken down and no memory cells are made, so protection fades within weeks to months. Situation 3 is active: the first (primary) response takes one to two weeks, but memory cells survive for years and keep protecting.
*Marking:* • passive: immediate but short-lived, no memory (1) • active: slow start but long-lived thanks to memory cells (1)

**4a.** For a 1:100 dilution the sample must be 224 mL / 100 = **2.24 mL**, and the dilution fluid 224 − 2.24 = **221.76 mL**.
*Marking:* • insight sample = final volume / 100 → 2.24 mL (1) • fluid = 221.76 mL (1)

**4b.** Dilution: 136 colonies from 0.10 mL → 136 / 0.10 = 1.36 × 10³ bacteria per mL. The sample was 100× diluted, so the undiluted sample contained 1.36 × 10³ × 100 = **1.36 × 10⁵ bacteria per mL**.
*Marking:* • colonies per mL in the dilution: 1.36 × 10³ (1) • multiplication by the dilution factor 100 (1)

**4c.** Treated sample: 4 / 0.10 × 100 = 4.0 × 10³ per mL. Killed fraction: 1 − (4.0 × 10³ / 1.36 × 10⁵) = 1 − 0.029 = 0.971 ≈ **97%**.
*Marking:* • correct concentration of the treated sample (1) • correct use of the ratio and answer ≈ 97% (1)

**4d.** The outer skin layer consists of dead, keratinized cells that microorganisms can hardly cross; the slightly acidic surface also inhibits growth. So as long as the skin stays intact, the bacteria stay outside the tissues and cannot cause infection.
*Marking:* • dead, keratinized layer as barrier (1) • bacteria cannot enter as long as the skin is intact (1)

**5a.** The second response starts sooner (a lag of days instead of a week or more), rises faster, reaches a much higher concentration, and lasts longer.
*Marking:* • sooner/faster (1) • higher peak (1) • longer duration (1)

**5b.** Memory cells (memory B cells, helped by memory T cells). They were made during the first vaccination, when the selected B-cell clones divided into plasma cells and memory cells.
*Marking:* • memory (B) cells (1) • formed during the first vaccination / by clonal expansion (1)

**5c.** Even when the antibodies have disappeared, the memory cells remain. On a new contact they trigger the fast secondary response, and a booster shot increases their number again, so the volunteer is protected before the virus can cause illness.
*Marking:* • memory cells persist after antibodies disappear (1) • booster renews/strengthens the rapid response (1)

**5d.** Artificial, active immunity. Artificial: it is given medically. Active: the volunteer's own B cells make the antibodies and memory cells.
*Marking:* • artificial with reason (1) • active with reason (1)

**6a.** MHC-II: yes. MHC-I: yes (a macrophage is a nucleated body cell and presents fragments of its own proteins on MHC-I). T helper on MHC-II: yes. Cytotoxic T on MHC-II: no (cytotoxic T cells read MHC-I). B cell activated by T-helper cytokines: yes. Macrophage makes antibodies: no (only B cells/plasma cells do).
*Marking:* • first two lines correct (MHC II yes, MHC I yes) (2) • T helper yes and cytotoxic T no, with MHC-I/MHC-II logic (2) • B cell yes (1) • macrophage no (1)

**6b.** MHC-II molecules present fragments of material that the cell has taken up from outside and digested in vesicles. The streptococcus was engulfed by phagocytosis, so its fragments are loaded onto MHC-II. The cell's own proteins, made in the cytoplasm, are presented on MHC-I instead.
*Marking:* • MHC-II = engulfed/extracellular material (1) • phagocytosed bacterium → MHC-II, own proteins → MHC-I (1)

**6c.** The cytotoxic T cell: it kills infected or abnormal body cells via their MHC-I and belongs to the cellular response; the humoral response against the bacterium runs via the T helper cell, the B cell and its plasma cells.
*Marking:* • cytotoxic T cell, with reference to cellular (MHC-I) versus humoral response (1)

**7a.** The other person must have the same one of the 16 possible combinations: probability = 1/16 = 0.0625 ≈ **6%**.
*Marking:* • use of 1/16 (1) • correct answer ≈ 6% (1)

**7b.** Humans have several MHC genes, and a full match requires matching at all of them; the small probabilities multiply, so the total becomes much smaller than 6% (for two such genes already 1/16 × 1/16 ≈ 0.4%).
*Marking:* • probabilities multiply over several genes, so much lower (1)

**7c.** Strategy 1: choose a donor whose ABO blood group is compatible, so the recipient's existing anti-A or anti-B antibodies do not attack the organ. Strategy 2: give immunosuppressive drugs for life, which dampen the recipient's immune response (at the cost of a higher vulnerability to infections).
*Marking:* • ABO blood-group compatibility between donor and recipient (1) • immunosuppressants (1)

**8a.** Antigens: only antigen A. Antibodies: only anti-B.
*Marking:* • antigen A (1) • anti-B (1)

**8b.** Only group O rhesus-negative is safe: group O red cells carry neither A nor B antigens, so the patient's anti-B finds nothing to bind. Group B cells carry the B antigen, which the patient's anti-B would bind: agglutination. Group AB cells carry B (and A) antigens, so anti-B would clump them too.
*Marking:* • O safe, no A/B antigens (1) • B dangerous because of anti-B–B binding (1) • AB dangerous likewise (1)

**8c.** O rhesus-negative cells carry no A or B antigens, and no D (rhesus) antigen either, so there is nothing for the patient's plasma antibodies to attack; a group A, rhesus-positive patient never has anti-D anyway. An Rh− recipient who receives Rh+ cells usually tolerates them the first time because anti-D appears only after contact with the D antigen (sensitization). After that contact the recipient's memory cells and anti-D would clump any later Rh+ transfusion, so it must not be repeated (and pregnancy makes the same mechanism dangerous).
*Marking:* • O cells lack A, B (1) • Rh− donor lacks D antigen / recipient A Rh+ has no anti-D (1) • first-time tolerance because sensitization needed (1) • after sensitization anti-D + memory → rejection on repeat (1)

**9a.** Wrong. Making antibodies takes days (the primary response has a lag phase), so eating one peanut for the first time cannot instantly produce antibodies or symptoms. Immediate symptoms of allergy are caused by histamine released from mast cells that already carry IgE from an earlier sensitization.
*Marking:* • primary response takes days, not instant (1) • immediate symptoms = histamine after earlier sensitization (1)

**9b.** Correct. The first contact only sensitizes: B cells make IgE against the allergen and the IgE binds to mast cells, without symptoms. Later contacts trigger the symptoms.
*Marking:* • sensitization without symptoms at first contact (1)

**9c.** Wrong. An allergy is a specific (acquired) response: IgE is made against one particular allergen. Mast cells and histamine are also used in non-specific inflammation, but the allergic reaction itself is directed at one antigen.
*Marking:* • allergy is a specific response (IgE against one allergen) (1) • histamine/mast cells shared with inflammation do not make the allergy non-specific (1)

**10a.** Mechanical defence, e.g. thorns (or the waxy cuticle, bark, spines, lignified walls). Chemical defence, e.g. lavender scents (or cyanide in clover, tannins, nicotine).
*Marking:* • mechanical with an example (1) • chemical with an example (1)

**10b.** Thorns are a mechanical (physical) defence: they deter larger grazing animals that would bite or browse the plant. Aphids are very small insects that walk between the thorns and pierce the phloem with their mouthparts, so thorns do not stop them.
*Marking:* • thorns = mechanical, effective against grazers (1) • aphids too small / piercing feeding, thorns ineffective (1)

**10c.** The lavender scent is present continuously (released from surface oil glands, so it acts before any damage). Clover cyanide is released only after cells are damaged, when the tissues are bitten or crushed.
*Marking:* • constitutive (always present) versus released on damage (1)

**10d.** Both systems recognize non-self: a plant receptor recognizes a specific molecule of the pathogen, much as human lymphocytes recognize specific antigens. The key difference is that plants have no circulating immune cells, no antibodies and no memory cells; each cell defends itself locally with barriers and defensive substances, whereas the human specific defence uses mobile lymphocytes, clonal expansion and immunological memory.
*Marking:* • similarity: specific recognition of a pathogen molecule (1) • plants: no mobile immune cells / no antibodies (1) • plants: no memory/clonal expansion, local cell defence only (1)

**10e.** Possible problem of frequent spraying: it selects for resistant aphids (the aphids that survive multiply, so the pesticide loses effect), and it can kill beneficial insects such as ladybirds that eat aphids. Advantage of the resistant variety: less or no spraying is needed, which saves costs, avoids residues on the flowers and slows the build-up of resistance.
*Marking:* • resistance selection in aphids (1) • harm to natural enemies or other downsides (1) • resistant variety reduces spraying and its problems (1)
