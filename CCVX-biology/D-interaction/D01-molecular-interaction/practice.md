### bio-d01-q01 | points 3 | outcome D1.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The gut bacterium strain Ku-12 lives on plant remains and can use the sugar lurulose, which is released in the gut when seaweed is eaten. Two structural genes lie under one promoter and one operator: *lurA*, which codes for a transport protein that takes lurulose into the cell, and *lurP*, which codes for an enzyme that splits lurulose into smaller sugars. A separate regulator gene with its own promoter continuously produces a repressor protein. With no lurulose, the repressor is bound to the operator.

Describe what happens at the DNA and at the repressor from the moment lurulose enters the bacterium, until the proteins needed to use lurulose are made.

#### Answer

Lurulose binds to the repressor protein, the repressor changes shape and lets go of the operator, so the operator is free.

#### Solution

Lurulose binds to the repressor protein, the repressor changes shape and lets go of the operator, so the operator is free. RNA polymerase can now bind the promoter and transcribe *lurA* and *lurP* together into one mRNA, which ribosomes translate into the transport protein and the splitting enzyme.
*Marking:* • lurulose binds repressor, repressor changes shape and releases the operator (1) • RNA polymerase binds the (now free) promoter/operator region and transcribes both structural genes into one mRNA (1) • translation gives the transport protein and the enzyme (1)

### bio-d01-q04 | points 2 | outcome D1.1.5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

### bio-d01-q16 | points 3 | outcome D1.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The long double-stranded RNA is cut inside the plant cell into short pieces of double-stranded RNA (iRNA/siRNA, about 20–25 nucleotides).

#### Solution

The long double-stranded RNA is cut inside the plant cell into short pieces of double-stranded RNA (iRNA/siRNA, about 20–25 nucleotides). *Marking:* • dsRNA cut into short iRNA pieces (1) • guide strand finds complementary (viral) mRNA in a protein complex (1) • mRNA cut/translation blocked → no coat protein (1)

#### Question

Researchers suspect that the gene *TF-D* of a dune grass codes for a transcription factor that switches on salt-tolerance proteins. They make two recombinant-DNA constructs in cultured grass cells. Construct 1: the promoter of a salt-tolerance gene fused to a reporter gene whose protein fluoresces green. Construct 2: a construct that makes the cells produce the TF-D protein. They measure the fluorescence (arbitrary units):

| cells | no salt added | salt added |
|---|---|---|
| with construct 1 only | 40 | 40 |
| with constructs 1 and 2 | 250 | 980 |

Explain why construct 1 contains the promoter of the salt-tolerance gene but not the structural gene of the salt-tolerance protein itself.

#### Answer

The researchers want to measure whether TF-D switches on the *promoter* of the salt-tolerance gene, not the product of that gene.

#### Solution

The researchers want to measure whether TF-D switches on the *promoter* of the salt-tolerance gene, not the product of that gene. Fusing this promoter to a reporter gene (for a fluorescent protein) puts an easily measurable protein under the control of that promoter; the real structural gene would give a protein that is hard to measure and would mask the effect.
*Marking:* • the experiment tests whether the promoter is switched on (1) • reporter/fluorescent protein makes the promoter's activity measurable (1)

### bio-d01-q26 | points 3 | outcome D1.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Researchers suspect that the gene *TF-D* of a dune grass codes for a transcription factor that switches on salt-tolerance proteins. They make two recombinant-DNA constructs in cultured grass cells. Construct 1: the promoter of a salt-tolerance gene fused to a reporter gene whose protein fluoresces green. Construct 2: a construct that makes the cells produce the TF-D protein. They measure the fluorescence (arbitrary units):

| cells | no salt added | salt added |
|---|---|---|
| with construct 1 only | 40 | 40 |
| with constructs 1 and 2 | 250 | 980 |

Show with two calculations what the table reveals about the action of TF-D and about the influence of salt (an abiotic factor).

#### Answer

TF-D switches the promoter on: 250 / 40 = 6.25 times more fluorescence than without TF-D.

#### Solution

TF-D switches the promoter on: 250 / 40 = 6.25 times more fluorescence than without TF-D. Salt strongly increases the effect: 980 / 250 ≈ 3.9 times more with salt than without salt when TF-D is present (overall 980/40 = 24.5). So TF-D is a transcription factor that switches on the salt-tolerance promoter, and the abiotic factor salt boosts its action (TF-D is made or activated more under salt).
*Marking:* • 250/40 = 6.25 → TF-D activates the promoter (1) • 980/250 ≈ 3.9 (or 980/40 = 24.5) → salt increases the effect (1) • interpretation: transcription factor whose activity the abiotic factor salt raises (1)

#### Question

Researchers suspect that the gene *TF-D* of a dune grass codes for a transcription factor that switches on salt-tolerance proteins. They make two recombinant-DNA constructs in cultured grass cells. Construct 1: the promoter of a salt-tolerance gene fused to a reporter gene whose protein fluoresces green. Construct 2: a construct that makes the cells produce the TF-D protein. They measure the fluorescence (arbitrary units):

| cells | no salt added | salt added |
|---|---|---|
| with construct 1 only | 40 | 40 |
| with constructs 1 and 2 | 250 | 980 |

The constructs were built by fusing pieces of DNA of different origin. Name the two enzymes needed to cut and to join the DNA pieces, and name one way to bring the constructs into the grass cells.

#### Answer

Cut with a restriction enzyme; join with DNA ligase.

#### Solution

Cut with a restriction enzyme; join with DNA ligase. Bring into the plant cells for example with the bacterium *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* (or another vector such as a plasmid or particle gun).
*Marking:* • restriction enzyme **and** DNA ligase (1) • suitable transfer method/vector, e.g. *Agrobacterium* (1)

### bio-d01-q28 | points 2 | outcome D1.1.5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infects cells of the cervix. Two proteins made by the virus bind to and inactivate two human tumour suppressor proteins: p53, which can halt the cell cycle and start apoptosis, and Rb, which restrains progression through the cell cycle. A persistent HPV infection can lead to cervical cancer.

Explain how HPV, as a biotic factor, can change the working of the host cell's genes without changing the base sequence of the host's DNA.

#### Answer

HPV changes the *activity* of host proteins, not the DNA base sequence: its proteins bind p53 and Rb and switch them off.

#### Solution

HPV changes the *activity* of host proteins, not the DNA base sequence: its proteins bind p53 and Rb and switch them off. The host genes for p53 and Rb are still intact and transcribed, but their products no longer work. So a biotic factor (a virus) changes which proteins are active in the cell — a change in the cell's functioning without any mutation of the host genome.
*Marking:* • viral proteins inactivate host proteins (p53, Rb) at protein level (1) • host DNA sequence unchanged / genes still present, only their products switched off (1)

### bio-d01-q29 | points 3 | outcome D1.1.5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infects cells of the cervix. Two proteins made by the virus bind to and inactivate two human tumour suppressor proteins: p53, which can halt the cell cycle and start apoptosis, and Rb, which restrains progression through the cell cycle. A persistent HPV infection can lead to cervical cancer.

Explain why inactivating p53 and Rb makes uncontrolled cell division more likely, and why this counts as a disturbance of gene regulation (of the cell-division regulators).

#### Answer

p53 and Rb are products of tumour suppressor genes: they form brakes on the cell cycle (halting division and, via p53, starting apoptosis when damage is too great).

#### Solution

p53 and Rb are products of tumour suppressor genes: they form brakes on the cell cycle (halting division and, via p53, starting apoptosis when damage is too great). If both brakes are inactivated, division is no longer restrained and cells can keep dividing uncontrollably. Because these brakes normally regulate when and how often the cell-division genes are used, switching them off disturbs the regulation of cell division.
*Marking:* • p53/Rb are tumour-suppressor (brake) proteins on the cell cycle (1) • without brakes → unrestrained/uncontrolled division (1) • this is a disturbance of gene regulation of cell division (1)

### bio-d01-q30 | points 2 | outcome D1.1.5 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infects cells of the cervix. Two proteins made by the virus bind to and inactivate two human tumour suppressor proteins: p53, which can halt the cell cycle and start apoptosis, and Rb, which restrains progression through the cell cycle. A persistent HPV infection can lead to cervical cancer.

Explain why vaccination against HPV lowers the incidence of cervical cancer.

#### Answer

Vaccination prevents infection with the cancer-linked HPV types, so the viral proteins that inactivate p53 and Rb never appear in the cervical cells.

#### Solution

Vaccination prevents infection with the cancer-linked HPV types, so the viral proteins that inactivate p53 and Rb never appear in the cervical cells. Without that disturbance of the tumour suppressors, the chain of events that can lead to cervical cancer does not start, so the incidence of this cancer falls.
*Marking:* • vaccination prevents (persistent) HPV infection (1) • no viral proteins → tumour suppressors keep working → fewer cancers (1)
