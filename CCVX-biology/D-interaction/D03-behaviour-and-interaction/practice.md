### bio-d03-q08 | points 3 | outcome D3.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A choice chamber is half covered with wet soil (humid) and half with dry soil. Ten woodlice are released in the middle. Every two minutes the number of woodlice in the humid half is counted:

| Time (min) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| In humid half | 5 | 6 | 8 | 9 | 9 | 10 |

A student concludes: "The woodlice know where the humid side is and walk straight to it." Assess this claim using their behaviour in dry air: woodlice walk faster and turn more often in dry conditions and slow down in humid conditions.

#### Answer

The claim is wrong: woodlice do not orient towards humidity; in dry air their activity rises (kinesis), so they leave dry places, while on the humid side they slow down and stay.

#### Solution

The claim is wrong. Woodlice show kinesis, an undirected change in activity, not a directed taxis: in the dry half they walk faster and turn more, so they often re-enter the middle and the humid half, where they slow down and turn less and therefore stay. Chance and differential speed accumulate them on the humid side without any knowledge of direction; the table shows this gradual build-up (5 → 10 over 10 min), not a straight walk.
*Marking:* • claim assessed as incorrect (1) • kinesis: higher activity/turning in dry air, lower in humid, undirected (1) • gradual accumulation matches the data, no goal-directed orientation (1)

### bio-d03-q15 | points 3 | outcome D3.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A dog trainer teaches a beagle to "stay". Each session she gives a food reward every time the dog stays for 10 s. The number of correct stays per 10-minute session is recorded:

| Session | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Correct stays | 2 | 5 | 9 | 12 | 14 | 15 | 15 |

In sessions 8–10 the trainer stops giving rewards; the counts fall to 6, 2 and 0.

Name the type of learning in sessions 1–7 and the process shown in sessions 8–10, and explain why the behaviour first rises and then falls.

#### Answer

Sessions 1–7: operant conditioning (trial-and-error learning) — the behaviour is linked to its consequence, a reward, so its frequency rises.

#### Solution

Sessions 1–7: operant conditioning (trial-and-error learning) — the behaviour "stay" is repeatedly followed by a food reward, so the dog performs it more and more often (2 → 15). Sessions 8–10: extinction — without the reward the link is no longer reinforced, so the response declines (15 → 0).
*Marking:* • operant conditioning named (1) • reward links behaviour to consequence → frequency rises (1) • extinction without reward → frequency falls (1)

### bio-d03-q20 | points 2 | outcome D3.1.6 | difficulty medium

#### Question

This is a displacement activity (overspronggedrag): an out-of-place action from another behaviour system. #### Question

In a driving simulator, sober drivers brake on average 0.80 s after a child runs into the road; after two glasses of alcohol this rises to 1.30 s. The car drives at 25 m/s (90 km/h), still a sober reaction time.

Calculate the extra distance the car travels during the longer reaction time, and argue what result of behavioural research like this justifies for society (think of legal limits and campaigns).

#### Answer

Extra reaction time: 1.30 − 0.80 = 0.50 s. Distance = speed × time = 25 × 0.50 = **12.5 m** extra travelled before braking starts.

#### Solution

Extra reaction time: 1.30 − 0.80 = 0.50 s. Distance = speed × time = 25 × 0.50 = **12.5 m** extra travelled before braking starts. Research shows alcohol slows reaction and increases risk-taking, so society is justified in setting legal blood-alcohol limits (e.g. 0.5‰, stricter for novice drivers) and in running public campaigns against drinking and driving.
*Marking:* • extra time 0.50 s and distance 12.5 m computed (1) • implication: legal limits/campaigns justified by measured risk increase (1)

#### Question

Harlow's young rhesus monkeys spent most of their time on a soft cloth "mother" that gave no milk, rather than on a wire "mother" that gave food; monkeys raised without real social contact later showed disturbed behaviour.

Argue one implication of these results for human society, for example in hospitals or child care.

#### Answer

Because young primates need comfort and social contact, not just food, hospitals changed rules so parents can stay with their young children and rooms-in (rooming-in); institutions likewise avoid social isolation of infants.

#### Solution

Because young primates need comfort and social contact, not just food (they chose the cloth mother over the wire one), hospitals changed rules so parents can stay with their young children and rooms-in; child-care practice also stresses stable attachment figures. Behavioural research here directly altered policy, an implication for society.
*Marking:* • result correctly used: need for contact/comfort beyond feeding (1) • concrete societal implication named (hospital visiting/rooming-in, day-care attachment) (1)
