This is our own walkthrough of that official exam: for every question, what it asks (in our words), a step-by-step solution, the subdomain in our folder structure, the BINAS tables to use, common mistakes and how the points work. Nothing is copied from the paper: no question text, no figures, no data tables, no marking scheme wording. The exam is in Dutch; Dutch terms are glossed in English. Open the official PDF next to this file.

Exam facts worth knowing first: 3 hours (Tuesday 14 May 2024, 13:30–16:30); maximum **68 points** over **42 questions** (10 multiple-choice, 32 open). Cover rule that bites: if an open question asks for an explanation or calculation, the bare answer scores 0; and give no more reasons than asked — only the first ones count. Many open questions are "write down numbers 1–3 and mark each statement juist/onjuist (true/false)": those are marked 3 correct = 2 points, 2 correct = 1, fewer = 0, so one wrong statement already costs a point. Six contexts: nicotine in early embryonic development, the Svalbard reindeer changing its diet, great tits possibly poisoned by moth control, liquorice and blood pressure, favism ("bonenziekte", a G6PD enzyme defect), and PFAS in vegetable gardens. One official aanvulling (supplement) was published after marking began: it softens question 32 — read its entry below. BINAS or ScienceData is allowed; only one question (22) explicitly sends you to the information book.

Subdomain ids refer to the folders in this package (e.g. `bio-b02` = B02 Metabolism in Cells; `bio-a05` = A05 Research; `0-basic-01` = basic chemistry).

## Question 1 (official paper)

Three statements about a synapse diagram: nicotine binds acetylcholine receptors on the axon endings of dopamine-producing neurons, and both acetylcholine and nicotine raise dopamine release; dopamine is taken back up into the presynaptic neuron.

**Solve:** Judge each claim. (1) "Nicotine is an antagonist of acetylcholine" — FALSE: an antagonist blocks; here nicotine binding *increases* dopamine release just like acetylcholine, so nicotine acts as an agonist/mimic. (2) "Dopamine enters the synaptic cleft by endocytose (endocytosis)" — FALSE: transmitters are released by *exo*cytosis; endocytosis is uptake, the opposite direction. (3) "Reuptake of dopamine happens via dopamine receptors" — FALSE: reuptake runs via transport/carrier proteins in the presynaptic membrane; receptors sit (mostly postsynaptically) to receive the signal, not to pump it back. All three false. → `bio-d01` (ligand–receptor interaction) + `bio-d02` (synapse as cellular communication). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** classifying nicotine as antagonist because it's a poison (function is decided by effect at the receptor, not by toxicity); mixing up exo-/endocytosis.
**Points:** 2 (3 statements right = 2, 2 right = 1, fewer = 0).

## Question 2 (official paper)

Explain why nicotine activates the reward centre *more* than acetylcholine does. Given in the paper: nicotine is not broken down by acetylcholinesterase.

**Solve:** Build the causal chain in three links. (1) Acetylcholinesterase in the tissue fluid clears acetylcholine but not nicotine, so nicotine stays bound to the acetylcholine receptors longer / rebinds more often, producing more action potentials in the presynaptic axon. (2) More presynaptic firing → more (or longer) dopamine release into the synaptic cleft. (3) More dopamine on the postsynaptic side → more impulses in the postsynaptic neuron → the reward centre of the cerebrum is more strongly activated. Each link earns a point. Note: arguing from the acetylcholine side (ACh is broken down, so its effect stops sooner) earns the same points by analogy — the scheme allows this. → `bio-d02`, `bio-b04` (self-regulation/reward system). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** claiming nicotine *binds more strongly* — the marking scheme explicitly refuses that for the first point; stopping the chain at "more dopamine" without the postsynaptic consequence.
**Points:** 3 (one per link: longer receptor occupancy, more dopamine release, more postsynaptic impulses).

## Question 3 (official paper)

What is the function for the plant of making nicotine (a toxic alkaloid produced by the nightshade family, most of all by *Nicotiana*)?

**Solve:** One point, one idea: chemical defence — nicotine deters or poisons herbivores, protecting the plant against being eaten (*vraat*). The scheme also accepts the opposite-role answer (attracting animals for pollination/seed dispersal), and forgivingly accepts "predation" where "herbivory" is meant. → `bio-d05` (interaction in ecosystems: plant–herbivore). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** answering what nicotine does *in humans* (addiction) instead of what it does *for the plant*.
**Points:** 1 (defence against grazing, or the accepted alternative).

## Question 4 (official paper)

Tobacco production varieties have higher nicotine contents than wild tobacco plants, achieved through repeated crossing and selection. Name this process.

**Solve:** The process is (classical) breeding — Dutch *veredeling* / *veredelen*: crossing varieties and selecting the offspring with the desired trait over generations. Careful: the scheme explicitly refuses "kunstmatige selectie" (artificial selection) as the answer, even though breeding *is* artificial selection biologically — they want the domain word. → `bio-f01` (selection/breeding), `bio-e03`-adjacent. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** writing "artificial selection" (refused) or "evolution" (too broad, and it's directed by humans).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 5 (official paper, mc)

The researchers started from stem cells of a 5-day-old human embryo (blastula) and let them develop into embryoid bodies for 21 days. Which potency did the cells have at the start, and which after 21 days?

**Solve:** Blastula stem cells (embryonic stem cells) are **pluripotent**: they can form any body cell type but not a complete organism (no extra-embryonic tissues). After 21 days of development the cells have differentiated into specific cell types — their potential has narrowed to **multipotent**. Answer: the option pluripotent → multipotent (C). Totipotent (zygote/first divisions) is never right here. → `bio-e03` (development), `bio-c02` (self-organization of organisms). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking totipotent for the blastula stage (that window has passed by the blastocyst); thinking differentiated cells stay pluripotent "because they're stem-cell-derived".
**Points:** 2.

## Question 6 (official paper, mc)

Dot plot of embryoid-body diameters after 21 days: control group versus nicotine-exposed group, one dot per EB, with a star marking a *significant* difference in mean diameter. Two statements: does the data support them?

**Solve:** (1) "The number of cells in a control EB is on average larger than in a nicotine-exposed EB" — SUPPORTED: the paper states diameter is a measure of cell number, and the control distribution sits higher on average. (2) "The difference in mean diameter rests on chance" — NOT supported: the figure marks the difference as significant, i.e. too large to be attributed to chance. Answer: only 1 (B). → `bio-a05` (research: reading significance) + `bio-e03`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** seeing the overlap of the two clouds of dots and calling it chance anyway — the significance flag overrules eyeballing overlap; mixing up diameter with cell size instead of cell number.
**Points:** 2.

## Question 7 (official paper)

Three statements about the workflow to profile gene expression: isolate single cells, make cDNA from all RNA, amplify by PCR, build an expression profile per cell type.

**Solve:** (1) "Making cDNA requires other enzymes than PCR" — TRUE: cDNA synthesis needs reverse transcriptase (RNA → DNA); PCR needs a (thermostable) DNA polymerase. (2) "Making cDNA requires other nucleotides than PCR" — FALSE: both build DNA strands from the same four deoxynucleotides (dATP, dTTP, dGTP, dCTP). (3) "In this PCR, in total one characteristic gene is amplified" — FALSE: cDNA was made from *all* RNA, so many genes are amplified; the characteristic genes are found afterwards from the expression profile. → `bio-e01` (DNA synthesis/PCR) + `bio-b01` (gene expression). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking reverse transcription uses RNA nucleotides (the product is DNA, so DNA building blocks); missing the word "all RNA" in the description.
**Points:** 2 (3 right = 2, 2 right = 1).

## Question 8 (official paper)

A table lists two cell types whose gene expression changed under nicotine: (1) epithelial cells — increased cell division; (2) endothelial cells — reduced formation of capillaries. Explain for each, via a factor from Fick's law of diffusion, how this can cause later-life gas-exchange problems in the lungs.

**Solve:** Fick's law: diffusion rate scales with surface area A and concentration gradient Δc, and *inversely* with diffusion distance Δx. For (1): extra division of (airway/alveolar) epithelium thickens the wall between air and blood → larger Δx, so slower O₂/CO₂ diffusion (accepted variant: narrower airways → poorer ventilation → smaller Δc). For (2): fewer capillaries → smaller exchange surface A between alveoli and blood (or loss of close alveolus–capillary contact, again a larger effective distance) → slower gas exchange. → `bio-b03` (gas exchange in organisms), `bio-a11` (form and function). BINAS: the transport-phenomena table with Fick's diffusion law (physics section).
**Common mistakes:** naming the right factor without tying it to the anatomy (e.g. "A decreases" without saying *why* nicotine's effect shrinks it); swapping the two cell types' stories.
**Points:** 2 (one per cell type with a correct Fick factor).

## Question 9 (official paper)

Embryoid bodies are an imperfect model for nicotine effects on a real pregnancy. The paper gives one shortcoming (an EB is not a real embryo, so later pregnancy can't be studied). Name another.

**Solve:** Examples that score: the culture cannot include the mother's lifestyle or other environmental factors; conditions in the womb (placenta, maternal circulation) are not mimicked; nicotine was added only from day 5 of the culture, whereas real exposure starts earlier/continuously. Explicitly NOT accepted: "an EB is not a complete embryo/organ" (that's the given example), and ethical or financial objections. → `bio-a05` (research design), `bio-a09` (evaluation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** repeating the given shortcoming in other words; drifting into ethics ("animal testing is better") instead of validity.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 10 (official paper)

Svalbard reindeer have short legs. Explain how shorter legs change the surface-area-to-volume ratio, and how that lowers their energy use.

**Solve:** Two links, one point each. (1) Shorter legs give the body a more compact shape: the surface area relative to the volume becomes smaller (a smaller *oppervlakte-inhoudsverhouding*). (2) Heat is lost across the body surface, so a smaller relative surface means less heat loss to the arctic cold — less energy needs to be burned for thermoregulation. → `bio-b04` (thermoregulation), `bio-a11` (form and function). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying short legs give a *smaller volume* (volume is set by body mass; the point is surface vs. volume); forgetting the second link — the ratio itself isn't the energy saving, heat loss is.
**Points:** 2 (ratio effect, heat-loss effect).

## Question 11 (official paper)

The Svalbard reindeer is the smallest subspecies; there are no wolves on Spitsbergen, so small size is not punished by predation. Explain how such a smaller subspecies could arise there.

**Solve:** Classic three-step selection story, one point per step. (1) Genetic variation in body size existed (through mutation) within the reindeer population. (2) With predation absent, smaller individuals had the advantage: they need less food/energy to grow and maintain themselves (scarce tundra resources) and/or mature earlier — the scheme also accepts "find shelter from cold more easily". (3) Therefore smaller individuals had higher fitness: more surviving offspring, so the alleles for small size spread and a small subspecies formed. → `bio-f01` (selection), `bio-f02` (speciation/subspecies). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** Lamarckist phrasing ("the cold made them smaller") instead of variation + selection; using the *absence* of wolves as the selecting advantage itself — it only removes a disadvantage of being small, the advantage must come from food/energy/reproduction.
**Points:** 3 (variation; advantage of small size; higher fitness/more offspring).

## Question 12 (official paper)

Arctic warming raises vegetation productivity. Three listed effects; state for each whether net primary production (NPP) will increase because of it.

**Solve:** NPP = gross photosynthetic production − plant respiration. (1) "Dissimilation (respiration) by plant cells increases" — NPP does NOT increase: more respiration burns more of the gross production. (2) "The active period of decomposers (*reducenten*) in the soil lengthens" — NPP DOES increase: more mineralisation releases more inorganic nutrients for plants. (3) "Nitrogen assimilation proceeds faster" — NPP DOES increase: faster uptake of nitrogen lets plants build more. So: 1 not, 2 yes, 3 yes. → `bio-b08` (production/regulation in ecosystems), `bio-b02`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking warming simply speeds everything up equally (the question separates gross production from respiration losses); equating decomposer activity with nutrient *loss* (it's nutrient release).
**Points:** 2 (3 right = 2, 2 right = 1).

## Question 13 (official paper)

Higher plant productivity lets some herbivore populations grow. Explain why other herbivore species' populations can shrink instead.

**Solve:** One clean ecological mechanism is enough: warming changes the plant species composition, so the specific food plant another herbivore depends on may disappear; or the herbivores that profit from the extra productivity outcompete the others (interspecific competition), leaving them with less food. → `bio-d05` (interaction in ecosystems), `bio-a12` (ecological thinking). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** answering at the wrong level ("the climate worsens") — productivity is rising, so you need a *competitive or compositional* mechanism; predator argumentation not given in the context.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 14 (official paper)

Scatter plot: per population per year, the thickness of the rain-on-snow ice layer versus the share of reindeer foraging at the coast (three populations, dotted over nine years). Judge three statements.

**Solve:** Read relations, not single dots. (1) "Only when an ice layer is present do reindeer forage at the coast" — FALSE: at (near-)zero ice thickness there are already dots above zero coastal foraging, so coast use happens without ice. (2) "In population 1 the coastal share grows with thicker ice" — TRUE: that population's dots trend upward from left to right. (3) "With more than 9 cm of ice, 31% of population 2 forages at the coast" — FALSE: one year's dot sits high, but another thick-ice year for the same population sits near zero — one dot is not a rule. → `bio-a05` (graph interpretation), `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reading a single extreme dot as a fixed percentage; ignoring the zero-ice points that kill the word "only" in statement 1.
**Points:** 2 (3 right = 2, 2 right = 1).

## Question 15 (official paper)

Faeces research proved the reindeer now also eat washed-up seaweed when ice locks the land plants away. Explain how the changed diet affects the nitrogen cycle (*stikstofkringloop*) of the tundra.

**Solve:** Seaweed nitrogen comes from the sea. The reindeer eat it on the tundra and return it in faeces and urine — so (more) organic nitrogen is transported from sea to land and enters the tundra's nitrogen cycle, where decomposers can mineralise it for plants. → `bio-b08` (nitrogen cycle). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** vague "nitrogen increases" without the sea→land transport direction; saying the seaweed *fixates* nitrogen.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 16 (official paper)

Pyrethroid insecticides prevent voltage-gated Na⁺ channels in neuron processes from closing. Judge three statements about the neuron.

**Solve:** (1) "Pyrethroids cause constant new impulses in the axons" — FALSE: with Na⁺ channels stuck open the membrane stays depolarised; no repolarisation means no *new* action potentials can be fired — signal transmission stalls rather than storms. (2) "Because the Na⁺ channels don't close, hyperpolarisation occurs" — FALSE: open Na⁺ channels keep Na⁺ flowing in, i.e. sustained *depolarisation*; hyperpolarisation is the more-negative-than-rest state after extra K⁺ outflow. (3) "The original resting potential is no longer reached" — TRUE: the cell can't reset to its resting value. → `bio-b04` (action potentials), `bio-d01` (membrane channel proteins). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** equating "stimulant poison" with "constant firing" — a blocked repolarisation silences the axon; confusing depolarisation with hyperpolarisation.
**Points:** 2 (3 right = 2, 2 right = 1).

## Question 17 (official paper)

DDT — banned for over 50 years — still showed up in dead great tit chicks. Give the term for such a poorly degradable poison.

**Solve:** The term is a **persistent** poison / persistent substance (Dutch: *persistent*). → `bio-b08` (substances in ecosystems), `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** writing "biodegradable" variants or "toxic/cumulative" alone — the asked word is *persistent* ("accumulating" describes the build-up, not the degradability).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 18 (official paper)

Explain how a herbicide (a *plant* poison) can end up inside young great tits, which are fed insects.

**Solve:** Via the food chain: the herbicide sits on or in sprayed plants → insects/caterpillars eat the plants → the parent tits feed those insects to the chicks, and the poison travels along (accumulating up the chain). The scheme also accepts "via plant material used as nest lining", but NOT "the chicks eat plant food" — nestlings get insects. → `bio-d05` (food chain), `bio-a12`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** answering "through plant-based food of the chicks" (refused); stopping at "plants absorb it" without the insect link.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 19 (official paper)

Poisons are taken up far more easily through the gut than through the skin. Give one property at organ level and one at cell level that explains this.

**Solve:** Organ level (one of): the gut has a much larger absorption surface (folds and intestinal villi), no protective horny layer like the skin, better blood supply, higher temperature. Cell level (one of): gut epithelium bears microvilli (surface amplification per cell), is packed with membrane transport proteins, and can run active transport. → `bio-a11` (form and function) + `bio-b03` (uptake in organisms). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** naming an organ-level feature twice ("villi" and "large surface" are the same argument); naming skin-side features instead of gut-side.
**Points:** 2 (one organ-level + one cell-level property).

## Question 20 (official paper)

A newspaper headlined that great tit chicks "die massively from flea and tick poison". Argue why the researchers would never have presented that as their conclusion.

**Solve:** Any one solid methodological objection scores: the poisons' presence says nothing about the *cause* of death (correlation, and concentrations were not even measured); other substances were found in more nests than the flea/tick products; only nests with dead chicks were examined — no comparison with healthy nests; the sample is small and the total number of nests in the areas is unknown, so "massively" is unsupported. Not accepted: appeals like "scientists don't write that". → `bio-a09` (evaluation and judgment), `bio-a05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** moralising instead of arguing ("the headline is sensational"); claiming the poison was absent — it wasn't, it just wasn't proven causal.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 21 (official paper)

Liquorice's glycyrrhizine (GL) is hydrolysed by gut bacteria to glycyrrhetinic acid (GA), which is absorbed; the liver converts part of it for bile excretion, and part of that is converted back to GA in the gut and reabsorbed — the enterohepatic (gut–liver) cycle. Judge three statements.

**Solve:** (1) "GA reaches the liver only via the portal vein (*poortader*)" — FALSE: GA is absorbed into the blood generally, and once in the circulation it also reaches the liver through the hepatic artery; "only" fails. (2) "Because of the enterohepatic circulation it takes longer before GA is fully removed" — TRUE: excreted-with-bile GA is partly reabsorbed in the gut, a recycling loop that delays elimination. (3) "A slow passage of the chyme through the gut lowers the amount of GA in the body" — FALSE: slow passage gives bacteria *more* time to hydrolyse GL and the body more time to absorb GA, so the amount rises, not falls. → `bio-b03` (digestion/liver), `bio-a14` (systems thinking). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking the portal vein is the *only* blood route into the liver (forgetting the hepatic artery/systemic recirculation); reasoning "slower passage = less uptake" — it's the reverse.
**Points:** 2 (3 right = 2, 2 right = 1).

## Question 22 (official paper, mc)

Cortisol activates the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR); the complex moves to the nucleus and binds a hormone-response element (HRE) on the DNA, after which genes for Na⁺ channels are expressed. "The activated MR is involved as a transcription factor" — which function does it have *in transcription*? Use the information book.

**Solve:** The MR–HRE complex switches transcription ON — it *activates* it. That makes the MR an **activator**: a transcription factor that stimulates transcription of specific genes (option A). Enhancer is a DNA sequence, not a protein; a promotor is the RNA-polymerase binding site; a repressor would shut transcription down. → `bio-b01` (regulation of gene expression). BINAS: the table on transcription factors / regulation of gene expression (activator, enhancer, promotor, repressor terms).
**Common mistakes:** choosing "enhancer" because it appears in the same table row of terms — an enhancer is DNA, the question asks the protein's function; choosing repressor because GA is an inhibitor (MR activation is what's asked, not GA's role).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 23 (official paper)

The uptake of GA increases the osmotic value of the blood. Explain this with the cell diagram (GA blocks the enzyme 11β-HSD2, which normally deactivates cortisol to cortisone; cortisone cannot bind MR).

**Solve:** Two links, one point each. (1) GA inhibits 11β-HSD2, so less cortisol is converted to cortisone; more cortisol remains to activate MR → the activated-MR pathway produces *more Na⁺ channels* in the tubule cell membranes. (2) More Na⁺ channels → more Na⁺ reabsorption from the pre-urine (*voorurine*) back into the blood → more dissolved particles in the blood → the osmotic value of the blood rises. → `bio-b03` (kidney: reabsorption), `bio-b01` (gene expression downstream). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** explaining water retention but never mentioning Na⁺ reabsorption (the middle step is what's scored); claiming GA itself adds osmotic particles.
**Points:** 2 (more Na⁺ channels via blocked enzyme; more Na⁺ reabsorption from pre-urine).

## Question 24 (official paper)

Explain how a high blood pressure can cause oedema (fluid swelling in tissues).

**Solve:** Higher pressure inside the capillaries pushes more fluid out of the blood into the tissue (stronger ultrafiltration and/or weaker back-reabsorption), so tissue fluid accumulates: oedema. → `bio-b03` (blood pressure, tissue fluid). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reversing the direction (absorbing *into* vessels); attributing it to kidney failure — the question wants the capillary pressure argument.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 25 (official paper)

Calculation: the "no effect level" for GA is 2.0 mg per kg body weight per day; people with high blood pressure must divide that by 10. Sweet liquorice contains 1259 mg GA per kg; one liquorice sweet weighs 5 g. Anna weighs 70 kg and has high blood pressure. How many sweet liquorice sweets may she eat per day at most?

**Solve:** Apply the safety margin first: 2.0/10 = 0.2 mg/kg/day → for 70 kg that is 14 mg GA per day. Per sweet: 1259 mg/kg × 0.005 kg = 6.295 mg GA. Then 14 / 6.295 ≈ 2.2, so at most **2 sweets** (round down: 2.2 sweets cannot be eaten, and the third would exceed the limit). Marking: one point for a calculation correctly using all four given values (2.0; 1259; 5; 70), one point for applying the ÷10 margin. → `bio-b03`, `0-basic-01` (dose/concentration arithmetic). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** forgetting the ÷10 (gives 22 sweets); using 1259 mg as per-gram or per-sweet; rounding 2.2 up to 3.
**Points:** 2 (values used correctly; safety margin applied).

## Question 26 (official paper, mc)

In the pentose-phosphate pathway (PPP) diagram, NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH in two steps; NADPH is then used to regenerate glutathion and to build fatty acids. Does NADPH give off or take up energy-rich electrons, and in which type of metabolism is it consumed?

**Solve:** NADPH is the *loaded* carrier: it **gives off** energy-rich electrons (it is oxidised back to NADP⁺). It is consumed in **assimilation** — the build-up of organic substances (glutathion regeneration, fatty-acid synthesis), exactly its role in photosynthesis' Calvin cycle. Answer: give off + assimilation (A). → `bio-b02` (carriers in cell metabolism). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reflex "NADPH comes from breakdown, so dissimilation" — NADPH is *spent* in building processes; mixing NADH (dissimilation) with NADPH.
**Points:** 2.

## Question 27 (official paper, mc)

G6PD catalyses the first step of the PPP; the PPP's NADPH is needed for fatty-acid production. What happens to fatty-acid production when G6PD is non-functional?

**Solve:** Follow the chain in the figure: no G6PD → PPP stalls at its first reaction → far less NADPH is produced → the cell lacks the reducing power for fatty-acid synthesis → production **decreases** (B). → `bio-b02`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** "no enzyme = no change, other pathways compensate" (the figure shows no bypass); thinking fatty-acid *breakdown* is asked.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 28 (official paper)

In Fabio's other cells, extra G6PD is produced when G6PD activity runs short. Explain why this cannot happen in his red blood cells.

**Solve:** Mature red blood cells have **no nucleus (no DNA) and no ribosomes**, so no transcription/translation — no protein synthesis at all, hence no extra G6PD. → `bio-b01` (protein synthesis) + `bio-c01` (cell specialisation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying "red blood cells have no mitochondria" (true but irrelevant — G6PD is made in the cytoplasm, the missing nucleus/ribosomes are the point).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 29 (official paper)

In favism, red blood cells haemolyse after the sufferer eats broad beans. Explain how haemolysis leads to yellow skin and yellowing of the white of the eye.

**Solve:** Two steps, one point each. (1) Haemoglobin is released from the burst red blood cells and broken down. (2) The breakdown pigment **bilirubin** (bile pigment — the scheme does not penalise saying "galkleurstof") accumulates in the blood faster than the liver can excrete it and deposits in skin and eye white → the yellow colour of jaundice. → `bio-b03` (excretion/liver). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying the yellow *is* haemoglobin itself (the yellow comes from its breakdown product); forgetting to mention accumulation in the tissues.
**Points:** 2 (haemoglobin broken down; bilirubin accumulates in skin/eye white).

## Question 30 (official paper, mc)

After Fabio eats broad beans, haemolysis causes anaemia. Will his breathing frequency and heart rate be raised or lowered?

**Solve:** Fewer red blood cells → lower oxygen transport capacity → the body compensates to keep O₂ supply up: *both* breathing frequency and heart rate are **raised** (option A). → `bio-b03` (O₂ transport) + `bio-b04` (regulation of breathing/circulation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking less "work" for the heart because less blood (the amount of blood hasn't changed — its O₂ capacity has).
**Points:** 2.

## Question 31 (official paper)

G6PD mutations are mostly point mutations and small deletions. Judge three statements about mutations.

**Solve:** (1) "A deletion of one base pair in an exon changes the reading frame" — TRUE: all codons downstream shift (frameshift). (2) "Every point mutation in an exon affects the activity of the encoded protein" — FALSE: silent substitutions (same amino acid, degenerate code) or conservative changes can leave the protein's function intact. (3) "A point mutation in an exon can result in the absence of the protein" — TRUE: e.g. a premature stop codon (or destroyed start/splice signal) can leave no functional protein at all. → `bio-b01` + `bio-e01` (mutations). BINAS: the genetic-code table.
**Common mistakes:** calling "every point mutation" harmful (ignoring silent ones); thinking a one-base deletion only changes one amino acid.
**Points:** 2 (3 right = 2, 2 right = 1).

## Question 32 (official paper — see the aanvulling!)

Pedigree, G6PD gene on the X chromosome. Grandmother Francesca is a carrier (heterozygous). Her children are lettered; among them Q is an unaffected son, R an affected son, P and S unaffected daughters. "From the phenotype of one of Francesca's children it also follows that she cannot be homozygous for the mutated allele." Give the letter + explanation.

**Solve:** The intended answer is **Q**: Q is an unaffected son, and a son's only X chromosome comes from his mother — had Francesca been homozygous for the mutated allele, every son would have received it and would be affected; Q is healthy, so Francesca must carry a healthy allele. Marks: Q (1) + the "his X can only come from his mother" reasoning (1). **Aanvulling surprise:** the official supplement awards **1 point in total** if a candidate answers **R** with reasoning showing that R could only have obtained the (mutated) allele from his mother (so she cannot be homozygous for the *healthy* allele). Note the asymmetry: the full 2 points belong to Q. → `bio-e03` (X-linked inheritance). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking a daughter (P or S) — daughters also receive an X from their *father*, so their phenotype can't prove the mother's homozygosity; writing the right letter without the "only from the mother" argument.
**Points:** 2 (letter; explanation) — R + correct mother-only reasoning earns 1 per the supplement.

## Question 33 (official paper)

In female carriers, a shortage of working G6PD can still arise because one X chromosome is inactivated in red-cell precursor cells. Explain that this X-inactivation is a form of epigenetics.

**Solve:** Epigenetics = changed gene activity **without any change in the DNA base sequence**: the inactivated X's genes (here the healthy G6PD allele) are switched off / not expressed, altering the phenotype while the genotype stays identical. → `bio-b01` (gene regulation), `bio-e03`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** calling it a mutation (the sequence is untouched); saying the X is *lost* (it's silenced, present but condensed).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 34 (official paper, mc)

Fabio (an affected son of daughter P) proves P is a carrier. Stella is the daughter of Francesca's other daughter S, and is expecting a boy. What is the chance the baby inherits the mutated G6PD allele?

**Solve:** Half the links in the pedigree, multiplying along the way. (1) Francesca is a carrier, so daughter S had a 1/2 chance of receiving the mutated X. (2) If S is a carrier, she passed it to Stella with probability 1/2 → Stella: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 carrier. (3) Stella's baby — the paper stipulates it will be a boy — gets his single X from Stella: if she's a carrier, 1/2 chance it's the mutated one. Total: 1/4 × 1/2 = **1/8** (option C). → `bio-e03` (X-linked probability). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** stopping at Stella's carrier chance (1/4) and forgetting the transmission to the son; using Fabio's branch (P) instead of S's; counting 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 by dragging in an extra generation.
**Points:** 2.

## Question 35 (official paper)

In a Nigerian population the mutated (X-linked) G6PD allele has frequency 20%. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg (and ignoring epigenetics), what percentage of women and of men has favism?

**Solve:** For an X-linked recessive with allele frequency q = 0.2: men are hemizygous, so their affected fraction equals the allele frequency itself: **20%**. Women need the allele on both X chromosomes: q² = 0.2² = 0.04 → **4%**. One point each. → `bio-e03`, `bio-f01` (Hardy–Weinberg). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** giving q for women too (20%); using 2pq (the *carrier* frequency, 32%) for men — carrier status doesn't exist for hemizygous men.
**Points:** 2 (women 4%; men 20%).

## Question 36 (official paper, mc)

The mutated allele reaches high frequencies exactly where malaria prevails; red cells with less working G6PD are less susceptible to the malaria parasite. How is the high frequency best explained?

**Solve:** Carriers/deficient individuals survive malaria better, so the allele is favoured there — **natural selection** (option E). Gene flow, bottleneck, founder effect and migration don't explain the tie to malaria areas. → `bio-f01` (selection) + `bio-b05`-context. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking founder/bottleneck because "high frequency in one region" (those are *random* effects; the malaria link signals directed selection).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 37 (official paper)

Some websites call favism a "bean allergy". Argue that favism is not an allergic reaction.

**Solve:** Aim at what an allergy *requires* and show it's absent: no antibodies are involved, no histamine is released, the immune system is not activated — favism is an enzyme defect letting reactive oxygen damage red cells, not an immune response. Note the scheme's trap: describing what favism *is* (an enzyme defect), or listing allergy symptoms, alone scores nothing — you must engage with the allergy machinery's absence. → `bio-b05` (allergy/immune system). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** only defining favism; only listing allergy symptoms without stating they're absent here.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 38 (official paper, mc)

The tolerable daily intake of PFOA is 0.63 ng/kg body weight for humans, derived from a value of 750 ng/kg found in rats. Why is the human value lower?

**Solve:** The decisive difference given the options: **humans live longer than rats** (option C) — a near-indigestible, near-unexcretable substance accumulates over a lifetime, and decades more exposure (plus a long window for effects like tumours) demand a stricter daily limit. Size (option D) is already normalised away because both values are *per kg body weight*; trophic level (A) is irrelevant to a direct toxicity limit; more fat tissue (B) is not the reasoning for a water-soluble-protein-bound compound like PFOA. → `bio-a05` (extrapolation in research), `bio-a16` (health contexts). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** choosing "humans are bigger" — the per-kg dosing cancels size; guessing fat storage because many poisons are lipophilic (PFOA binds plasma proteins, not fat).
**Points:** 2.

## Question 39 (official paper)

PFOA is barely excreted because much of it binds plasma proteins in the blood; only unbound PFOA can be excreted. Explain why the protein binding hampers excretion.

**Solve:** In the kidney's glomeruli, ultrafiltration passes small molecules into the pre-urine but **plasma proteins are too large and stay in the blood**; PFOA riding on those proteins is held back from the (pre)urine and thus escapes excretion. → `bio-b03` (kidney ultrafiltration). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying the bond is "too strong" chemically (the point is size-based retention at ultrafiltration); involving the liver instead of the kidney filter.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 40 (official paper)

Tumours can arise through mutations in genes with a regulating role in cell division. Name a type of such genes.

**Solve:** Accepted types: **tumour suppressor genes**, **(proto-)oncogenes**, or "suicide"/**apoptosis genes** (a concrete example of such a gene also scores). Explicitly refused: "regulator genes" — that's the operon term, not a cell-division-control gene class. → `bio-e02` (cell cycle and cancer). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** writing "regulator gene" (refused); naming a carcinogen instead of a gene.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 41 (official paper, mc)

The extra tumours in the test animals might result from a reduced number of T-lymphocytes. Are cancer cells recognised via MHC-I or MHC-II, and are they killed by phagocytosis or by lysis?

**Solve:** Cancer cells are the body's own cells gone wrong: they present abnormal (tumour) antigens on **MHC-I**, which cytotoxic T-lymphocytes check on all body cells; cytotoxic T cells kill the flagged cell by inducing **lysis** (perforin/granzyme → membrane destruction, apoptosis), not by eating it. Answer: MHC-I + lysis (B). MHC-II is the route of antigen-presenting cells toward helper T cells; phagocytosis is the macrophage route. → `bio-b05` (cellular immunity). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking MHC-II because "immune recognition = MHC" without distinguishing class I (own cells) from class II (presenting cells); imagining macrophages eat tumour cells as the standard T-cell mechanism.
**Points:** 2.

## Question 42 (official paper)

Bar chart of vegetable samples from gardens near the Dordrecht chemical plant versus gardens in Bilthoven, 60 km away: around Dordrecht a substantial share of samples contains PFOA, in Bilthoven none of the (few) samples do. Explain why Bilthoven vegetables were tested at all.

**Solve:** Bilthoven is the **control**: a comparable garden area *not* next to a chemical plant. Comparing shows whether the PFOA found near Dordrecht is tied to the local contamination rather than to background levels everywhere. → `bio-a05` (research: control group). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying "to test more vegetables" (quantity isn't the point — contrast is); calling it a "repetition" of the experiment.
**Points:** 1.
