# Walkthrough — Dutch central exam (CvTE) VWO Biology, 2025 tijdvak 1 (English)

**Official paper:** *Examen VWO 2025, tijdvak 1, biologie* (VW-1018-a-25-1-o), sat Friday 23 May 2025, 13:30–16:30; 19 pages, 40 questions in 5 long contexts.
**Sources (official, free):**
- Questions (opgaven): https://www.examenblad.nl/2025/vwo/documenten/cse-1/vw-1018-a-25-1-o
- Marking scheme (correctievoorschrift): https://www.examenblad.nl/2025/vwo/documenten/cse-1/vw-1018-a-25-1-c
- Score→grade table and N-term: https://www.examenblad.nl/2025/vwo/documenten/cse-1/biologie-vwo-omzettingstabel-normering
- Year page: https://www.examenblad.nl/2025/vwo/vakken/exacte-vakken/biologie-vwo
- Addendum: none — no *aanvulling op het correctievoorschrift* (supplement to the marking scheme) was ever published for this time slot (examenblad lists none and the NVON exam archive has an empty "Aanvulling CV" slot for 2025-1).

This is our own walkthrough of that official exam: for every question, what it asks (in our words), a step-by-step solution, the subdomain in our folder structure, the BINAS tables to use, common mistakes and how the points work. Nothing is copied from the paper: no question text, no figures, no tables, no marking-scheme wording. Open the official PDF next to this file.

Exam facts worth knowing first: 3 hours; 40 questions; the cover's maximum of 67 points matches the sum in the marking scheme (this walkthrough's per-question points add to 67). The official score→grade conversion used a forgiving N-term of 1.3 (about 35/67 was already a 6.0 on paper norms). The paper is a mixed bag: 9 letter-choice (A–D) questions worth 1 or 2 points with no partial credit, a lot of open explain-questions, and 8 "write down the numbers and mark each one true/false (or up/down, supported/not supported)" judgement questions — those use tiered scoring (for 3-item lists: 3 correct = 2 points, 2 correct = 1, fewer = 0; for 4-item lists: 4 correct = 2, 3 correct = 1, fewer = 0), so one slip in such a list costs exactly one point. Two rules bite as always: if an open question demands a *verklaring/uitleg* (explanation) or calculation, a bare answer scores 0; and give no more answers than asked — extras are ignored. Binas or ScienceData is allowed, and is genuinely needed at Q8 (Fick's law) and Q19 (the carbon-fixation product); the genetic-code table also helps at Q30.

Subdomain ids refer to the folders in this package (e.g. `bio-b02` = B02 Metabolism in Cells). The exam is Dutch; this walkthrough is English with Dutch exam terms glossed.

## Question 1 (official paper)

Judge three claims about the snake's venom gland and its drainage duct (*afvoerbuisje*): true or false?

**Solve:** Claim 1 (the gland is exocrine): TRUE — exocrine = secreting through a duct to an outside surface; the venom leaves via a duct and fang, like saliva. Claim 2 (the mitochondria-rich acid cells make ATP for the compression muscle): FALSE — ATP is not shipped between cells; the muscle fibres fuel their own contraction with their own mitochondria. Claim 3 (the duct's inner lining is mainly epithelium): TRUE — body tubes and cavities are lined with epithelial tissue. Write "1 juist, 2 onjuist, 3 juist" in your own list. → `bio-c01` (tissues from differentiated cells), `bio-b02` (cell energy). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking specialised "power stations" export ATP to neighbouring cells; calling the gland endocrine because hormones were in the news (no duct = endocrine, duct = exocrine).
**Points:** 2 (list item 1 of 3: 3 correct = 2, 2 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 2 (official paper)

Explain the function, in the venom-producing cells, of the many ribosomes and the large Golgi system.

**Solve:** Ribosomes are the protein-synthesis sites: venom is a protein mixture, so mass production needs many ribosomes. The Golgi system gives the venom proteins their final form (modification/folding finishes) and packages and transports them for secretion out of the cell (vesicles to the membrane). One point per organelle WITH its function linked to venom production. → `bio-b01` (protein synthesis), `bio-c01` (cell organisation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** writing "ribosomes make energy"; describing the Golgi as storage without the modification/transport-for-export role.
**Points:** 2 (one per organelle + fitting function).

## Question 3 (official paper, mc)

Which defence cells make the antibodies in the production animals (horses, sheep)?

**Solve:** Antibodies are secreted by plasma cells (*plasmacellen* — activated effector B cells). Macrophages present antigen and eat, NK cells kill body cells, T-helper cells only stimulate. Answer C. → `bio-b05` (immune system). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking T-helper cells because they "help" the response — helpers never secrete antibodies.
**Points:** 1 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 4 (official paper)

Explain at the MOLECULAR level why each snake species needs its own specific antiserum.

**Solve:** Antibodies only work where their antigen-binding site spatially and chemically fits an epitope. Every snake species has a different mix of venom proteins, so the 3-D shapes to be bound differ per species; an antiserum against one species' venom will not bind (properly) to another species' venom molecules. The scheme insists your answer hinges on the binding between venom (molecules) and antibody/antiserum molecules — a generic "venoms differ" remark without the binding earns nothing. → `bio-d01` (molecular recognition), `bio-b05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** staying at the whole-animal level ("every snake is different"); forgetting the fit/binding word pair.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 5 (official paper)

Which fact from the boxed text shows there were stem cells among the loosened gland cells?

**Solve:** The defining talent of stem cells is that they can differentiate into several cell types. The text says the cells of each little ball later differentiated into various cell types (including venom producers) — that observation is the stem-cell signature. Name THAT fact (not "they divided", any cell can divide). → `bio-c01` (stem cells, differentiation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** citing cell division (somatic cells divide too); citing the growth into hollow organoids without the differentiation step.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 6 (official paper)

The researchers compared organoid mRNA with mRNA from real venom-gland cells and from pancreas cells of the same snake. Describe the results that back the conclusion "these organoids are mini venom glands".

**Solve:** Gene activity = mRNA profile. Two required halves: the organoids' mRNA (profile) MATCHES that of ordinary venom-gland cells (same genes expressed), AND DIFFERS from that of pancreas cells, which are related gland cells but of another type. Both comparisons must appear; one without the other drops a point. → `bio-b01` (gene expression), `bio-a05` (interpreting research results). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** only giving the "matches venom gland" half; comparing DNA instead of mRNA/expression (all body cells hold the same DNA — that's the point of using mRNA).
**Points:** 2 (agreement with the venom gland 1; difference from the pancreas 1).

## Question 7 (official paper)

Judge three claimed advantages of a biobank of frozen venom-gland organoids: true or false?

**Solve:** Claim 1 (fewer venomous snakes need milking): TRUE — the organoids supply venom in the lab. Claim 2 (no more production animals needed for antiserum): FALSE — organoids make VENOM, not antibodies; you still need horses/sheep to raise the antibodies. Claim 3 (antiserum then directly available): FALSE — a freezer full of organoids is not a stock of antiserum; the production route (and time) stays. So "1 juist, 2 onjuist, 3 onjuist". → `bio-b05` (antiserum logic), `bio-c01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reasoning "organoids exist → antiserum exists", skipping the immune animal in the chain; over-reading "direct available" as "faster".
**Points:** 2 (tiered: 3 correct = 2, 2 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 8 (official paper)

Using a factor from Fick's law, explain why a flooded soil reduces oxygen uptake by the roots.

**Solve:** Pull the formula from BINAS (diffusion per unit time = constant × area × Δc/Δx) and tie ONE factor to the flood: water replaces the air in the soil pores, so (a) the concentration difference Δc shrinks (far less O₂ dissolves in water than sits in air); or (b) the distance Δx to the air above the water grows; or (c) the contact area A between roots and air spaces collapses; or (d) the diffusion constant D for O₂ through water is much smaller than through air. Structure your answer in the order: consequence for the root cells (less O₂ uptake) + the factor that changes. Strict dependency: the second point is only awarded if the first (the consequence) is there. → `bio-b02`, `0-basic-02` (diffusion physics). BINAS: the Fick's-law diffusion formula.
**Common mistakes:** naming a factor without linking it to oxygen uptake; writing "the gradient gets smaller" without saying WHICH quantity changes (that phrasing is accepted only if at least one part of the fraction is correctly explained).
**Points:** 2 (consequence 1 + matching Fick factor 1; the 2nd point needs the 1st).

## Question 9 (official paper)

Explain how a shortage of oxygen in root cells leads to reduced water transport to the leaves.

**Solve:** Three-link chain, one point each: (1) oxygen shortage → less aerobic dissimilation/respiration → ATP production in the root cells drops; (2) less ATP → less ACTIVE transport — the active uptake of ions/minerals into the root cells falls; (3) with fewer ions inside, the osmotic value drops, so less water follows by osmosis and root pressure falls → less water is pushed/carried up to the leaves. → `bio-b02` (respiration, active transport, osmosis), `bio-b03` (water transport). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** jumping straight from "no O₂" to "no water" without the ATP → active-transport → osmosis chain (hunger for the middle links is what the 3 points pay for); blaming evaporation from the leaves instead of the root-side mechanism asked.
**Points:** 3 (one per link in the chain).

## Question 10 (official paper)

Through which part of the leaf does most of the ethylene escape when the plant is not submerged?

**Solve:** Gases leave a leaf through the stomata (*huidmondjes*) — the cuticle is nearly gas-tight for vapours; that is also the CO₂/O₂/water-vapour route. One word suffices. → `bio-b03` (leaf gas exchange). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** "via the cuticle/wax layer" (that is what blocks escape); over-answering with a mechanism when only the part is asked.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 11 (official paper)

In the incomplete model of the survival-mode mechanism, three substances and one process carry numbered boxes: for full submergence, does each go up (↑) or down (↓)?

**Solve:** Walk the cascade: ethylene accumulates (given, ↑) → signal cascade → PGB1 concentration rises (given, ↑) → PGB1 scavenges NO. So: (1) the NO bound in the PGB1 complex: ↑ (more PGB1 catches more NO); (2) the FREE NO available to the breakdown enzyme PRT6: ↓; (3) O₂ inside the submerged plant: ↓; PRT6 needs both NO and O₂ to degrade the transcription factor ERF7, so ERF7 piles up and (4) ERF7-driven transcription of the survival-mode genes (OMG): ↑. Pattern: 1 ↑, 2 ↓, 3 ↓, 4 ↑. → `bio-b04` (regulation), `bio-d01` (molecular interaction). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** treating the two NO entries as one pool (the figure splits bound vs free NO); forgetting that under water oxygen itself runs out — a box many mis-annotate.
**Points:** 2 (tiered: 4 correct = 2, 3 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 12 (official paper)

From the bar chart of root-tip survival after four hours without oxygen (wild type, ERF7-mutant, PRT6-mutant; with/without ethylene pre-treatment): judge three statements as supported or not.

**Solve:** Read the data relations, not the rhetoric: (1) Wild type survives much better after ethylene pre-treatment than without → SUPPORTED. (2) "ERF7 mutants do worse than wild type under BOTH treatments" → NOT supported: without pre-treatment their survival is similar to wild type (the claim fails on one arm). (3) "The enzyme PRT6 raises survival at flooding" → NOT supported, indeed the opposite: PRT6 mutants survive markedly better, matching the model (no PRT6 → ERF7 stays up → permanent survival mode). Mark 1 wel, 2 niet, 3 niet. → `bio-a05` (reading experimental results), `bio-b04`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** answering from the text-book mechanism instead of the bars; reading "not different in one arm" incorrectly as support for a both-arms claim.
**Points:** 2 (tiered: 3 correct = 2, 2 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 13 (official paper)

Pick the two genetic modifications (from knock-outs and cisgene additions of the PGB1 gene / ethylene-production genes) that could make crops more flood-tolerant.

**Solve:** The survival pathway needs ethylene build-up and PGB1 to catch NO. So you ADD, not remove: add cisgene PGB1 genes (2) and add cisgene ethylene-production genes (4). (Cisgenesis = inserting extra copies of the species' own or a crossable relative's gene.) Knocking out either pathway gene (1, 3) cripples survival mode. Answer: 2 and 4 only. → `bio-b04` (regulation of the pathway), `bio-f03`-adjacent (crop use). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** slipping in a knock-out because "knocking out sounds powerful"; giving one number or three — only the pair 2+4 scores.
**Points:** 1 (both numbers, no partial credit).

## Question 14 (official paper)

Judge three claims about the growth curve/prediction of the Colombian hippo population: true or false?

**Solve:** (1) "Density-dependent factors matter less as the population grows": FALSE — they bite HARDER at higher density (that is their definition and the reason logistic growth bends). (2) "The strong growth is possible because a fitting niche is available": TRUE — an introduced species explodes exactly when resources/niche space exist and enemies are lacking. (3) "By 2080 the number will lie between 13,000 and 15,000": FALSE — the predicted (open) points rise ever more steeply and shoot through the 8,000-line well before 2060, so 2080 lands far above that band. → `bio-c03` (population growth), `bio-b08`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reading density-dependence backwards (it restrains MORE as numbers rise); extrapolating linearly from the observed dots instead of following the curve shape.
**Points:** 2 (tiered: 3 correct = 2, 2 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 15 (official paper)

Explain with one example how a biotic factor could eventually limit the hippo population's growth in Colombia (no serious droughts exist there).

**Solve:** Pick one living-actor mechanism and make the causal link: an outbreak of disease/parasite that kills animals; intraspecific or interspecific competition that shrinks the grazing available per hippo so fewer calves survive / fertility drops; or predation/hunting (accepted with a fitting explanation). The chain must end at deaths up or births down. → `bio-b08` (density-dependent regulation), `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** naming an ABIOTIC factor (water level, temperature) — the question pins "biotic"; writing "competition" bare without a consequence for survival or reproduction.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 16 (official paper)

Name the biological term for this form of genetic drift: the whole Colombian population descends from four imported animals.

**Solve:** A new population started by a handful of founders carries only a sliver of the original gene pool: the **founder effect** (*stichtereffect*). Do NOT write bottleneck effect (*flessenhalseffect*) — the scheme explicitly gives zero for it: a bottleneck shrinks an existing population, here a NEW population was founded. → `bio-f01` (evolutionary mechanisms). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** the bottleneck/founder swap above; answering "genetic drift" itself — the question asks for the specific form.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 17 (official paper)

If the Colombian hippos became a separate species, would that be sympatric or allopatric speciation? Explain.

**Solve:** ALLOPATRIC (*allopatrische soortvorming*): the Colombian population is geographically separated from the African populations — an ocean lies between the Magdalena lakes and Africa, no contact, no gene flow — and can drift/evolve apart. The explanation must mention the (spatial) separation / absence of contact between the populations, not just the word "allopatric". → `bio-f02` (speciation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reasoning "same kind of lake habitat → sympatric" (sympatry means shared AREA, not shared habitat type); giving the term without the separation argument (explanation required → bare term scores 0).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 18 (official paper)

Describe with one example how hippos can slow succession on land, stating how climax species are affected.

**Solve:** Frame one concrete mechanism, then its hit on the climax species: hippos graze also young saplings, so the late/climax tree species never grow up; or their trampling compacts the soil, so climax species cannot establish; or nutrient export (eat on land, defecate in water) leaves soils where mainly pioneers cope; or trampled vegetation where climax species can't survive. The point of the question: succession stalls because the late species — not necessarily the pioneers — lose out. → `bio-c03` (succession), `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** describing how grazers HELP pioneers without naming the cost to climax establishment/growth/survival (the second point is paid only for that); picking an aquatic effect while the question is about land succession.
**Points:** 2 (valid example 1 + negative effect on climax growth/establishment/survival 1).

## Question 19 (official paper)

In C3 plants the CO₂-fixing enzyme binds CO₂, and a C3-molecule is formed. Name that molecule (use your information book).

**Solve:** In the Calvin cycle (BINAS carbon-fixation table) CO₂ is fixed onto the C5 acceptor, and the 6-carbon product splits into two C3 molecules: glycerate-3-phosphate (3-PGA; Dutch *glycerinezuur-3-fosfaat*). Generously, "glycerinezuur", the 1,3-diphosphate intermediate, or glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate also score; vague "glucose/pyruvate" does not. → `bio-b02` (photosynthesis), `0-basic-01`. BINAS: the photosynthesis carbon-fixation table.
**Common mistakes:** naming RuBP/the C5 acceptor or the end product glucose; writing pyruvate (that is respiration, not fixation).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 20 (official paper, mc)

Animals higher in the food chain hold relatively more ¹⁵N. Which process explains it: amino acids bearing ¹⁴N are relatively more/less often broken down / converted / excreted / formed?

**Solve:** Enzymes discriminate by mass: bonds with the LIGHT isotope react slightly more readily. Deamination/catabolism of amino acids therefore preferentially removes ¹⁴N, which leaves the body in nitrogen waste — the tissue that remains grows relatively richer in ¹⁵N, and each trophic step enriches further. Merely "converting" amino acids keeps the N inside the body, and excreting/forming ¹⁴N-compounds LESS often would not remove it. Answer A. → `bio-b03` (amino-acid breakdown, excretion), `0-basic-01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** choosing "less often excreted" (that would keep ¹⁴N IN the body, flattening the ratio); ignoring isotope discrimination in enzyme kinetics.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 21 (official paper)

Argue from the isotope scatter plot (one dot per lake, lakes with vs without hippos) that the data support: hippos move plant material from land to water.

**Solve:** Two-step argument, one point each: (1) lakes WITH hippos show a HIGHER ¹³C/¹²C ratio (dots shifted to the right; the ¹⁵N axis shows no such split); (2) that high ratio fingerprints C4-plant material — C4 fixers discriminate little between the carbon isotopes — and tropical land grasses are C4, so hippo dung is the plausible carrier taking terrestrial C4 carbon into the lakes. C3-based aquatic autochthonous matter cannot explain the shift. → `bio-b08` (matter flow between ecosystems), `bio-b02` (C3/C4 photosynthesis), `bio-a05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** describing only "the dots differ" without attaching the difference to the C4 signature; arguing via the ¹⁵N axis where no separation exists.
**Points:** 2 (higher ¹³C/¹²C in hippo lakes 1 + caused by C4-plant material from land via dung 1).

## Question 22 (official paper)

Explain why the 24-hour O₂ curve of hippo lakes differs from hippo-free lakes BOTH at night and by day.

**Solve:** The hippo lakes swing with a much larger amplitude around the mean. Night half: the extra organic matter (dung, more algal biomass) is broken down by reducers/bacteria, and decomposition burns O₂, so night-time O₂ dips LOWER than in control lakes. Day half: the added nutrients feed dense algae/submerged plants, so photosynthesis runs hotter than in control lakes and daytime O₂ peaks HIGHER. One point per half with its mechanism. → `bio-b08` (eutrophication), `bio-b02` (photosynthesis vs decomposition). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** explaining only the night drop (the question has two bullets, one point each); claiming hippos themselves consume/produce the O₂.
**Points:** 2 (night: more breakdown by decomposers → O₂ use 1; day: more nutrients → more photosynthesis → O₂ production 1).

## Question 23 (official paper)

Four consequences of hippos: which ones raise the area's biodiversity, which not?

**Solve:** (1) Competition with native manatees, river turtles and otters: NOT — losers of competition decline, diversity falls. (2) Hippos create more different habitats in the river: YES — more niches, more species. (3) Hippo carcasses present: YES — carrion feeds scavengers and decomposers and adds a resource pulse. (4) Eutrophication occurs: NOT — algal dominance and oxygen stress narrow the community. Mark 1 niet, 2 wel, 3 wel, 4 niet. → `bio-f03` (biodiversity), `bio-b08`, `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** scoring "carcasses" as negative because dying animals "reduce numbers" (the resource effect counts, not the mortality); assuming eutrophication boosts diversity because "more algae = more life".
**Points:** 2 (tiered: 4 correct = 2, 3 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 24 (official paper)

Reason from the text AND the pedigree that the Marsili mutation is (a) autosomal and (b) very probably dominant.

**Solve:** (a) The text locates the gene on chromosome 14, and chromosomes 1–22 are autosomes — THAT is the credited route. Be warned: the scheme explicitly refuses credit for pedigree reasoning of the "cannot be X-linked" type. (b) If the allele were recessive, every affected child in the tree would need a carrier father — three unrelated married-in men, ALL carriers of an extremely rare mutation: vanishingly unlikely. With dominance, one allele suffices and the every-generation pattern (affected mother → affected children of both sexes) follows directly. → `bio-e03` (inheritance patterns). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** the excluded "not X-linked" detour above; arguing dominance from "many affected" alone without the carrier implausibility.
**Points:** 2 (autosomal via chromosome 14, 1; dominant via the three in-married men, 1).

## Question 25 (official paper)

In which of three periods could the Marsili mutation have arisen in a cell: (1) during formation of the gametes Maria came from, (2) during the cleavage divisions of the zygote Maria grew from, (3) after Maria's birth?

**Solve:** Maria has the phenotype everywhere and passes the allele to children, so essentially all/most of her cells including germ cells carry it. (1) YES — a de novo mutation in a parent's germline. (2) YES — a mutation in an early cleavage cell makes her a mosaic with the mutation spread through much of her body (germline included). (3) NO — after birth it would affect only one cell's descendants, too little to give her the full-body condition. Mark 1 wel, 2 wel, 3 niet. → `bio-e01` (origin of mutations), `bio-e03`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** rejecting (2) because "mosaic = not fully affected" — the scheme accepts cleavage timing; accepting (3) because "mutations happen anytime" — timing must match the phenotype and heredity.
**Points:** 2 (tiered: 3 correct = 2, 2 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 26 (official paper)

The table of the two pain-fibre types shows "component P" present in the fast fibres, absent in the slow C-fibres. Name component P.

**Solve:** The property pairing "present ↔ much faster conduction" plus absence in the thin, slow fibres is the signature of the **myelin sheath** (Schwann-cell wrapping enabling quick, saltatory conduction). "Node of Ranvier" also scores; answers like "insulation" without the biological name do not. → `bio-b04` (nerve conduction). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** writing "the axon" or "the synapse"; answering "thin diameter" — the table lists diameter separately, so P must be something else.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 27 (official paper, mc)

For 1.0-metre sensory fibres, what is the MINIMUM time between the start of the stabbing pain (Aδ fibres, 6–30 m/s) and the start of the aching pain (C fibres, 0.5–2 m/s)?

**Solve:** "Minimum gap" means: let the stabbing signal arrive as LATE as possible and the aching signal as EARLY as possible — while the stab still comes first. Slowest Aδ: 1.0 m / 6 m/s ≈ 0.17 s. Fastest C: 1.0 m / 2 m/s = 0.50 s. Gap ≈ 0.50 − 0.17 ≈ 0.33 s → about 0.3 s, option A. The trap: using both FASTEST values (0.5 − 0.03 ≈ 0.47) lands on the wrong option. → `bio-b04`, `0-basic-02` (speed = distance/time). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking the fastest speeds for both fibres ("minimum" applies to the INTERVAL, so push the two arrivals towards each other); mixing up which fibre carries which pain.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 28 (official paper)

In the spinal-cord cross-section, three letters mark places where neuron cell bodies sit. Where is the ZFHX2 gene present, and where does its abnormal expression produce the Marsili phenotype?

**Solve:** Every nucleated body cell holds the same genome, so the gene is present at all three lettered places: the grey-matter cell bodies inside the cord (P on the relay side, R on the motor-output side) and in the dorsal-root ganglion outside the cord (Q, sensory cell bodies). The phenotype, however, is caused by disturbed expression in the C-sensory pain neurons, whose cell bodies lie in the dorsal-root ganglion: only Q. Fill the template: presence P, Q, R; abnormal expression Q only. → `bio-b01` (gene presence vs expression), `bio-b04`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** answering "only at Q" for presence too (every cell has the gene — differentiation is about EXPRESSION); naming both P and Q for the phenotype (the C-fibre bodies sit outside the cord).
**Points:** 2 (present in all three, 1; expression-effect only in Q, 1).

## Question 29 (official paper)

The mutation sits in a segment of the gene that shows no variation across humans and the listed animals. Explain why that segment stayed identical through evolution.

**Solve:** This is purifying selection: individuals in whom this segment mutates respond poorly to body damage/pain (the ZFHX2 protein fails them), so they have lower survival/fitness/reproductive success — such alleles get selected out and the functional sequence persists unchanged. The mirrored phrasing (non-mutants respond better and so enjoy greater survival/reproduction) earns the same credit. Both the functional consequence AND the fitness consequence are needed. → `bio-f01` (selection). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** writing "the segment is protected from mutation" (mutation hits everywhere; survival filters it); stopping at "the protein is important" without the selection step.
**Points:** 2 (poor pain/damage response when mutated, 1; lower survival/reproduction → selected out, 1).

## Question 30 (official paper, mc)

Which point mutation in the CODING strand of the gene made the Marsili change in the mice (compare the single differing amino acid in the protein alignment and use the code table)?

**Solve:** The Marsili row differs from the human wild-type row (and from all animal rows) at exactly one residue: a lysine where the wild types carry an arginine. Lysine codons are AAA/AAG; arginine codons are CGN plus AGA/AGG. Only one single-letter change bridges the two: AGA → AAA or AGG → AAG — a G→A substitution at the codon's middle position in the coding strand. Answer C (G→A); the C→… options never turn Arg into Lys. → `bio-b01` (code table), `bio-e01` (point mutations). BINAS: the genetic-code table.
**Common mistakes:** reading the template/complement strand instead of the coding strand named in the question; guessing C→T because that change is common without checking the codons.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 31 (official paper, mc)

In one family the Na⁺ channels in the C-sensory neurons open at a smaller rise of the membrane potential than normal. Higher or lower threshold potential — and CIP or IEM?

**Solve:** "Opens on a smaller depolarisation" = the threshold sits LOWER than normal. Lower threshold → channels fire too easily → EXAGGERATED pain signalling, which is IEM (extreme pain at slight warmth), not the pain-free CIP. Answer D. → `bio-b04` (action-potential threshold), `bio-d01` (channel behaviour). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** swapping the diseases (CIP = no pain at all; IEM = far too much pain); reasoning "smaller rise → higher threshold" — the threshold is the level REACHED.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 32 (official paper, mc)

Does a loss-of-function mutation usually inherit dominantly or recessively — and a gain-of-function mutation?

**Solve:** Loss of function: one intact allele usually still makes enough working protein, so the defect hides in heterozygotes → RECESSIVE (CIP fits this). Gain of function: the altered protein does something extra/stronger, so one mutant allele already changes the phenotype → DOMINANT (IEM, and Marsili, fit this). Answer C. → `bio-e03` (dominance/recessivity). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** matching by severity ("dominant = worse") instead of asking what ONE allele does in a heterozygote; flipping loss vs gain.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 33 (official paper, mc)

A drug that plugs the pore of the C-neuron Na⁺ channel also plugs the identical pore of cardiac Na⁺ channels. Which condition could that cause?

**Solve:** Blocking voltage-gated sodium channels in the heart disturbs the generation and spread of electrical impulses → disturbed rhythm: an arrhythmia (*hartritmestoornis*). It neither starves heart muscle of blood (infarct) nor damages valves. Answer B. → `bio-b04` (heart excitation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking a heart attack because "blocked = no flow" — here the block is electrical, not vascular; choosing the valve option (no structural link).
**Points:** 1 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 34 (official paper)

Explain at the molecular level why enzyme reactions (like histidine decarboxylation in spoiled food) barely run below 4 °C.

**Solve:** Rate depends on collisions: at low temperature molecules move slowly, so enzyme and substrate molecules meet rarely and with too little energy — far fewer (effective) collisions per second, i.e. a much smaller chance that enzyme binds substrate. State it in those molecular terms, not as "the enzyme is inactive/denatured" (cold enzymes aren't denatured). → `bio-b02` (enzyme kinetics), `0-basic-01` (particle model). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying the enzyme "freezes/denatures" — denaturation is a HEAT story; describing bulk spoilage slowing instead of the collision argument.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 35 (official paper)

Explain why histidine occurs mainly in protein-rich products (fish, cheese).

**Solve:** Histidine is an amino acid — one of the twenty building blocks of proteins. Where protein (and thus amino-acid content) is high, histidine is high; low-protein foods supply little of it. The single credited insight is "histidine is an amino acid". → `bio-b01`, `0-basic-01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** explaining via bacteria making it (the bacteria only CONVERT it); saying "it is part of protein" without the amino-acid identification.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 36 (official paper)

Four molecules from the H1-receptor cascade figure: mark whether each serves as a second messenger.

**Solve:** A second messenger relays the signal INSIDE the cell after an extracellular ligand docks. (1) H1-receptor: NOT — a membrane protein, the docking site itself. (2) Histamine: NOT — it is the extracellular (first) messenger/ligand. (3) IP3: YES — formed in the cascade, diffuses in the cytosol, releases Ca²⁺. (4) GDP: NOT — it sits in the G-protein GDP/GTP switch machinery; it is not the diffusible relay. Mark 1 niet, 2 niet, 3 wel, 4 niet. → `bio-d01` (signal transduction). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** awarding second-messenger status to the hormone itself; including the G-protein's GDP because "it appears inside the cell" — presence in the cytosol is not enough.
**Points:** 2 (tiered: 4 correct = 2, 3 correct = 1, else 0).

## Question 37 (official paper)

Explain how contraction of smooth muscle in the bronchioles leads to stimulation of the breathing centre (*ademcentrum*).

**Solve:** Three links, one point each: (1) contracted smooth muscle narrows the bronchioles → less air (per unit time) passes → ventilation drops; (2) poor ventilation changes blood gases: CO₂ concentration rises (equivalently pH falls, or O₂ falls); (3) (chemo)receptors sense that change and signal the breathing centre in the medulla, which raises the breathing frequency. → `bio-b04` (breathing regulation), `bio-b03` (gas exchange). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** skipping the blood-gas middle link and writing "brain notices shortness of breath"; naming stretch receptors in the lung — the graded signal here is the blood CO₂/pH/O₂ change.
**Points:** 3 (narrower airways → less ventilation 1; CO₂ up / pH or O₂ down 1; chemoreceptor signal 1).

## Question 38 (official paper, mc)

H2-receptors on the smooth muscle of the small arteries (arterioles) make those cells relax when histamine binds. Which blood pressure reading fits?

**Solve:** Relaxed arteriolar muscle = wide vessels = much lower peripheral resistance → blood pressure FALLS. Among the options only the low reading 80/40 mmHg fits; 120/80 is normal, 160/120 is high. Answer A. → `bio-b04`, `bio-b03` (circulation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reading "relaxes" as "slows the heart" and dithering — the effect is vascular, on resistance; forgetting that systolic AND diastolic both drop together.
**Points:** 1 (all-or-nothing).

## Question 39 (official paper)

Two symptoms of the poisoning seem contradictory: fever, yet a strongly perfused red skin. Explain why the skin effect OPPOSES fever.

**Solve:** More blood through the skin dumps more heat to the surroundings (radiation/convection) — the body LOSES heat. Fever is the opposite strategy: the raised set-point makes the body hold and produce heat. So the flushed skin works against the fever by cooling the body. → `bio-b04` (thermoregulation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** explaining the redness instead of the heat balance (the point is paid for the heat-LOSS conclusion); claiming vasodilation "causes" fever.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 40 (official paper, mc)

Clemastine is fully absorbed from the gut, strongly broken down in the liver, and gut uptake starts about 30 minutes after intake. Ninety minutes after a tablet, is the concentration in the hepatic vein (*leverader*) higher or lower than in the portal vein (*poortader*)? And in the hepatic artery (*leverslagader*) compared with the portal vein?

**Solve:** Map the plumbing: absorbed drug drains from the intestine into the PORTAL vein → liver → hepatic vein. At 1.5 h the gut is still releasing the drug, so the portal inflow carries the highest concentration. Passing the liver, much of the drug is degraded, so the HEPATIC VEIN (outflow) holds LESS than the portal vein. The HEPATIC ARTERY brings systemic arterial blood; the drug reaches it only after surviving the liver and recirculating, so its concentration there is also LOWER than in the portal vein. Answer D (lower, lower). → `bio-b03` (liver circulation, drug breakdown). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking the artery "feeds" the liver the drug (the gut feeds it via the portal vein); assuming breakdown raises the downstream concentration; reading the absorption start-time backwards.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing).
