- Questions: https://www.examenblad.nl/2022/vwo/documenten/cse-1/vw-1018-a-22-1-o

This is our own walkthrough of that official exam: for every question, what it asks (in our words), a step-by-step solution, the subdomain in our folder structure, the BINAS tables to use, common mistakes and how the points work. Nothing is copied from the paper: no question text, no figures, no tables, no marking-scheme wording. Open the official PDF next to this file.

Exam facts worth knowing first: 3 hours, handwritten on paper, BINAS allowed. No addendum (*aanvulling correctievoorschrift*) was ever issued for tijdvak 1 — the only 2022 addendum belongs to tijdvak 2, so this scheme is final. The nine MC questions are the classic VWO-biologie "letterlijst" type: two forced choices combined into one letter, up to option F. Five open questions ask you to judge a run of numbered statements as *wel/niet* (derivable / correct or not); these are scored steeply: all three (or four) right = 2 points, exactly one wrong = 1 point, worse = 0 — no room for one lucky guess. As usual: if an explanation or reasoning is required, an answer without it scores 0, and if *two* examples or aspects are asked, extra ones are ignored.

Subdomain ids refer to the folders in this package (e.g. `bio-b02` = B02 Metabolism in Cells). Dutch exam terms are glossed in brackets.

## Question 1 (official paper)

A "natural" slimming remedy based on *Aristolochia* works because the aristolochic acid in it is diuretic (drives off water). Reason out to what extent such a diuretic will really make you slimmer.

**Solve:** Separate weight from slimming. On the scales you do get lighter, because the kidneys expel extra water — so yes, short-term weight loss. But "slimming" means losing body fat, and no fat (no organic material) is broken down or lost; as soon as you stop drinking less / the drug wears off, the lost water is simply taken up again. So it is fake slimming: dehydration, not fat loss. → `bio-b03` (water balance of the body). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying it does not work at all (you do lose weight — the trick is distinguishing weight from fat); claiming fat is converted into urine water.
**Points:** 2 (one for the water-weight concession, one for the no-fat-loss / temporary core).

## Question 2 (official paper, mc)

Early researchers thought the acid acts on release of a pituitary hormone (*hypofysehormoon*). Which hormone, and would its release be stimulated or inhibited by a diuretic?

**Solve:** The water-balance hormone released from the pituitary is ADH (antidiuretic hormone): more ADH → more water reabsorbed in the collecting ducts → *less* urine. A substance that produces *more* urine must therefore suppress ADH release (inhibit, geremd). The decoys: renine — renin is not a pituitary hormone at all (it comes from the juxtaglomerular cells of the kidney); TSH drives the thyroid, not water loss. Answer: the letter standing for ADH, release inhibited (F). → `bio-b03`, `bio-b04` (hormonal regulation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** picking renin because "it also regulates water" — the question explicitly restricts you to pituitary hormones; flipping the direction of ADH's effect.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing letter).

## Question 3 (official paper)

It is now assumed aristolochic acid *lowers* the collecting ducts' permeability to urea. Reason out how that leads to more water loss in urine.

**Solve:** Follow the urea. Less permeability → less urea is reabsorbed from the collecting duct into the kidney tissue (renal medulla), so more urea stays behind in the pre-urine. That changes the osmotic balance in two linked ways: the tissue's osmotic value drops and the (pre-)urine's osmotic value rises, so the gradient that normally pulls water out of the collecting duct collapses — more water stays in the tubule and leaves as urine. State both steps. → `bio-b03`, `bio-b02` (osmosis). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** stopping at "urea holds water" without the osmotic-value comparison; saying urea "blocks the water channels" (the mechanism is osmotic, not a plug).
**Points:** 2 (one for less urea reabsorption, one for the osmotic-value consequence).

## Question 4 (official paper)

The patients' kidney damage was often discovered by chance through routine blood tests showing severe anaemia (too few red blood cells). Explain.

**Solve:** The kidney is also an endocrine organ: it makes EPO (erythropoietin), the hormone that switches the red bone marrow to produce red blood cells. Damaged, shrunken kidneys produce too little EPO → red-cell production falls → anaemia. → `bio-b03`, `bio-b04`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** explaining via blood *loss* through the urine (that is not in the story); naming iron deficiency — the question points at the kidney's hormonal role.
**Points:** 1 (the EPO link must be explicit).

## Question 5 (official paper, uitwerkbijlage)

A DNA nucleotide with the aristolactam DNA-adduct attached is drawn on the leaflet. Circle the adduct and name the nitrogen base it is bound to.

**Solve:** The adduct is everything that does not belong to a normal nucleotide: the foreign multi-ring group hanging off the base (you may include or omit the H of the amino group). The base itself is **guanine**: recognise it in BINAS from the two-ringed purine carrying BOTH a carbonyl (C=O) and an amino (NH₂) group — adenine has no carbonyl, cytosine and thymine are single rings. → `bio-b01`, `0-basic-01` (organic structures). BINAS: the table with the structures of the four DNA bases.
**Common mistakes:** circling only a single atom instead of the whole adduct; answering adenine (confusing the two purines).
**Points:** 2 (circle + name separately). Partial generosity: just "G" also earns the base point; if you circled including the amino N and wrote "hypoxanthine / I", one of the two points is still given.

## Question 6 (official paper)

p53 checks DNA during the cell cycle and pushes cells with heavy DNA damage into apoptosis (programmed cell death). Explain why a defective TP53 gene makes cancer much more likely in that cell line.

**Solve:** Two links, both required. (1) Without working p53, a cell with damaged DNA is not stopped and not killed: it keeps dividing. (2) Therefore damage/mutations are passed on and accumulate over the generations of the cell line, until a combination arises that turns a descendant into an unregulated (cancer) cell. → `bio-e02` (cell-cycle control), `bio-e01` (mutations). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** only saying "p53 repairs DNA" (it does not repair; it decides on arrest/death); giving one link only — "keeps dividing" without the accumulation, or accumulation without survival.
**Points:** 2 (survival/continued division + accumulation).

## Question 7 (official paper)

Why were mutations *inside* introns themselves not investigated — and why *were* mutations on the splice sites of introns investigated?

**Solve:** Within-intron changes are silent for the protein: introns are cut out of the pre-mRNA before translation, so their sequence never reaches the ribosome. Splice sites are different: they mark the start and end of each intron, and a mutation there can wreck splicing — introns left in or exons lost — producing deviant gene expression / a deviant protein. → `bio-b01` (gene expression, splicing). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying introns "are not translated because they are not genes" (they are transcribed, then removed); calling a splice-site mutation silent because "it is not in an exon".
**Points:** 2 (one per part).

## Question 8 (official paper)

Judge three statements about the TP53 mutation map (all mutations found in kidney tumours of 42 users, shown as arrows at base pairs, with intron splice sites numbered):

1. the exon mutations are all point mutations;
2. the exon mutations are all caused by aristolochic acid;
3. the introns are shorter than the exons.

**Solve:** (1) Derivable — the figure shows single substitutions at A–T and G–C pairs: substitutions ARE point mutations, so **wel** (yes, it can be derived). (2) **Niet** — location and mutation type say nothing about the *cause*; point mutations also occur spontaneously, so "all caused by the acid" cannot be read from the figure. (3) **Niet** — introns are only marked by number and splice-site triangles on a schematic map; their true lengths are not represented. Answer: 1 wel, 2 niet, 3 niet. → `bio-b01`, `bio-a01` (reading data strictly). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** treating "typical aristolochic-acid mutation signature" as proof of cause (the figure alone cannot prove origin); assuming a gene map is drawn to scale for introns.
**Points:** 2 — graded steeply: 3 right = 2, exactly 2 right = 1, fewer = 0.

## Question 9 (official paper)

To estimate cancer risk in the urinary tract you could either count aristolactam adducts in the DNA, or count 'A–T → T–A' mutations. Both methods are equally reliable but not equally valid. Show why method 1 (adducts) is less suitable.

**Solve:** Validity = do you measure what you actually want to know? The risk depends on *mutations that actually happened*; adducts only show exposure. Many adducts may sit on harmless DNA positions or be repaired before they ever become a mutation — so a high adduct count need not mean high risk; and adducts may disappear over time, making past exposure invisible. Method 2 measures the consequences themselves. → `bio-a05` (research: reliability vs validity), `bio-a09`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** answering "adducts are harder to count" (that is reliability — the question grants both methods equal reliability); confusing adduct with mutation throughout.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 10 (official paper)

Of the two variants of eelgrass (*groot zeegras*), the flexible annual one lies in the tidal zone and regularly falls dry at low tide; the robust one is permanently submerged. Explain why the flexible variant's limp (slap) leaf sheaths fit where it lives.

**Solve:** You need advantage + the matching environmental factor. At low tide a limp sheath lets the shoot flop flat onto the wet sediment: it stays in contact with the damp bottom and dries out far less than a stiff, upright shoot would. Equally acceptable: a limp sheath bends with the waves/current instead of snapping — wave action is the tidal-zone factor that stiff sheaths would not survive. Either angle works. → `bio-f01` (adaptation), `bio-a11` (form and function). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** giving the advantage ("does not dry out") without naming the tidal factor it matches (or vice versa) — the scheme wants both; answering about the robust, submerged variant.
**Points:** 2 (advantage + tidal factor).

## Question 11 (official paper)

Reintroduction of the robust variant only works if trawling and eutrophication are both limited. Reason out how eutrophication can block a successful reintroduction.

**Solve:** Build the three-link chain. (1) Eutrophication = surplus nutrients → algae multiply massively (algal bloom). (2) The bloom clouds the water / covers the leaves → less light reaches the eelgrass → less photosynthesis. (3) Less photosynthesis → growth is hampered and the plants weaken/die → the reintroduction fails. → `bio-b08` (ecosystem regulation), `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** the scheme explicitly refuses "the eelgrass dies from oxygen shortage" — decay of the bloom lowers O₂, but that argument earns nothing here because the chain asked for runs through light; missing the middle link (light) between bloom and poor growth.
**Points:** 3 (one per link: bloom → less light/photosynthesis → hampered growth/death).

## Question 12 (official paper)

The flexible variant spreads mainly by seeds, the robust one mainly by rhizomes (wortelstokken). Explain how the robust variant's way of reproducing may have contributed to its local extinction when a disease struck around 1930.

**Solve:** Three full routes earn the marks; one is enough. Route A (genetics): vegetative spread by rhizomes = clones → almost no genetic variation → a single disease or adverse condition hits every individual equally → the whole population dies. Route B (spread): rhizomes only creep locally, no seeds that travel → the population cannot (re)colonise other spots, so a local catastrophe wipes out everything at once. Route C (trawling): bottom trawls churn the sediment and shred the shallow rhizomes → with the rhizomes gone, vegetative reproduction stops and the remnant dies out. → `bio-e03` (reproduction strategies), `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** calling rhizome growth sexual reproduction that "makes weak seeds" (it is asexual — no seeds involved); mentioning variation but not linking it to the disease killing ALL clones.
**Points:** 2 (premise about the reproduction + consequence for the population). Both links needed — a bare "no variation" is only half.

## Question 13 (official paper)

At night the sediment's oxygen runs out and the root cells switch to ethanol fermentation. Judge four partial processes of dissimilation for the oxygen-free root cell: 1 oxidative phosphorylation; 2 NADH → NAD⁺; 3 NAD⁺ → NADH; 4 formation of ATP.

**Solve:** With no O₂ the respiratory chain has no final electron acceptor → oxidative phosphorylation stops: **1 niet**. Glycolysis still runs: NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH (**3 wel**) and a net amount of ATP is formed (**4 wel**). To keep glycolysis turning, NADH must be unloaded again — that is exactly what pyruvate → ethanol does: NADH → NAD⁺ (**2 wel**). Answer: 1 niet, 2 wel, 3 wel, 4 wel. → `bio-b02` (fermentation vs respiration). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking "no oxygen = no ATP at all" (glycolysis still yields a small net ATP); forgetting that fermentation's whole point is NAD⁺ regeneration — process 2 is the one most often missed.
**Points:** 2 — steep: 4 right = 2, 3 right = 1, fewer = 0.

## Question 14 (official paper)

On four seagrass locations, eight plots each: seven plots each got a different combination of treatments (keeping out seaweed with mesh cages, wave action with sandbags, lugworms with gauze on the seafloor), one plot was the control. Explain why the researchers built in seven combinations, and give the number of the control.

**Solve:** The seven = every possible non-empty subset of the three factors: three singles, three pairs, one triple. Removing factors one at a time shows the effect of EACH factor separately; removing two or three at once exposes INTERACTIONS between factors (does wave action matter more when seaweed is also gone?). The control is the plot with no treatment at all — the one where nothing is excluded: number 8 in the paper's table. → `bio-a06` (research design), `bio-a05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** only justifying the three single-factor plots (that answers half the design question); picking a "no treatment" combination that is not the full-control number — the table's numbering matters.
**Points:** 3 (single-factor effects + interactions + control number 8).

## Question 15 (official paper)

A female tiger mosquito passes through different trophic levels in her life cycle. Larvae eat dead plant remains in small water bodies; adults sip nectar; the female also bites mammals and birds for a blood meal. Assign the larva and the adult female to the listed trophic levels (consumers of 1st/2nd/3rd order, producer, reducent).

**Solve:** Larva: it eats dead *plant* material — detritus of producers still counts as feeding on the first trophic level → consumer of the 1st order (number 1 only). Note it is NOT classed as reducent here: a reducent decomposes dead matter chemically down to mineral nutrients; a larva just eats the fragments. Adult female: nectar → 1st-order consumer; blood of mammals/birds that are themselves 1st-order consumers (herbivores) → she is then 2nd order; blood of 2nd-order consumers (e.g. insect-eating birds) → 3rd order. So the female: **1, 2 and 3**. → `bio-b08` (food chains), `bio-d05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** labelling the larva a reducent because "it lives on dead stuff"; giving the female only level 1 (nectar) and forgetting that the blood meals push her up two more levels.
**Points:** 2 (larva right + all three numbers, and ONLY those, for the female).

## Question 16 (official paper)

Day length and temperature both announce winter, yet tiger mosquitoes rely on day length to time their diapause eggs. Explain why day length is the more reliable stimulus.

**Solve:** Day length follows an identical, fixed pattern every single year (set by astronomy, not weather); temperature wanders — an autumn can be warm or cold. Basing a life-or-death decision (lay cold-resistant eggs in time) on a temperature dip risks being fooled by one mild spell. → `bio-d03` (abiotic stimuli and responses). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** writing that day length "is easier to measure" for the mosquito (reliability is about predictability for the coming winter, not measurability); giving a human-weather answer instead of the yearly-pattern argument.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 17 (official paper)

Judge three statements against the two graphs of critical photoperiod (the day length at which 50% of females lay diapause eggs) versus latitude, measured in the USA and Japan shortly after the 1985 invasion and again about 20 years later:

1. in 2008 the US mosquitoes laid diapause eggs later in the year, on average, than in 1988;
2. populations living south of 26° N cannot produce diapause eggs;
3. the North American mosquitoes descend from a Japanese population from a temperate region, north of 30° N.

**Solve:** (1) **Wel** — the later US points sit at shorter critical day lengths; if diapause is triggered only when days are shorter still, that trigger arrives later in autumn. (2) **Niet** — the graphs simply contain no data below 26° N; absence of data is not evidence of absence of diapause. (3) **Wel** — the US 1988 values lie in the same (high, i.e. northern) critical-photoperiod range as Japanese populations from temperate latitudes above 30° N, matching the known origin in temperate East Asia. → `bio-f01` (rapid evolution/adaptation), `bio-a01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reading a lower critical photoperiod as "earlier" (shorter trigger day length = LATER onset of diapause behaviour); extrapolating a graph beyond its data range for statement 2.
**Points:** 2 — steep: 3 right = 2, exactly 2 right = 1, fewer = 0.

## Question 18 (official paper)

Give one explanation for the small variation in critical photoperiod among the American populations in 1988.

**Solve:** The whole US population had only just descended from very few founders — plausibly one shipment of tyres from one Japanese region. Starting from so little genetic material, all US populations were still genetically almost identical for photoperiod response: the founder effect (stichtereffect/foundereffect). → `bio-f02`, `bio-f01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** explaining via strong *selection* making them uniform (20 years of selection comes later; in 1988 they were freshly founded); invoking mutation instead of the bottleneck.
**Points:** 1 (naming descent from a few founders, or the founder effect by name, suffices).

## Question 19 (official paper)

The figure numbers the three symbiotic relations among human, tiger mosquito and chikungunya virus. Name the most fitting form of symbiosis for each.

**Solve:** Ask for each pair who profits (+) and who suffers (–). Mosquito → human: the mosquito takes blood, the human is bitten/harmed → parasitism. Virus ↔ mosquito: the virus gets transport, the mosquito "has no problems with it" — unaffected (0) → commensalism. Virus → human: the virus multiplies and makes the human seriously ill → parasitism. So relation 1 parasitism, 2 commensalism, 3 parasitism (matching the paper's numbering). → `bio-d05` (symbiosis forms). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** calling the mosquito–virus relation mutualism (the mosquito gains nothing — transport is no benefit to IT); calling biting "predation" — a parasite that leaves the host alive.
**Points:** 2 — steep: 3 right = 2, 2 right = 1, fewer = 0.

## Question 20 (official paper)

A dog anti-flea drug taken by a human killed every biting mosquito for 18 hours. Before it may be marketed for humans, more research is needed. Name two aspects that must have been investigated.

**Solve:** Think like a drug-registration form, and pick two DIFFERENT aspects: the minimal effective dosage; the maximal safe dose (per kg body weight per day); side effects; interactions with other medicines; contra-indications (pregnancy, illness); effects on the unborn child; long-term effects. Any two distinct ones score. → `bio-a05` (research), `bio-a09` (weighing risks). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** giving two examples of the SAME aspect (two different side effects = only one point — the scheme caps that at 1); naming "does it work on other mosquito species" (the question is about safety/dosing for HUMAN use).
**Points:** 2 (one per distinct aspect).

## Question 21 (official paper)

Judge three statements against the mammal phylogenetic tree marked with menstruating / non-menstruating / unknown:

1. menstruation arose independently at least three times;
2. in primate evolution, menstruation was lost in at least one line;
3. menstruating bats are more closely related to primates than non-menstruating bats are.

**Solve:** (1) **Wel** — menstruating marks appear on several separate branches whose common ancestors are non/unknown markers, so the trait evolved convergently multiple times. (2) **Wel** — within the primate clade a menstruating ancestor's descendant line shows non-menstruating members: a loss event. (3) **Niet** — all bats form one clade; a menstruating bat does not become a closer cousin of primates by sharing this convergent trait. Relatedness is read from branching, not from one shared feature. → `bio-f02` (phylogeny), `bio-a01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** counting branches instead of independent *origins* (two sisters sharing one menstruating ancestor = one origin); letting statement 3 seduce you — shared trait ≠ shared recent ancestor.
**Points:** 2 — steep: 3 right = 2, 2 right = 1, fewer = 0.

## Question 22 (official paper)

The bleeding after pseudopregnancy in the mouse and the human monthly menstruation are set in motion the same way. Explain how the absence of an embryo leads to menstruation in humans.

**Solve:** No embryo → no HCG (the embryonic signal that would rescue the corpus luteum; with the normal LH surge gone too). The corpus luteum (geel lichaam) therefore degenerates. The corpus luteum is the progesterone source, and progesterone maintains the decidua — so progesterone production falls away and the prepared lining/decidua is shed: menstruation. → `bio-e03` (cycle), `bio-b04` (hormonal control). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying the corpus luteum disappears "because there is no progesterone" (that inverts cause and effect — progesterone is what the corpus luteum MAKES); omitting the middle step and jumping from "no embryo" straight to "lining is shed" — one of the two marked links is then missing.
**Points:** 2 (loss of the corpus luteum via missing HCG/LH + progesterone failure as the shedding trigger).

## Question 23 (official paper, mc)

Some scientists think primate menstruation evolved from an ancestral trait "bleeding after pseudopregnancy" (as seen in mice). Which one datum would make that evolutionary link plausible — A: a tree-dwelling primate relative menstruates; B: a dwarf mouse lemur bleeds after pseudopregnancy; C: a rabbit bleeds after pseudopregnancy.

**Solve:** The proposed path runs through the primate lineage: the ANCESTOR of primates had "bleeding after pseudopregnancy", and menstruation crystallised out of it. You therefore want that ancestral trait showing up *inside or next to the primate group*. A dwarf mouse lemur IS a primate (prosimian) that does not menstruate — if it still bleeds after pseudopregnancy, the raw material sits exactly where the theory needs it: option **B**. A menstruating distant relative adds nothing new, and a rabbit is a far relative (like the mouse) — its pseudopregnancy bleeding cannot tie the trait to primates. → `bio-f02` (evidence of common descent), `bio-f01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** choosing C by "mouse and rabbit are comparable" logic — comparability is not relatedness; choosing a menstruating primate relative, which supports "menstruation in the family" but not the specific precursor hypothesis.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 24 (official paper)

The evolutionary mother–fetus conflict: the fetus maximises what it extracts, the mother must also save for future children. Show that both changes are good for the fetus: 1 the small arteries in the mother's decidua lose their muscle tissue; 2 placental hormones make the mother less sensitive to insulin.

**Solve:** Change 1: arterial wall muscle is what lets the mother narrow those vessels (vasoconstriction). Without muscle she cannot throttle them → blood keeps flowing to the placenta unimpeded → more nutrients and oxygen reach the fetus. Change 2: less insulin sensitivity → mother's cells take up less glucose from her blood → her blood glucose stays HIGHER → a steeper supply of glucose across the placenta for the fetus. Both are fetal "wins" at maternal expense. → `bio-e03` (pregnancy), `bio-b04` (blood-sugar regulation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** saying change 2 "gives the fetus more insulin" — the mother's insulin stays hers; the point is the glucose that is left over; for change 1, vague "better blood flow" without the lost vasoconstriction mechanism.
**Points:** 2 (one per change, with its mechanism).

## Question 25 (official paper)

Decidua cells seem to "judge" embryo quality: embryos with major chromosomal abnormalities see implantation inhibited and are shed — an estimated 30–60% of embryos miscarry this way. Explain the evolutionary advantage of such a miscarriage.

**Solve:** Reproduction is an investment problem. Aborting a doomed embryo early stops the energy/time leak: the mother can become pregnant again almost immediately with a realistic chance of a HEALTHY offspring, and she keeps resources for her existing or future young. A failing embryo carried to term would cost a whole pregnancy and yield nothing. → `bio-f01` (fitness reasoning), `bio-e03`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** answering at the wrong level ("it is better for the species" without the individual payoff — although the scheme does explicitly also accept "prevents accumulation of serious chromosomal abnormalities in the population"); saying the miscarriage repairs or cures the embryo.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 26 (official paper)

In the signal cascade for decidualisation, an extracellular signal molecule activates a membrane receptor, which raises cAMP inside the cell, which triggers differentiation. Show why cAMP is called a SECOND messenger.

**Solve:** Because it passes on a message it did not originate. The FIRST messenger is the extracellular signal/hormone — it carries the instruction but (cannot or does not need to enter and) acts at the membrane. cAMP is generated INSIDE the cell upon receptor activation and relays the instruction onward to the cellular response. First messenger outside, second messenger inside. → `bio-d01` (signal transduction), `bio-b04`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** defining "second" as "less important" or "arriving second in time in the blood"; forgetting to mention that the first messenger is the outside signal substance.
**Points:** 1.

## Question 27 (official paper)

Judge whether each proposed link between progesterone and the rise of cAMP in endometrial cells COULD be right:

1. progesterone and the membrane receptor form a complex that binds DNA and raises the expression of cAMP;
2. progesterone makes certain cells release a signal substance that binds the membrane receptor on endometrial cells;
3. progesterone causes reduced expression of the enzyme adenyl cyclase in endometrial cells.

**Solve:** (1) **Niet** — cAMP is a small molecule, not a gene product; there is no such thing as "expression of cAMP" (and the steroid-DNA route does not fit the depicted membrane cascade). (2) **Wel** — progesterone can act upstream: induce neighbouring cells to secrete a paracrine signal, which then hits the endometrial membrane receptor → adenyl cyclase → cAMP. (3) **Niet** — adenyl cyclase MAKES cAMP; less of the enzyme means less cAMP, the opposite of the observed rise. Answer: 1 niet, 2 wel, 3 niet. → `bio-d01`, `bio-b01` (what "expression" means). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** accepting anything that sounds molecular (statement 1 fails on the word "expression" alone); marking 3 wel because "a changed expression was mentioned" — read the DIRECTION of the effect.
**Points:** 2 — steep: 3 right = 2, 2 right = 1, fewer = 0.

## Question 28 (official paper, mc)

During pregnancy and the subsequent nursing period the menstrual cycle is silent: the suckling young influences the release of GnRH and LH. Is GnRH release stimulated or inhibited, and LH release then stimulated or inhibited?

**Solve:** Lactation suppresses the reproductive axis: suckling INHIBITS GnRH release from the hypothalamus. GnRH is the driver of LH release from the pituitary, so LH falls as well. No LH pulses → no follicle maturation/ovulation → the cycle stays off. Both inhibited → the letter for inhibited + inhibited (D). → `bio-e03`, `bio-b04`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** thinking the cycle pauses via INCREASED GnRH "holding" the system (suppression is the active signal here); knowing GnRH drops but letting LH "compensate upward" — downstream follows upstream.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing letter).

## Question 29 (official paper, mc)

The NMDA receptor is an ion channel activated when glutamate and glycine bind simultaneously; active NMDA receptors excite the postsynaptic neuron. Is the receptor in the pre- or postsynaptic membrane, and does N₂O binding prevent depolarisation, repolarisation or hyperpolarisation?

**Solve:** "Activated receptors excite the POSTsynaptic neuron" — the receptor those transmitters bind to sits where the excitation happens: the postsynaptic membrane. Excitation of a neuron = depolarisation (an excitatory postsynaptic potential toward triggering an action potential). N₂O BLOCKS the channel, so blocked NMDA receptors prevent that depolarisation. Answer: the letter for postsynaptic + depolarisation prevented (D). → `bio-b04` (synapse), `bio-d02` (cellular signalling). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** placing the receptor presynaptically because "glutamate is released there" (it is released FROM there, but acts on the far side); picking hyperpolarisation — N₂O does not make the cell more negative, it just blocks the positive swing.
**Points:** 2 (all-or-nothing letter).

## Question 30 (official paper)

List, in the right order, the blood vessels a N₂O molecule passes on the shortest route from uptake (lungs) to effect (brain): aorta, jugular vein, carotid artery, pulmonary vein, pulmonary artery, inferior vena cava.

**Solve:** Inhaled drug enters the blood in the lung capillaries → leaves the lungs via the **pulmonary vein** (longader) · left atrium/ventricle (vessels not listed) → **aorta** → up to the head in the **carotid artery** (halsslagader). Numbers: **4 – 1 – 3**. Veins leaving the head and the lung ARTERY (which carries blood TO the lungs) have no place on this ride. → `bio-b03` (circulation). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** inserting the pulmonary artery — that is the route INTO the lungs, not out; choosing the jugular vein (drains the head back to the heart); adding the vena cava as if the molecule must first return to the right heart.
**Points:** 1 (full correct sequence).

## Question 31 (official paper, mc)

Breathing in and out of a balloon drops lung pO₂ to about 6 kPa and raises lung pCO₂. What does the higher pCO₂ do to the oxygen-saturation curve of haemoglobin, and does the amount of O₂ bound in the lung capillaries then fall or rise?

**Solve:** Higher pCO₂ lowers the pH → by the Bohr effect the oxygen-saturation curve shifts to the RIGHT: haemoglobin needs a higher pO₂ for the same saturation. Read the consequence at the (already low) lung pO₂: at the same pO₂ a right-shifted curve means LOWER saturation → less oxygen is bound in the lung capillaries → it decreases. Answer: the letter for right + decreases (C). → `bio-b03` (gas transport). BINAS: the oxygen-saturation curve of haemoglobin.
**Common mistakes:** knowing the Bohr shift but drawing the wrong conclusion (a right shift helps UNLOAD in tissues — in the lungs it hurts LOADING); reasoning "more CO₂, more breathing, therefore more O₂ bound" — the question is about the curve, not ventilation.
**Points:** 2.

## Question 32 (official paper, mc)

Where is the respiratory centre (ademcentrum), and in which vessels sit the chemoreceptors of the breathing reflex?

**Solve:** The respiratory centre is in the medulla oblongata — the medulla belongs to the **brainstem** (hersenstam), not the cerebral cortex. The breathing reflex is driven by chemoreceptors sensing pCO₂/H⁺ (and pO₂) in the **aorta (aortic bodies) and the carotid arteries (carotid bodies)**. Answer: brainstem + aorta and carotids (D). → `bio-b04` (regulation of breathing), `bio-b03`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** placing the centre in the cortex because breathing can be influenced voluntarily (voluntary override does not house the centre); picking the pulmonary veins — gas sensors must sample ARTERIAL blood to steer ventilation.
**Points:** 2.

## Question 33 (official paper, mc)

Myelin sheaths occur in which part of the nervous system — only central, only peripheral, or both?

**Solve:** Both. Myelin is made by oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and by Schwann cells (cellen van Schwann) in the peripheral nervous system; the story itself repeatedly ties Schwann cells to myelin in a leg nerve → letter **C**. → `bio-b04`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reasoning "brain = central, so myelin is central" (the brain's white matter is myelinated, but so are peripheral nerves — how else would leg reflexes be slowed by myelin damage?).
**Points:** 1.

## Question 34 (official paper)

With two sensors on a leg nerve, Lia's impulses arrive later than in a healthy person. Explain why damaged myelin sheaths slow impulse conduction.

**Solve:** Healthy myelin insulates the axon so that action potentials are effectively regenerated ONLY at the nodes of Ranvier — the impulse jumps node to node (saltatory, sprongsgewijze conduction), which is fast. With the sheaths damaged, the bare stretches must conduct the action potential continuously: the jumps get shorter (the distance between functioning nodes becomes effectively larger), so conduction slows down. → `bio-b04`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** claiming fewer neurotransmitters are released (conduction speed is about the axon membrane, not the synapse); saying the myelin "carries the impulse" itself.
**Points:** 1 (the saltatory-conduction argument).

## Question 35 (official paper)

In the suspected Guillain–Barré reaction, antibodies lead to Schwann cells being broken down by macrophages. Explain how antibodies and macrophages together destroy the Schwann cells.

**Solve:** Two marked links. (1) The body's antibodies bind to the antigens on the surface of the Schwann cells — labelling (opsonising) them. (2) The macrophage has receptors for the Fc tail of those antibodies: it docks onto the antibody-coated Schwann cell and phagocytoses/destroys it. → `bio-b05` (specific + non-specific defence cooperating). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** having the macrophage eat "any cell because it is a phagocyte" without the antibody tag; letting the ANTIBODIES do the killing (they only mark — the macrophage executes).
**Points:** 2 (antibody tagging + macrophage binding/eating the tagged cell).

## Question 36 (official paper, mc)

IVIg treatment inhibits production of the cytokine IL-2 by T-helper cells; IL-2 stimulates among others T-helper cells themselves. Which process does the treatment prevent: antigen presentation by T-helper cells, antibody production by T-helper cells, or cloning and differentiation of T-helper cells?

**Solve:** IL-2 is the growth signal that makes an activated T-helper cell multiply (clonal expansion) and specialise. Cut its production and that cloning/differentiation stops → letter **C**. The decoys fail on function: T-helper cells do not present antigen (APCs such as macrophages/dendritic cells do) and do not make antibodies (B cells/plasma cells do). → `bio-b05`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** choosing antibody production because "IL-2 stimulates the immune response" — know which cell does what; thinking helper cells present antigen because their name says "help".
**Points:** 1.

## Question 37 (official paper)

Match each protein property to the right protein of vitamin-B12 uptake: haptocorrine, intrinsic factor, or animal protein from food. Properties: 1 broken down only by pancreatic proteases; 2 broken down by both stomach and pancreatic proteases; 3 resistant to both stomach and pancreatic proteases.

**Solve:** Follow the route. Haptocorrine ferries B12 through the ACID stomach and must survive there, but in the small intestine it has to HAND OVER B12 to intrinsic factor — so it is destroyed once the pancreatic proteases arrive: 1 = haptocorrine. The animal protein in which B12 arrives from food is digested like any food protein, starting in the stomach and finishing with the pancreas: 2 = animal protein. Intrinsic factor must escort B12 all the way to the ileum receptors: it must resist both protease sets: 3 = intrinsic factor. → `bio-b03` (digestion/absorption logistics). BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** assuming the carrier of the stomach phase must already be digestible there (haptocorrine survives the stomach, dies in the intestine); making intrinsic factor stomach-sensitive — then nothing would deliver B12.
**Points:** 1 (all three matches).

## Question 38 (official paper, mc)

Blood tests cannot tell functional from non-functional B12, so the deficit is shown indirectly: two B12 forms act as cofactor in two enzymatic conversions. Which blood values of methylmalonic acid and homocysteine point to a functional B12 deficit?

**Solve:** B12 is the COFACTOR for the enzymes that CONVERT methylmalonyl-CoA (→ succinyl-CoA) and homocysteine (→ methionine). No functional B12 → both conversions stall → both SUBSTRATES pile up in the blood: methylmalonic acid raised AND homocysteine raised → letter **A**. → `bio-b02` (enzymes/cofactors), `bio-d01`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** reasoning "less enzyme activity → less product, so lower values" — the blood measures the SUBSTRATES that cannot be processed; mixing up cofactor with substrate.
**Points:** 2.

## Question 39 (official paper)

Why is a B12 injection preferable to a B12 pill for Lia?

**Solve:** The pill depends on the whole fickle uptake machinery (intrinsic factor-mediated absorption in the ileum): much of an oral dose is never absorbed and ends up in the faeces — especially useless when intrinsic factor is lacking. An injection puts B12 directly into the blood in the right form, fast and independent of the gut. Any one such argument earns the point. Note what does NOT: claiming the pill's B12 is digested or destroyed by stomach acid — B12 survives the stomach fine (the uptake route, not breakdown, is the bottleneck), and that claim explicitly scores 0. → `bio-b03`. BINAS: none.
**Common mistakes:** the digestion/stomach-acid claim above; saying "a pill acts too slowly altogether" without any absorption argument.
**Points:** 1.
