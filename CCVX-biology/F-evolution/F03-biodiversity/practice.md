### bio-f03-q01 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A region in the east of the Netherlands contains the following:

1. forty old varieties of apple, kept in one collection orchard;
2. a flower-rich hay meadow with 26 plant species and 120 recorded insect species;
3. a landscape of dunes, salt marshes, deciduous woodland and heathland.

Biodiversity can be studied at three levels of organisation: genes, species and ecosystems. Give for each of the three examples the level at which it describes biodiversity.

#### Answer

1. genetic (gene) diversity, 2. species diversity, 3. ecosystem diversity.

#### Solution

1. genetic (gene) diversity — variety of alleles within one species; 2. species diversity — number and spread of species in an area; 3. ecosystem diversity — variety of ecosystems in a region.
*Marking:* • example 1 = genetic diversity (1) • example 2 = species diversity (1) • example 3 = ecosystem diversity (1)

### bio-f03-q02 | points 4 | outcome F3.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Two heathland meadows of equal size are surveyed. Meadow P received fertiliser every year; meadow Q was never fertilised. The table gives the number of individual plants counted per species.

| Plant species | Meadow P | Meadow Q |
|---|---|---|
| red fescue | 340 | 70 |
| common bent | 45 | 75 |
| sheep's sorrel | 15 | 55 |
| heath bedstraw | 0 | 60 |
| tormentil | 0 | 50 |
| lousewort | 0 | 45 |
| yellow-rattle | 0 | 45 |

Calculate the species richness (number of species) of each meadow, and argue which meadow has the greater species diversity, using the evenness of the numbers as well.

#### Answer

P: 3 species; Q: 7 species.

#### Solution

P: 3 species; Q: 7 species. Both meadows contain 400 individuals (P: 340 + 45 + 15; Q: 70 + 75 + 55 + 60 + 50 + 45 + 45), so the numbers are directly comparable. In P one species dominates with 340/400 = 85% of all individuals, while in Q the commonest species is only 75/400 ≈ 19% and the individuals are spread much more evenly over the species. Species diversity is about both the number of species (richness) and how evenly the individuals are spread over them (evenness): on both criteria meadow Q is the more diverse.
*Marking:* • richness 3 vs 7 (1) • both meadows 400 individuals (1) • dominance/evenness compared with a percentage or fraction (1) • conclusion Q, because species diversity includes evenness (1)

### bio-f03-q03 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

Breeders keep gene banks with many wild potato species and old, low-yielding potato varieties, although farmers no longer grow them.

Explain why maintaining this genetic diversity is useful for future potato cultivation.

#### Answer

The gene bank preserves alleles that modern varieties may lack, for example for resistance to diseases.

#### Solution

The gene bank preserves alleles that modern varieties may lack, for example for resistance to diseases such as potato blight or for tolerance to drought. If a new disease or a changed climate appears, breeders can cross these alleles into cultivated potatoes; a genetically uniform crop, like the Irish potato crop of the 1840s, can be destroyed by a single disease.
*Marking:* • gene bank = reservoir of alleles/genetic variation (1) • usable to adapt the crop to new diseases or changed conditions (1)

### bio-f03-q04 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

An apple grower has an orchard of 25 hectares. With free pollination by wild insects (solitary bees, bumblebees, hoverflies) the harvest is worth €18 000 per hectare per year. Without wild pollinators the grower would have to rent bee colonies: 4 colonies per hectare at €90 per colony per year, and even then the income per hectare would be 15% lower.

Calculate the yearly value of the free pollination service that the wild insects provide to this grower (rent saved plus income preserved).

#### Answer

Rent: 25 × 4 × €90 = €9 000 per year; lost income: 15% × 25 × €18 000 = €67 500; total value of the free service: €9 000 + €67 500 = **€76 500 per year**.

#### Solution

Rent: 25 × 4 × €90 = €9 000 per year. Income with wild pollinators: 25 × €18 000 = €450 000; income loss without them: 15% × €450 000 = €67 500. Total yearly value of the free service: €9 000 + €67 500 = **€76 500 per year**.
*Marking:* • rent calculation €9 000 (1) • income loss 15% of €450 000 = €67 500 (1) • total €76 500 per year (1)

### bio-f03-q05 | points 4 | outcome F3.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Besides food supply and pollination, humans depend on biodiversity for other ecosystem services.

Name two other ecosystem services from the CCVX list (pharmaceuticals, raw materials and fuels, water treatment, decomposition of toxins, a stable climate), and explain for each how organisms or ecosystems provide it.

#### Answer

Example: water treatment — in sewage plants and along reed beds, bacteria break down organic waste and convert nitrogen compounds.

#### Solution

Example: water treatment — in sewage plants and along reed beds, bacteria break down organic waste and convert nitrogen compounds (nitrification and denitrification), and plants take up nitrate and phosphate. A stable climate — forests, peat bogs and phytoplankton take up CO₂ by photosynthesis and store carbon in biomass and soil; oceans perform about half of all photosynthesis. (Also correct: pharmaceuticals — moulds and plants produce active substances such as penicillin; raw materials and fuels — wood, cotton, rubber, firewood and biofuels are made by organisms; decomposition of toxins — bacteria and fungi break down oil and pesticides.)
*Marking:* • first service correctly named (1) • mechanism correctly explained (1) • second service correctly named (1) • mechanism correctly explained (1)

### bio-f03-q06 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.2 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A water board plants reed beds along polluted ditches to improve the water quality before the water reaches a nature reserve.

Explain, naming the organisms and processes involved, how such a reed bed cleans the water.

#### Answer

The reed plants take up nitrate and phosphate from the water as nutrients for growth.

#### Solution

The reed plants take up nitrate and phosphate from the water as nutrients for growth. Around their roots and in the sediment, bacteria break down organic waste (decomposition) and convert nitrogen compounds: nitrification of ammonium to nitrate, and denitrification of nitrate to harmless N₂ gas. Together this removes nutrients and organic pollution.
*Marking:* • nutrient uptake by the plants (1) • breakdown/conversion by bacteria (decomposition, nitrification/denitrification) (1)

### bio-f03-q07 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.2 | difficulty easy

#### Question

Peat bogs and old forests store large amounts of carbon in their soil and biomass.

Explain how the loss of these ecosystems strengthens the intensified (enhanced) greenhouse effect. Give the two ways in which it does so.

#### Answer

The stored carbon is released as CO₂ when peat dries out or forest is cleared and burned or decomposes.

#### Solution

The stored carbon is released as CO₂ when peat dries out or forest is cleared and burned or decomposes. At the same time the CO₂ uptake by photosynthesis disappears, so less CO₂ is removed from the air. Both raise the atmospheric CO₂ concentration and thus the retention of heat radiation.
*Marking:* • stored carbon released as CO₂ (1) • CO₂ uptake by photosynthesis disappears (1)

### bio-f03-q08 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The moorland dormouse, a red-list mammal, lives only in the interior of heathland: it avoids the outer 100 m of every patch (the edge zone with farmland disturbances). A province must choose between two reserve designs with the same total area of 1 200 ha:

- design A: one connected rectangle of 2 km × 6 km;
- design B: three separate squares of 2 km × 2 km, divided by roads and farmland.

Calculate the total area of core habitat (the area more than 100 m from the edge) in each design.

#### Answer

Design A: core = (2 000 − 200) m × (6 000 − 200) m = 1 800 × 5 800 = 10 440 000 m² = 1 044 ha.

#### Solution

Design A: core = (2 000 − 200) m × (6 000 − 200) m = 1 800 × 5 800 = 10 440 000 m² = 1 044 ha. Design B: core per patch = (2 000 − 200) × (2 000 − 200) = 1 800 × 1 800 = 3 240 000 m² = 324 ha; for three patches: 3 × 324 = 972 ha. So with the same total reserve area, design A offers 1 044 − 972 = 72 ha more core habitat.
*Marking:* • subtraction of 200 m from each dimension (edge on both sides) (1) • A = 1 044 ha (1) • B = 324 ha per patch, 972 ha total (1)

### bio-f03-q09 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

For the same moorland dormouse as in the previous question: a breeding pair needs 2 ha of core habitat.

Calculate the maximum number of breeding pairs each design can support.

#### Answer

Design A: 1 044 / 2 = 522 pairs.

#### Solution

Design A: 1 044 / 2 = 522 pairs. Design B: 972 / 2 = 486 pairs (162 pairs per patch). Design A supports 36 more pairs; in design B the pairs are split into three isolated sub-populations of at most 162 pairs.
*Marking:* • A = 522 pairs (1) • B = 486 pairs (1)

### bio-f03-q10 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Using the data of the previous two questions, argue that design A is better for the survival of the moorland dormouse than design B. Give two arguments in addition to the core-area calculation.

#### Answer

Design A holds one continuous population instead of three isolated sub-populations, so animals can disperse and exchange individuals.

#### Solution

Design A holds one continuous population instead of three isolated sub-populations, so animals can disperse and exchange individuals: gene flow keeps the gene pool varied and prevents inbreeding and the loss of alleles by genetic drift in small groups. If a sub-population in one patch of design B dies out (for example after a fire or disease), it can hardly be recolonised, because the roads are a barrier and crossing animals are killed by traffic. A larger continuous population also fluctuates less and dies out less easily than three small ones.
*Marking:* • dispersal/exchange between sub-populations, so gene flow is maintained (1) • less inbreeding/genetic drift, or recolonisation possible after local extinction (1) • roads in B: barrier effect and/or traffic casualties (1)

### bio-f03-q11 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A heathland reserve lies next to intensive livestock farming. Measurements show a high nitrogen deposition. After ten years, the once species-rich heathland has become much poorer in species.

Describe the chain of events by which eutrophication leads to this loss of biodiversity.

#### Answer

The nitrogen input raises the nutrient level of the originally nutrient-poor soil.

#### Solution

The nitrogen input raises the nutrient level of the originally nutrient-poor soil. A few fast-growing species, such as grasses, profit strongly and outcompete the slow-growing plants adapted to poor soil (heather and other heathland plants), which disappear. The insects and other animals specialised on those plants lose their food and habitat and disappear as well: the result is few species with many individuals each, instead of many species — a lower species diversity.
*Marking:* • nitrogen raises soil fertility (1) • fast growers outcompete the plants of nutrient-poor soil, which disappear (1) • dependent (insect) species also disappear, so species diversity falls (1)

### bio-f03-q12 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty easy

#### Question

Name two measures the reserve managers can take against the eutrophication of the heathland from the previous question, and explain how each measure works.

#### Answer

Sod cutting: the nutrient-rich top layer of soil is removed, so the soil becomes poor in nutrients again.

#### Solution

Any two of: sod cutting — the nutrient-rich top layer of soil is removed, so the soil becomes poor in nutrients again; mowing and removing the cuttings — nitrogen accumulated in plant material is exported from the area instead of returning to the soil; grazing by sheep or cattle with removal of the manure — nutrients leave the area; buffer zones with less fertilisation/livestock around the reserve — the nitrogen input itself is reduced; lowering the groundwater is reversed (higher water tables) — less nitrogen is processed/mineralised into available forms. ### bio-f03-q13 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty easy

#### Question

In heathland pools, researchers monitor the small emerald damselfly, a species that occurs only in weakly acidic, nutrient-poor water and disappears quickly when the water becomes more alkaline or nutrient-rich.

Give the term for a species used in this way, and explain why this kind of species is so suitable for it.

#### Answer

An indicator species.

#### Solution

An indicator species. Such a species has a narrow tolerance range (it occurs only within narrow limits of, for example, acidity and nutrient level), so it reacts early and visibly when its environment changes. From its presence or absence researchers can judge the quality of the environment without measuring everything themselves.
*Marking:* • indicator species (1) • narrow tolerance range, reacts early, reveals the environmental quality (1)

### bio-f03-q14 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The mountain plant glacier saxifrage grows in a mountain range between 1 200 and 2 000 m altitude; above 2 000 m there is only bare rock. As a result of the intensified greenhouse effect, the lower limit of its distribution area shifts upward by 300 m this century; the upper limit cannot shift.

Calculate by what percentage the elevation range of its distribution area shrinks, and explain what this means for the survival of this species in this mountain range if the warming continues.

#### Answer

The range shrinks from 2 000 − 1 200 = 800 m to 2 000 − 1 500 = 500 m.

#### Solution

The range shrinks from 2 000 − 1 200 = 800 m to 2 000 − 1 500 = 500 m. Loss: 300/800 × 100% = 37.5% of the elevation range (and roughly of the habitat). If warming continues, the lower limit keeps rising while the upper limit is fixed by the mountain tops, so the distribution area keeps shrinking towards zero: the population becomes small and vulnerable, and the species can disappear from this mountain range entirely — it cannot "move" beyond the tops.
*Marking:* • new range 1 500–2 000 m = 500 m, old range 800 m (1) • percentage loss 300/800 = 37.5% (1) • explanation: fixed upper limit, so continued warming shrinks the area to (near) zero → extinction risk (1)

### bio-f03-q15 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty easy

#### Question

The golden fan worm is a marine animal from the Pacific Ocean that reached the Eastern Scheldt in the ballast water of ships. It has no native population in Europe. Within eight years it spread over large parts of the estuary, forming dense reefs that overgrow native mussel and oyster beds.

Distinguish between the terms indigenous species, introduced (exotic) species and invasive species, and state to which category the golden fan worm in the Eastern Scheldt belongs.

#### Answer

An indigenous species occurs naturally in an area, without human help.

#### Solution

An indigenous species occurs naturally in an area, without human help. An introduced (exotic) species was brought by humans from outside its natural distribution area. An invasive species is an exotic that spreads rapidly in the new area and displaces or harms native species (or the economy). The golden fan worm is an introduced species that is invasive in the Eastern Scheldt: human transport brought it in, and it spreads fast at the expense of native mussels and oysters.
*Marking:* • indigenous = occurs naturally/without humans (1) • exotic = introduced by humans from outside the natural range (1) • invasive = spreading fast and harmful; fan worm correctly placed as invasive exotic (1)

### bio-f03-q16 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

For the golden fan worm of the previous question, explain two reasons why introduced species can sometimes spread explosively in a new ecosystem, and name one measure that prevents new introductions of marine species.

#### Answer

Its natural enemies and diseases from its native range are absent, so far fewer individuals are eaten or killed.

#### Solution

Its natural enemies and diseases from its native range are absent, so far fewer individuals are eaten or killed (enemy release). And it reproduces and disperses fast: its planktonic larvae drift with the currents, and the hard substrate niche (mussel and oyster beds, harbour walls) offers space with few native competitors. Prevention: treating or exchanging ballast water of ships (and cleaning ship hulls), so no larvae or organisms are carried along.
*Marking:* • absence of natural enemies/diseases (1) • fast reproduction/dispersal and/or free niche with little competition (1) • ballast-water treatment or hull cleaning as prevention (1)

### bio-f03-q17 | points 4 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty hard

#### Question

To control the golden fan worm, two measures are proposed: (1) teams of divers mechanically remove the reefs; (2) a Pacific starfish that eats the fan worm is introduced.

Assess both measures: give one advantage and one disadvantage or risk of each for the Eastern Scheldt ecosystem.

#### Answer

Mechanical removal: advantage — it reduces the fan worm directly, without introducing any new species.

#### Solution

Mechanical removal: advantage — it reduces the fan worm directly, without introducing any new species. Disadvantage — it is very costly and must be repeated constantly, because remaining fragments and new larvae regrow, and other bottom life is disturbed along with the reefs. Starfish introduction: advantage — once established, the predator suppresses the fan worm continuously and everywhere, without repeated human work. Disadvantage/risk — the starfish is itself a new exotic: it may also eat native bivalves or have unforeseen effects on the food web, and an introduced predator can never be taken back.
*Marking:* • advantage of removal (1) • disadvantage of removal (1) • advantage of the predator (1) • risk of introducing a new exotic (1)

### bio-f03-q19 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

For a rare Dutch mammal species, a zoo breeding programme with an international studbook is proposed, aiming at later reintroduction.

Give one advantage and one disadvantage of conserving a species through such a breeding programme compared with protecting and restoring its habitat.

#### Answer

Advantage: the animals are protected from threats in the wild and the population can be built up under controlled conditions.

#### Solution

Advantage: the animals are protected from threats in the wild and the population can be built up under controlled conditions, ready for later reintroduction. Disadvantage: the captive population descends from few founders, so the gene pool stays small, with risks of inbreeding and loss of genetic variation (and adaptation to captivity); moreover the cause of the decline is not removed — without a protected, suitable habitat, reintroduction is pointless. The studbook (family-tree pedigree) is used to plan pairings that minimise inbreeding.
*Marking:* • advantage, e.g. safe and rapid build-up for reintroduction (1) • disadvantage, e.g. small gene pool/inbreeding, or habitat problem remains (1)

### bio-f03-q20 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

For four mammal species V, W, X and Y, researchers compare the same mitochondrial gene. The table gives the number of base-pair differences per 1 000 base pairs for each pair of species.

| Pair | Differences per 1 000 bp |
|---|---|
| V – W | 8 |
| V – X | 21 |
| V – Y | 22 |
| W – X | 19 |
| W – Y | 20 |
| X – Y | 4 |

Which two species are most closely related, and which pair is next? Explain your answer using the concept of kinship.

#### Answer

X and Y are most closely related (only 4 differences).

#### Solution

X and Y are most closely related (only 4 differences); then V and W (8 differences). ### bio-f03-q21 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The tuatara of New Zealand is the last surviving species of an ancient reptile group (order) whose other members are all extinct. A second reptile species, by contrast, has nineteen close relatives within its own genus.

Explain why the extinction of the tuatara would be a greater loss of biodiversity than the extinction of the common relative, using the concept of the pedigree (phylogenetic tree).

#### Answer

In the pedigree (phylogenetic tree) of the reptiles, the tuatara forms the last branch of an entire old lineage.

#### Solution

In the pedigree (phylogenetic tree) of the reptiles, the tuatara forms the last branch of an entire old lineage. If it dies out, that whole branch of the tree is lost, with its unique combination of heritable traits and its long independent evolutionary history. The common relative shares most of its traits and DNA with its nineteen close relatives, so after its loss that information is largely preserved in them: only a small twig of the tree is cut off.
*Marking:* • tuatara = last branch of a whole lineage, so more unique heritage is lost (1) • the relative's traits/DNA largely persist in its close relatives (1)

### bio-f03-q22 | points 4 | outcome F3.1.4 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A predatory bird species is introduced on an island with a skink population. The bird mainly catches skinks that bask in the open. Researchers measure the skinks every five years:

| Measurement | Year 0 | Year 5 | Year 10 | Year 15 | Year 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mean sprint speed (m/s) | 2.6 | 2.7 | 2.9 | 3.1 | 3.2 |

Explain, step by step, with the concept of natural selection how this change in the population came about.

#### Answer

The original population already contained heritable variation in sprint speed.

#### Solution

The original population already contained heritable variation in sprint speed (through mutation and recombination). The bird acted as a new selection pressure: slower skinks were caught more often, faster ones survived more and produced more offspring (higher fitness). The alleles for greater speed therefore became more common in each next generation, so the mean sprint speed of the population rose — an adaptation. Note that the population changed over generations; individual skinks did not adapt, and the underlying mutations arose at random, not because of the predator.
*Marking:* • heritable variation already present (1) • differential survival/reproduction: fast individuals survive the predator more (1) • alleles for speed become more common over generations (1) • it is the population that changes; mutations arise at random (1)

### bio-f03-q23 | points 2 | outcome F3.1.4 | difficulty easy

#### Question

The BINAS table of the geological timescale shows a sharp drop in biodiversity at the end of the Cretaceous period, about 66 million years ago.

Name this event and explain how biodiversity could recover afterwards, many millions of years later.

#### Answer

The mass extinction at the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary, in which the dinosaurs (except birds) and many other groups died out.

#### Solution

The mass extinction at the Cretaceous–Palaeogene boundary, in which the dinosaurs (except birds) and many other groups died out. The surviving species found many empty niches; over millions of years new species arose from them by speciation (adaptive radiation), for example the radiation of the mammals, so biodiversity increased again — but always starting from the survivors, never bringing back the extinct lineages.
*Marking:* • end-Cretaceous mass extinction correctly named/described (1) • recovery by speciation of survivors into empty niches (1)

### bio-f03-q24 | points 3 | outcome F3.1.4 | difficulty hard

#### Question

In a discussion evening, a writer defends the statement: "Extinction is not a problem. Extinction has always happened — more than 99% of all species that ever lived are extinct."

Give one argument in support of this statement from the history of life on Earth, and two arguments against it. Do not give your personal opinion.

#### Answer

In support: extinction is a natural process that has always occurred and most species that ever lived are indeed extinct.

#### Solution

In support: extinction is a natural process that has always occurred and most species that ever lived are indeed extinct. Against: (1) species now disappear many times faster than the natural background rate, mainly through human activities, so today's wave of extinction is not business as usual but resembles a new mass extinction; (2) humans depend on biodiversity for food, medicines, raw materials, water treatment, the breakdown of toxins and a stable climate, and losses also affect other species through food webs. (Whether species also have value in themselves is a normative question, not a scientific argument either way.)
*Marking:* • supporting argument: extinction is natural/always happened (1) • rate now far above background, human-caused (1) • human dependence on ecosystem services and/or knock-on effects through food webs (1)
