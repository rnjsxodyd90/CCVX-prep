## 1. Levels of biodiversity

- Biodiversity (*biodiversiteit*) is the variety of life, and it can be studied at three levels of organisation: genes within a species, species within an ecosystem, and ecosystems within a region. *(overlap: CvTE P3.2 spec 1)*
- Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles within a species or population, its gene pool (*genenpool*), seen for example in the many varieties of potato, apple or dog; it lets a population adapt to change. *(overlap: CvTE M8.4 spec 1)*
- The Irish potato famine of 1845–1849 was so severe partly because most potatoes belonged to one genetically uniform variety, which potato blight (*aardappelziekte*) destroyed.
- Species diversity is the number of species in an area and how evenly the individuals are spread over them. Species composition (*soortensamenstelling*) is which species occur and how many of each: a wood with ten tree species in equal numbers is more diverse than a wood with the same ten species in which one makes up 91% of the trees. *(overlap: CvTE P1.3 spec 1)*
- A species (*soort*) is a group of individuals that can interbreed in nature and produce fertile offspring; horse and donkey are different species because their offspring, the mule, is infertile. See [F02](../F02-speciation/README.md). *(overlap: CvTE P4.1 spec 1)*
- Ecosystem diversity is the variety of ecosystems in a region, such as the dunes, salt marshes, heathland, raised bogs, forests and lakes of the Netherlands, each with its own species composition.
- Indigenous species (*inheemse soorten*) live in an area naturally, without human help, such as the pedunculate oak (*zomereik*) and the badger (*das*) in the Netherlands.
- Introduced (exotic) species (*exoten*) were brought by people from outside their natural range, such as potatoes and maize; invasive species (*invasieve soorten*) are the exotics that spread fast and harm native species or the economy, such as Japanese knotweed (*Japanse duizendknoop*). *(overlap: CvTE P1.3 spec 5)*
- The distribution area (*verspreidingsgebied*) is the geographical area where a species occurs, set by its tolerances, the barriers to its dispersal and its history; a species with a small distribution area, such as one found on a single island, runs a higher risk of extinction.
- Indicator species (*indicatorsoorten*) are used to monitor biodiversity and the environment, because their narrow tolerances make them react early: stonefly larvae disappear from polluted streams and many lichens from polluted air. See [C03](../../C-self-organization/C03-self-organization-of-ecosystems/README.md). *(overlap: CvTE P1.3 spec 2)*
- Pedigree (*stamboom*): here CCVX means a phylogenetic tree, a branching diagram of how species descend from common ancestors, which the Dutch example contexts call *fylogenetische stambomen* (CvTE appendix 4). At this level biodiversity is the variety of lineages: the tuatara of New Zealand is the last survivor of an old reptile group, so its loss would cut off a whole branch. *(overlap: CvTE P4.4 spec 2)*
- Kinship (*verwantschap*) is how closely species are related, that is, how recently they shared a common ancestor. It is measured by comparing characteristics and DNA: the fewer the differences in DNA, the closer the kinship, which shows for example that chimpanzees and bonobos are our closest living relatives. *(overlap: CvTE M8.1 spec 3)*
- **Trap:** In genetics a pedigree is a family tree of individuals (also *stamboom*), which zoo breeding programmes use to avoid inbreeding; in F3 the word means a tree of species.

## 2. How humans depend on biodiversity

- People depend on what organisms and ecosystems provide, called ecosystem services (*ecosysteemdiensten*); the six that CCVX names follow.
- Food supply (*voedselvoorziening*): all crops and livestock descend from wild species, and wild relatives supply genes for breeding, such as resistance to potato blight from wild potato species.
- Pollinating insects such as bees and hoverflies are needed for many fruit crops, such as apples, pears and strawberries, and much of our fish comes from wild marine food webs.
- Production of pharmaceuticals (*geneesmiddelen*): many medicines come from organisms, such as penicillin from the mould Penicillium, and aspirin, which was developed from a substance in willow bark. A species that dies out can take undiscovered medicines with it.
- Raw material and fuel production (*grondstoffen en brandstoffen*): wood, paper, cotton, wool and natural rubber come from organisms, and so do fuels: firewood, biofuels and, from organisms of long ago, coal, oil and natural gas. *(overlap: CvTE P1.1 spec 4)*
- Water treatment (*waterzuivering*): in sewage treatment plants bacteria break down organic waste and remove nitrogen by nitrification and denitrification ([B08](../../B-self-regulation/B08-regulation-of-ecosystems/README.md)); reed beds and wetlands filter nutrients out of water; and in the Dutch dunes, river water that seeps through the sand to become drinking water is cleaned partly by soil organisms.
- Decomposition of toxins (*afbraak van gifstoffen*): bacteria and fungi break down many harmful substances, such as oil after a spill or pesticides in soil, and this is used on purpose to clean polluted soil (bioremediation).
- Contributing to a stable climate (*stabiel klimaat*): forests, peat bogs and phytoplankton take up CO₂ by photosynthesis and store carbon, and roughly half of all photosynthesis on Earth happens in the oceans. Forests also evaporate water that falls as rain further inland. *(overlap: CvTE P1.2 spec 4)*
- Losing these ecosystems releases their stored carbon and strengthens the greenhouse effect; see B08.
- **Trap:** Decomposition only works for biodegradable substances; persistent ones such as PCBs and PFAS are hardly broken down, so ecosystems can't clean them up.

## 3. Threats, the role of humans and conservation

- The main threats to biodiversity today come from human activity: habitat loss and fragmentation, eutrophication, climate change, introduced species, overexploitation and pollution. *(overlap: CvTE P1.2 spec 5)*
- Habitat loss is the largest threat on land: forests, wetlands and grasslands become farmland, towns and roads, as when tropical forest is cleared for soya, palm oil and cattle.
- Fragmentation (*versnippering*) splits a habitat into small, isolated patches: populations become small, lose genetic variation through inbreeding and genetic drift, and die out more easily, and animals that cross roads are killed. Island theory predicts fewer species in small, isolated patches (see B08). *(overlap: CvTE P1.3 spec 3)*
- Eutrophication (*eutrofiering*) from fertiliser, manure and nitrogen deposition favours a few fast-growing species and pushes out the many species of nutrient-poor places, such as heathland plants and the insects that depend on them; the steps are in B08. *(overlap: CvTE P1.2 spec 4)*
- The intensified greenhouse effect (*versterkt broeikaseffect*), CCVX's term for the enhanced greenhouse effect, warms the climate: distribution areas shift towards the poles, species of cold places such as mountaintops and the Arctic sea ice (the polar bear) lose their habitat, and corals bleach. *(overlap: CvTE P2.1 spec 4)*
- Introduced and invasive species can outcompete, eat or infect native species: the brown tree snake, brought to Guam by accident, wiped out most of the island's native forest birds, and a skin fungus probably brought from Asia with the pet trade has almost wiped out the fire salamander (*vuursalamander*) in South Limburg. *(overlap: CvTE P1.3 spec 5)*
- Overexploitation, such as overfishing, hunting and poaching for rhino horn and ivory, takes animals faster than they reproduce.
- Pollution includes pesticides, persistent toxins that accumulate in food chains (see [D05](../../D-interaction/D05-interaction-in-ecosystems/README.md)) and plastic.
- The red list (*Rode Lijst*): the Red List of the IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature) assesses each species' risk of extinction with criteria such as population size, population decline and the size of the distribution area.
- Its categories run from Least Concern via Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered to Extinct in the Wild and Extinct, plus Data Deficient when there is too little information; Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered together count as threatened. Countries, the Netherlands included, also make national red lists.
- Conserving species starts with their habitats: nature reserves and the European Natura 2000 network protect them, and corridors, wildlife overpasses (*ecoducten*) and fish ladders (*vistrappen*) connect them.
- Restoring the abiotic conditions (less nitrogen, higher water levels) and nature management (*natuurbeheer*) by mowing, grazing and sod cutting keep the habitats suitable.
- Invasive species are controlled, and new introductions prevented, for example by treating the ballast water of ships.
- Trade and harvest are regulated: the CITES treaty controls international trade in endangered species, and quotas limit fishing.
- Species are also conserved outside the wild and returned to it: zoo breeding programmes, seed banks such as the Svalbard Global Seed Vault, and reintroductions such as the beavers released in the Biesbosch in 1988.
- **Trap:** A red list is an assessment of extinction risk, not a law; legal protection comes from separate laws and treaties.

## 4. Changes in biodiversity over time

- Biodiversity changes as new species arise and others die out, and the balance between the two has shifted many times in Earth's history. *(overlap: CvTE P4.3 spec 1)*
- Natural selection (*natuurlijke selectie*): individuals differ genetically through mutation and recombination; those whose heritable traits suit the environment survive and reproduce more (higher fitness), so their alleles become more common in the next generation. See [F01](../F01-selection/README.md) and F02.
- Adaptation (*adaptatie*) is both a heritable trait that improves survival and reproduction in a particular environment and the process by which a population acquires it: in industrial England the dark form of the peppered moth became common on soot-blackened trees, where birds saw it less, and rare again after the air was cleaned.
- When populations of one species adapt to different environments and stop interbreeding, they can become separate species: on the Galápagos Islands one ancestral finch species gave rise to many species with beaks suited to different foods. *(overlap: CvTE P4.4 spec 1)*
- Extinction (*uitsterven*) happens when a species can't adapt fast enough to a change, such as a new predator, a new disease or another climate; it is often estimated that more than 99% of all species that ever lived are extinct.
- Five mass extinctions cut biodiversity sharply, the largest at the end of the Permian, about 252 million years ago, and the best known at the end of the Cretaceous, about 66 million years ago, when the dinosaurs other than birds died out; see the Binas table of the geological timescale.
- After each mass extinction the survivors diversified again over millions of years, as mammals did after the dinosaurs.
- Today species are disappearing many times faster than the natural background rate, mainly through human activity, which is why scientists speak of a possible sixth mass extinction.
- Phylogenetic trees (pedigrees) and DNA-based kinship show this history: the branching points are speciations, and branches that stop are extinct lineages.
- **Trap:** Individuals don't adapt through natural selection; populations change over generations. Mutations arise at random, not because the organism needs them.

## Specimen contexts

- An objection to a construction project that would harm the habitat of an endangered mammal (sustainability): the objection can argue that the species is on the red list, that the project destroys and fragments its habitat so that the remaining populations become small, inbred and likely to die out, and that the area matters for other species too. It can propose alternatives such as another site, wildlife overpasses or tunnels, and new habitat elsewhere (outcomes 1 and 3).
- Bas Haring's *Plastic Panda's* (world view): the claim under discussion is that species extinction is not a problem. Arguments for it are that extinction is natural and that most species that ever lived are extinct; arguments against it are that today's rate is far above the background rate, that humans depend on biodiversity for the six services in outcome 2, and that losing one species affects others through food webs. Whether a species also has value in itself is a normative question rather than a scientific one (outcomes 2 and 4, [A09](../../A-skills/A09-evaluation-and-judgment/README.md)).
