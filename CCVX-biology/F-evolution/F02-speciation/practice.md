#### Question

A researcher studies the common voles living in the meadows of one reclaimed polder in Flevoland. Voles of the same species also live in other polders, tens of kilometres away, with wide lakes and motorways in between.

Describe what is meant by "the population of common voles in this polder", and explain why the voles of the different polders are regarded as separate populations, although they belong to one species.

#### Answer

The population is the group of individuals of one species (the common vole) that live in the same area (this polder) at the same time and that can interbreed, so they share one gene pool.

#### Solution

### bio-f02-q02 | points 3 | outcome F2.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A student lists the following properties of the oystercatchers of a Wadden Sea island: (a) body mass per bird; (b) population density; (c) birth rate; (d) feather colour; (e) age structure; (f) allele frequency of a feather-colour gene.

Give three properties from this list that a population has but an individual does not, and explain why such properties are called "emergent properties".

#### Answer

Any three of: population density, birth rate, age structure, allele frequency.

#### Solution

Any three of: population density, birth rate, age structure, allele frequency. ### bio-f02-q03 | points 2 | outcome F2.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In the grass species slender hairgrass, an allele m that greatly increases tolerance to heavy-metal-rich soil occurs at a frequency of 0.62 around the spoil heaps of an old zinc mine in Belgium, but only at 0.05 in populations 30 km away on normal soil. In such a mine population, the frequency of m rose from 0.18 to 0.62 in the forty years after the mine opened.

Explain how the frequency of m can come to differ so strongly over space, and how it could change so strongly over time. Name the process responsible in each case.

#### Answer

In space: natural selection — under heavy-metal soil conditions only plants with allele m survive and reproduce well, while on normal soil allele m brings little or no advantage. Over time: the metal-rich soil acted from the mine's opening as a selection pressure, so the frequency of m rose generation after generation.

#### Solution

In space: natural selection — on metal-rich soil plants with allele m survive and reproduce much better than plants without it, whereas on normal soil this advantage is absent, so allele frequencies differ between places. Over time: from the moment the metal-rich soil existed, plants with m left more offspring each generation, so the frequency of m rose step by step from 0.18 to 0.62. (Gene flow through pollen and seed keeps the difference limited rather than complete.)
*Marking:* • selection pressure differs per location, causing different (geno/allele) frequencies in space (1) • repeated selection over generations raises the frequency over time (1)

### bio-f02-q05 | points 3 | outcome F2.2.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

On an archipelago lives a large, randomly mating population of wall lizards. The recessive allele g causes a grey instead of striped pattern (gg). The frequency of g in this population is 0.1. The population, which is in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, consists of 5 000 lizards.

Calculate the expected number of lizards of each of the three genotypes.

#### Answer

GG: 4 050; Gg: 900; gg: 50.

#### Solution

q = 0.1, so p = 1 − 0.1 = 0.9. In Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium: GG = p² = 0.81 → 0.81 × 5 000 = 4 050 lizards; Gg = 2pq = 2 × 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.18 → 900 lizards; gg = q² = 0.01 → 50 lizards. Check: 4 050 + 900 + 50 = 5 000.
*Marking:* • p = 0.9 and use of p², 2pq, q² (1) • 0.81 / 0.18 / 0.01 (1) • 4 050, 900 and 50 lizards (1)

### bio-f02-q06 | points 3 | outcome F2.2.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A regional newborn-screening programme finds that 1 in 10 000 newborns has an autosomal recessive disorder of fat metabolism. The population is large and may be regarded as being in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium for this gene.

Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele and the frequency of carriers in this population.

#### Answer

Allele frequency q = 0.01; carrier frequency 2pq = 0.0198 ≈ 2.0% (about 1 in 50 people).

#### Solution

Affected newborns are homozygous recessive: q² = 1/10 000 = 0.0001, so q = √0.0001 = 0.01. Then p = 1 − 0.01 = 0.99. Carriers are heterozygous: 2pq = 2 × 0.99 × 0.01 = 0.0198, which is about 2% of the population — 198 per 10 000 people, about 1 in 50.
*Marking:* • q² = 0.0001, so q = 0.01 (1) • p = 0.99 (1) • 2pq = 0.0198 ≈ 2% (1)

### bio-f02-q07 | points 4 | outcome F2.2.3 | difficulty hard

#### Question

On one dune island, researchers determine the banding genotype of 1 000 grove snails. The allele B gives a banded shell, b an unbanded shell. They count: 640 BB, 320 Bb, 40 bb.

Calculate the allele frequencies p and q, and check with the Hardy-Weinberg principle whether this population can be in equilibrium for this gene.

#### Answer

p = 0.8 and q = 0.2; expected genotype numbers are 640 BB, 320 Bb and 40 bb, exactly as observed, so the population may indeed be in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.

#### Solution

There are 2 000 alleles in total. p = (2 × 640 + 320) / 2 000 = 1 600 / 2 000 = 0.8. q = (2 × 40 + 320) / 2 000 = 400 / 2 000 = 0.2 (check: p + q = 1). Hardy-Weinberg expectation for 1 000 snails: BB = p² = 0.64 → 640; Bb = 2pq = 0.32 → 320; bb = q² = 0.04 → 40. The observed numbers match the expectation exactly, so this gene is consistent with Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium.
*Marking:* • allele counting with 2 000 alleles, p = 0.8 (1) • q = 0.2 (1) • expected numbers 640 / 320 / 40 from p², 2pq, q² (1) • conclusion: matches, so equilibrium possible (1)

### bio-f02-q09 | points 3 | outcome F2.2.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A computer simulation starts ten populations of 24 beetles and ten populations of 2 400 beetles, all with allele A at frequency 0.5 (A is neutral: no selection, no migration, no new mutations). After 15 generations the frequency of A is:

| Population | Small (N = 24) | Large (N = 2 400) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.00 | 0.52 |
| 2 | 0.95 | 0.48 |
| 3 | 1.00 | 0.55 |
| 4 | 0.71 | 0.47 |
| 5 | 0.00 | 0.50 |

Give two differences between the small and the large populations, and explain why genetic drift affects the small populations more strongly.

In the small populations the frequency of A scattered widely and was even lost (0.00) or fixed (1.00); in the large populations it stayed close to 0.5.

### bio-f02-q10 | points 2 | outcome F2.2.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

After a storm, twelve ground beetles of a mainland species are stranded on a small, empty island. On the mainland, a green elytra allele has a frequency of 0.10, but among the twelve founders it is 0.45. Ten generations later the island population counts thousands of beetles and the allele is still at about 0.45.

Name the effect shown here and explain how an allele that is rare on the mainland can be common on the island.

#### Answer

The founder effect.

#### Solution

The founder effect: a few individuals start a new population, so their gene pool is only a small random sample of the source population. ### bio-f02-q11 | points 2 | outcome F2.2.2 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A steppe-dwelling hamster species is almost wiped out by an epidemic: about 200 animals survive. Sixty years later the population has recovered to over 40 000 animals, but DNA tests show that it has much less genetic variation than museum specimens from before the epidemic.

Name the effect shown here and explain why the variation is still low, although the number of animals has long since recovered.

#### Answer

The bottleneck effect.

#### Solution

The bottleneck effect: the population shrank drastically, and the few survivors carried only a (random) part of the original alleles. When the population grows again, all descendants inherit only the alleles of those survivors; lost alleles return only through very rare new mutations, so the genetic variation stays low for a long time.
*Marking:* • bottleneck effect named (1) • survivors carry only part of the alleles; lost variants return only slowly (by new mutation), so variation stays low (1)

#### Question

Two populations of the dune pansy grow in dune valleys 4 km apart. Butterflies and wind carry pollen and seeds back and forth, so every generation about 5% of the plants in each valley descend from immigrants from the other valley.

Gene flow continually mixes the two gene pools, so allele frequencies in the two valleys are kept similar.

### bio-f02-q14 | points 3 | outcome F2.3.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

On a dry island, a seed-eating cardinal finch feeds mostly on small, soft seeds. After two extreme drought years, mainly large, hard-shelled seeds remain on the plants. Measurements of mean beak depth give:

| Group | Mean beak depth (mm) |

Beak depth varies heritably within the population; during the drought, birds with deeper beaks could crack the remaining hard seeds more often, so they survived and reproduced more than shallow-beaked birds (higher fitness).

### bio-f02-q21 | points 3 | outcome F2.4.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A lava flow splits one population of a flightless darkling beetle into two parts on opposite slopes of a volcano. There is no longer any gene flow between them. After thousands of generations the two populations differ strongly in size, colour and DNA.

Natural selection favours different traits on the two slopes (different climate, food plants, predators), so different alleles have the highest fitness on each side. Mutation, drift and selection therefore push the two gene pools ever further apart — divergence — until interbreeding is no longer possible.
*Marking:* • mutation: new, different alleles arise by chance in each population (1) • drift: allele frequencies wander apart by chance in the separate populations (1) • selection: different selection pressures favour different alleles/traits on each side (1)

Classify which structures are homologous and which analogous, and explain why only the homologous structures are usable evidence for kinship in a cladogram.

#### Answer

The ichthyosaur flipper and the bat wing are homologous; the dragonfly wing is analogous to them.

#### Solution

The ichthyosaur flipper and the bat wing are homologous: same evolutionary origin and same basic build (one upper-arm bone, two forearm bones, hand bones), although the function differs (swimming vs flying). The dragonfly wing is analogous: same function (flying) but a completely different origin and construction. Only homologous structures trace back to a common ancestor and so reveal descent; analogous structures result from convergent evolution under similar selection pressures and therefore mislead kinship analysis.
*Marking:* • correct classification: flipper + bat wing homologous, dragonfly wing analogous, with reason (same build/origin vs same function, different origin) (1) • only homology indicates common descent; analogy arises by convergence and says nothing about kinship (1)

#### Question

A Madagascan star orchid has a nectar spur 25 cm deep; its pollinator, a hawk moth, has a tongue of the same length. Neither partner can reproduce well without the other: orchids with a slightly deeper spur are pollinated better, and moths with a slightly longer tongue can reach nectar that other insects cannot reach.

Name the phenomenon shown here and explain how it arises.

#### Answer

Coevolution.

#### Solution

This is coevolution: two species evolve in response to each other because each is a selection pressure for the other. Orchids with a deeper nectar spur force the visiting moth to touch the flower properly, so they are pollinated better and leave more offspring — spurs become deeper over generations. Moths with a longer tongue reach more nectar and feed more offspring — tongues become longer over generations. Each side adapts to the other again and again, so the traits escalate in tandem.
*Marking:* • coevolution named (1) • explanation: each species is the other's selection pressure, so both traits (spur depth, tongue length) are selected step by step in response to each other (1)
