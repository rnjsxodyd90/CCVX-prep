## 1. When the first organisms lived

- The Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago (textbooks say 4.5–4.6). That age comes mainly from radiometric dating of meteorites, which formed at the same time: radioactive isotopes decay at a known rate (half-life), as explained in [F02](../F02-speciation/README.md). *(overlap: CvTE P4.4 spec 2)*
- The oldest widely accepted evidence of life is about 3.5 billion years old. It consists of stromatolites (*stromatolieten*), layered rock structures built by mats of microorganisms, and microscopic fossils of cell-like structures.
- Older traces, up to about 3.8 billion years or more, are chemical signs (carbon with an isotope ratio typical of living things) or disputed fossils, and they're debated.
- So life existed by about 3.5 billion years ago at the latest, within roughly the first billion years of Earth's history. That falls in the Archean eon (about 4.0–2.5 billion years ago), early in the Precambrian; see the Binas table of the geological timescale.

| Event | About how long ago |
|---|---|
| Earth forms | 4.5–4.6 billion years |
| oldest widely accepted evidence of life (prokaryotes) | 3.5 billion years; debated traces up to about 3.8 billion years |
| oxygen builds up in the atmosphere (the Great Oxidation Event) | 2.4 billion years |
| first eukaryotes | 1.8–2 billion years |
| most animal groups appear in the fossil record (Cambrian explosion) | 540 million years |

- Life has therefore existed for more than three-quarters of Earth's history, and for its first 1.5–2 billion years it consisted of prokaryotes only.
- The oxygen came from photosynthesis by cyanobacteria (6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂). At first it reacted away, for example with iron dissolved in the oceans, and only then built up in the air. Oxygen made aerobic respiration possible and formed the ozone layer, which shields life from UV radiation.
- **Trap:** the oldest evidence of life isn't the moment life began. Life must be older than its oldest traces, and very old traces are hard to recognize, which is why these dates are far less certain than the age of the Earth.

## 2. How the first organic molecules arose

- Chemical evolution (*chemische evolutie*) is the idea that, before there was life, simple inorganic molecules reacted step by step into ever more complex organic molecules: first monomers such as amino acids, sugars and nucleotide bases, then polymers. Alexander Oparin (1924) and J.B.S. Haldane (1929) proposed it independently.
- The energy for these reactions came from lightning, UV radiation (there was no ozone layer yet), volcanic heat and hot springs.
- The early atmosphere had little or no free oxygen (O₂). That mattered, because O₂ would have oxidized and broken down the organic molecules.
- In the primeval soup (*oersoep*) version of the idea, organic molecules built up over a long time in the oceans or in pools, making a "soup" in which further reactions could take place.
- The Miller-Urey experiment (1953) tested this. Stanley Miller, working in Harold Urey's lab, built a closed glass apparatus:
  - What went in: water, boiled to make water vapour (the "ocean"), and the gases methane (CH₄), ammonia (NH₃) and hydrogen (H₂), then thought to make up the early atmosphere.
  - How it ran: electric sparks between two electrodes imitated lightning, and a cooler condensed the vapour back into liquid ("rain") that flowed back to the flask.
  - What came out: after about a week the water had changed colour and contained several amino acids, including glycine and alanine, and other simple organic molecules. A 2008 re-analysis, with modern methods, of stored samples from Miller's 1950s experiments found more than 20 amino acids.
- The conclusion: organic building blocks of life can form from inorganic substances without any living organism. The gases contain all the elements needed. For the simplest amino acid the overall balance (not the actual reaction steps) is 2 CH₄ + NH₃ + 2 H₂O → C₂H₅NO₂ (glycine) + 5 H₂. Amino acids and nucleic acids as substances are in [chemistry G01](../../../CCVX-chemistry/G-society-chemistry-and-technology/G01-chemistry-of-life/README.md).
- The experiment has limitations:
  - The early atmosphere is now thought to have been mainly CO₂, N₂ and water vapour, and such a mixture gives far fewer organic molecules in these experiments.
  - It produced monomers, not proteins, nucleic acids or cells.
  - The amino acids came as equal mixtures of left- and right-handed forms, whereas proteins use only one of the two.
- Black smokers (*black smokers*) are hydrothermal vents (*hydrothermale bronnen*) on the ocean floor near mid-ocean ridges. Seawater seeps into the crust, is heated by magma to about 350–400 °C (staying liquid under the high pressure), dissolves minerals and gushes out again. Where it meets cold seawater, metal sulfides precipitate as black "smoke" and build chimneys.
- One hypothesis puts the first organic molecules at such vents. They supply energy (heat and chemical gradients), reduced substances such as H₂ and H₂S, and surfaces of iron and nickel sulfide minerals that can catalyse reactions, such as making simple organic compounds from CO₂ and H₂.
- Alkaline vents, such as the Lost City field discovered in 2000, are cooler. They have natural proton gradients across thin mineral walls, similar to the proton gradients that cells use to make ATP.
- Today black smokers support ecosystems that don't depend on sunlight. Bacteria and archaea there get energy by oxidizing H₂S and use it to make organic matter from CO₂ (chemosynthesis, *chemosynthese*); tube worms live on symbiotic bacteria of this kind. *(overlap: CvTE M2.3 spec 14)* Chemosynthesis is in [B02](../../B-self-regulation/B02-metabolism-in-cells/README.md).
- Organic molecules also reach Earth from space: the Murchison meteorite, which fell in Australia in 1969, contains dozens of amino acids. The idea that life itself came from space (panspermia) only moves the question of how life arose to somewhere else.
- **Trap:** chemical evolution isn't spontaneous generation (*generatio spontanea*), the old idea that life keeps arising from dead material. Around 1860 Pasteur showed with swan-neck flasks that microorganisms today come only from other microorganisms; chemical evolution happened under the very different conditions of the early Earth, with no O₂ and no organisms to eat the molecules. And Miller-Urey made building blocks, not life.

## 3. How the first cells arose

- Theories of the first cells try to explain a series of steps: monomers, then polymers, then self-copying molecules, then a membrane around them (a protocell), and finally a cell with its own metabolism and heredity.
- In water, monomers don't easily join: each bond forms in a condensation reaction that releases water, and in a lot of water the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, wins. They join more easily when concentrated, by evaporation in drying pools or by sticking to mineral surfaces such as clay; in experiments, clay helps link nucleotides into short RNA chains. Condensation and hydrolysis are in [chemistry C01](../../../CCVX-chemistry/C-chemical-processes-and-conservation-laws/C01-chemical-processes/README.md).
- The RNA world (*RNA-wereld*) hypothesis says that early life used RNA both for heredity and for catalysis. RNA can store information in its base sequence and can also act as an enzyme (a ribozyme, *ribozym*).
- That solves a chicken-and-egg problem: today DNA needs proteins (enzymes) to be copied, while proteins need DNA (via RNA) to be made.
- The evidence for an RNA world:
  - In the ribosome, the bond between amino acids is formed by rRNA, not by protein.
  - DNA replication still starts with short RNA primers ([E01](../../E-reproduction/E01-dna-replication/README.md)).
  - Key molecules such as ATP, NAD⁺ and coenzyme A contain RNA building blocks (nucleotides).
  - Ribozymes made in the lab can copy short stretches of RNA.
- Later, DNA took over the storage of information, because it's more stable (double-stranded, with deoxyribose, and repairable using the other strand). Proteins took over most catalysis, because 20 different amino acids make them far more versatile.
- Self-organization (*zelforganisatie*) means that molecules arrange themselves into ordered structures without outside guidance, driven by their own properties. Phospholipids and fatty acids in water spontaneously form double layers that close into vesicles, with the hydrophilic heads facing the water and the hydrophobic tails facing each other ([chemistry B03](../../../CCVX-chemistry/B-substances-and-materials/B03-bonds-and-properties/README.md)).
- Such vesicles can serve as protocells: they can enclose RNA, grow by taking up more fatty acids and split into smaller vesicles. A protocell whose RNA copies itself faster would multiply faster, so natural selection could start at this level. An older model of protocell-like droplets was Oparin's coacervates.
- The first cells were prokaryotic and lived without oxygen. Whether they fed on organic molecules around them (heterotrophs) or made their own from CO₂ with chemical energy, as at hydrothermal vents (chemoautotrophs), is still debated.
- All living things share DNA, RNA, the almost universal genetic code, ribosomes and ATP. So they descend from one common ancestral population, called LUCA (last universal common ancestor); the universal code is in [F01](../F01-selection/README.md).
- Archaea (*archaea*) are prokaryotes (cells without a nucleus) that form a domain of their own, next to the Bacteria and the Eukarya. Carl Woese recognized them in 1977 by comparing ribosomal RNA sequences.
- Archaea differ from bacteria in their membrane lipids (ether bonds and branched chains), in their cell wall (no peptidoglycan), and in some steps of gene expression, which resemble those of eukaryotes.
- Many archaea live in extreme places: heat-loving species in hot springs and at black smokers (some grow above 100 °C), salt-loving species in salt lakes, and methane producers in swamps and in the guts of cows. Many others live in ordinary soil and seawater.
- Their name means "ancient", after the idea that they resemble early life in conditions like those of the early Earth.
- The endosymbiotic theory (*endosymbiontentheorie*), made widely known by Lynn Margulis in the late 1960s, explains where two organelles of eukaryotes came from. Mitochondria descend from aerobic bacteria that a larger host cell, probably an archaeon, took in and that went on living inside it (endosymbiosis, *endosymbiose*). Chloroplasts descend from cyanobacteria taken in later by a eukaryote that already had mitochondria. *(overlap: CvTE M2.1 spec 3)* Mitochondria and chloroplasts are in [B02](../../B-self-regulation/B02-metabolism-in-cells/README.md), their DNA in [B01](../../B-self-regulation/B01-protein-synthesis/README.md).
- The evidence for the endosymbiotic theory:
  - Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own DNA, which is circular and lacks histones, like bacterial DNA.
  - They have their own ribosomes, which resemble bacterial ribosomes (70S) rather than the eukaryotic ribosomes in the cytoplasm (80S), and which are sensitive to some antibiotics that block bacterial ribosomes.
  - They are about the size of bacteria, and they arise only from existing mitochondria or chloroplasts, by binary fission, like bacteria.
  - They have two membranes, which fits a bacterium taken in by a host cell; the inner membrane resembles a bacterial cell membrane.
  - Their DNA sequences resemble those of particular bacteria: mitochondrial DNA that of α-proteobacteria, chloroplast DNA that of cyanobacteria.
  - Endosymbiosis still happens today: reef-building corals, for example, have single-celled algae living inside their cells.
- Most genes of the original bacteria have moved to the nucleus of the host cell, so mitochondria and chloroplasts can no longer live on their own.
- **Trap:** the endosymbiotic theory explains the origin of mitochondria and chloroplasts, not the origin of life or of the first cells, since prokaryotes existed long before. And archaea aren't older than bacteria: both lineages go back to the same common ancestor.

## 4. Implications for questions of belief

- Theories on the genesis of life are scientific because they make predictions that observation or experiment can test, and that could turn out wrong. The Miller-Urey experiment tested whether amino acids form under assumed early-Earth conditions; the endosymbiotic theory predicted that mitochondrial DNA would resemble bacterial DNA, which sequencing confirmed.
- Many origin-of-life ideas are still hypotheses: nobody has made a living cell from non-living molecules, and the steps from simple molecules to the first cell aren't known in detail. The theory of evolution, which explains how life diversified once it existed, is far better established ([F02](../F02-speciation/README.md), [F03](../F03-biodiversity/README.md)).
- World views and religions deal with other kinds of questions, about meaning, purpose, and God or a creator, which experiments can't test. People combine science and belief in different ways:
  - Creationism reads a creation story literally, for example that God made the world and all kinds of organisms in six days. Young-earth creationists hold that the Earth is only thousands of years old, which conflicts with radiometric dating.
  - Intelligent design claims that some biological structures are too complex to have arisen by natural processes, and so point to a designer. Most scientists don't regard it as science, because it makes no testable predictions.
  - Theistic evolution holds that God works through natural processes such as chemical evolution and natural selection. Many believers and churches accept the scientific account in this way; the Catholic Church, for example, accepts evolution.
  - Naturalism holds that natural processes are all there is, so no creator is needed; many atheists take this position.
- Science works only with natural explanations, so it can't prove or disprove the existence of God. Some people see the still-unexplained steps in the origin of life as room for a creator ("god of the gaps"); others expect research to fill those gaps.
- The CCVX outcome asks you to describe such implications, not to choose between them, and to keep scientific arguments apart from world-view positions and personal views ([A09](../../A-skills/A09-evaluation-and-judgment/README.md)).
- **Trap:** in science, "theory" doesn't mean "just a guess": a scientific theory is a well-tested explanation. And a gap in scientific knowledge is not evidence for or against a religious view.
