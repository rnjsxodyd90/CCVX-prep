### bio-f04-q01 | points 3 | outcome F4.1.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

A documentary about the genesis of life opens with a timeline of the Earth's history.

Give (a) how long ago the Earth was formed, (b) the age of the oldest widely accepted evidence of life, including the kind of evidence this is, and (c) the age of the first eukaryotic cells.

#### Answer

(a) About 4.5–4.6 billion years ago.

#### Solution

(a) About 4.5–4.6 billion years ago (from radiometric dating of meteorites). (b) About 3.5 billion years: stromatolites (layered rocks built by mats of microorganisms) and microscopic cell-like fossils; older chemical traces, up to about 3.8 billion years, are debated. (c) About 1.8–2 billion years ago.
*Marking:* • (a) 4.5–4.6 billion years (1) • (b) ≈3.5 billion years with stromatolites/microfossils named (1) • (c) ≈1.8–2 billion years (1)

### bio-f04-q03 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

A scientist in the documentary says: "The oldest evidence of life is 3.5 billion years old, so life arose exactly 3.5 billion years ago."

Explain why this conclusion is too strong. Name two reasons why the moment of origin is so hard to determine.

#### Answer

Evidence of life at 3.5 billion years only shows that life existed by then; the origin itself must be older.

#### Solution

Evidence of life at 3.5 billion years only shows that life existed by then; the origin itself must be older than its oldest traces. Reasons it is hard to pin down: very old rocks are rare and have been strongly altered by heat and pressure since, and the first organisms were small, soft and simple, so they fossilise badly and their traces are hard to recognise as biological.
*Marking:* • origin precedes the oldest evidence (1) • rocks altered/rare and/or microscopic, easily destroyed traces (1)

### bio-f04-q04 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Stromatolites of about 3.5 billion years old are regarded as strong evidence of early life.

Describe what stromatolites are, and explain which metabolic process of the microorganisms involved later changed the entire atmosphere of the Earth.

#### Answer

Stromatolites are layered rock structures built up by mats of microorganisms (such as cyanobacteria) that trapped sediment layer upon layer.

#### Solution

Stromatolites are layered rock structures built up by mats of microorganisms (such as cyanobacteria) that trapped sediment layer upon layer; similar mats still grow today, for example in shallow Australian bays. Later, cyanobacteria carried out oxygenic photosynthesis (6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂); the O₂ first reacted away (for example with dissolved iron) and from about 2.4 billion years ago accumulated in the atmosphere, enabling aerobic respiration and forming the ozone layer.
*Marking:* • description: layered structures from microbial mats (1) • photosynthesis by cyanobacteria → build-up of O₂ (1)

### bio-f04-q05 | points 3 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Describe the idea of chemical evolution (Oparin and Haldane) as an explanation for the first organic molecules: what sequence of steps was proposed, where the energy came from, and why the absence of free oxygen (O₂) in the early atmosphere mattered.

#### Answer

Chemical evolution proposes that simple inorganic molecules reacted step by step into ever more complex organic molecules: first monomers such as amino acids, sugars and nucleotide bases, then polymers.

#### Solution

Chemical evolution proposes that simple inorganic molecules reacted step by step into ever more complex organic molecules: first monomers such as amino acids, sugars and nucleotide bases, then polymers. The necessary energy came from lightning, UV radiation (there was no ozone layer yet), volcanic heat and hot springs. Free O₂ had to be absent because it is a strong oxidant that would have attacked and broken down the organic molecules; the early atmosphere contained little or no O₂.
*Marking:* • stepwise build-up: inorganic → monomers → polymers (1) • energy sources: lightning/UV/volcanic heat (1) • O₂ would have oxidised/broken down the organic molecules (1)

### bio-f04-q06 | points 3 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Describe the Miller-Urey experiment (1953): what mixture was used and what it represented, how "lightning" and "rain" were imitated, and what was found in the water after about a week.

#### Answer

The apparatus held water vapour (the "ocean" being boiled) together with methane (CH₄), ammonia (NH₃) and hydrogen (H₂), the gases then thought to form the early atmosphere.

#### Solution

The apparatus held water vapour (the "ocean" being boiled) together with methane (CH₄), ammonia (NH₃) and hydrogen (H₂), the gases then thought to form the early atmosphere. Electric sparks between electrodes imitated lightning, and a cooler condensed the vapour so that it flowed back like rain. After about a week the water contained several amino acids (among them glycine and alanine) and other simple organic molecules; a 2008 re-analysis of stored samples found more than 20 amino acids.
*Marking:* • correct gases + water vapour, representing ocean and early atmosphere (1) • sparks = lightning, cooler = rain (1) • amino acids (and other organic molecules) were formed (1)

### bio-f04-q07 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

For the formation of the simplest amino acid, glycine (C₂H₅NO₂), in a Miller-Urey type of mixture, the following overall balance is proposed (it does not show the real reaction steps):

2 CH₄ + NH₃ + 2 H₂O → C₂H₅NO₂ + 5 H₂

Show by counting the atoms on both sides that this equation balances.

#### Answer

C: left 2 (from 2 CH₄), right 2 ✓.

#### Solution

C: left 2 (from 2 CH₄), right 2 ✓. H: left 2×4 + 3 + 2×2 = 15, right 5 + 5×2 = 15 ✓. N: left 1, right 1 ✓. O: left 2, right 2 ✓. All four elements balance, so the equation is correct.
*Marking:* • correct counts of all elements on both sides (1) • explicit conclusion that the equation balances (1)

### bio-f04-q08 | points 3 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In a repetition of a Miller-Urey type experiment, the starting mixture contained 60 mmol of carbon (as CH₄). After one week, the water contained:

| Product | Amount (mmol) | Carbon atoms per molecule |
|---|---|---|
| glycine | 0.60 | 2 |
| alanine | 0.30 | 3 |
| aspartic acid | 0.10 | 4 |

Calculate what percentage of the original carbon ended up in these three amino acids.

#### Answer

Carbon in glycine: 0.60 × 2 = 1.2 mmol; in alanine: 0.30 × 3 = 0.9 mmol; in aspartic acid: 0.10 × 4 = 0.4 mmol.

#### Solution

Carbon in glycine: 0.60 × 2 = 1.2 mmol; in alanine: 0.30 × 3 = 0.9 mmol; in aspartic acid: 0.10 × 4 = 0.4 mmol. Total: 1.2 + 0.9 + 0.4 = 2.5 mmol C. That is 2.5/60 × 100% ≈ 4.2% of the original carbon — a small but significant yield of building blocks formed without any organism.
*Marking:* • multiplication of each amount by its C count (1) • total 2.5 mmol C (1) • 2.5/60 ≈ 4.2% (1)

### bio-f04-q09 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

The amino acids from Miller-Urey type experiments are an equal mixture of left-handed and right-handed forms, while proteins in living organisms consist almost exclusively of left-handed amino acids.

Explain why this equal mixture is a problem for the idea that such mixtures directly gave rise to proteins.

#### Answer

Both forms have the same chemical formula and arise equally easily in non-biological reactions.

#### Solution

Both forms have the same chemical formula and arise equally easily in non-biological reactions, so the experiment yields a 1:1 mixture. Living cells, however, build proteins from only one of the two forms, and chains built from a random mixture fold and function poorly. An extra, still not fully understood step was therefore needed to select one form before proteins could arise.
*Marking:* • non-biological synthesis gives a 1:1 mixture of both forms (1) • life uses only one form, so a selection step is missing (1)

### bio-f04-q10 | points 3 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty hard

#### Question

A textbook states: "The Miller-Urey experiment proved how life began on Earth."

Assess this statement: besides the handedness problem, name two other limitations of the experiment, and state what the experiment did show.

#### Answer

The gas mixture used (CH₄, NH₃, H₂) differs from the early atmosphere as it is now reconstructed: mainly CO₂, N₂ and water vapour.

#### Solution

The gas mixture used (CH₄, NH₃, H₂) differs from the early atmosphere as it is now reconstructed: mainly CO₂, N₂ and water vapour, and such a mixture gives far fewer organic molecules. And the experiment produced only monomers (amino acids and other small molecules), not proteins, nucleic acids, membranes or cells. What it did show is important enough: building blocks of life can form spontaneously from inorganic substances under plausible early-Earth conditions — but that is not the origin of life itself. The textbook claim is therefore too strong.
*Marking:* • atmosphere composition now thought different → lower yield (1) • only monomers, no polymers/cells (1) • correct verdict: building blocks yes, proof of life's origin no (1)

### bio-f04-q11 | points 3 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Describe what black smokers are and how they form, and explain why some researchers think the first organic molecules (and possibly the first life) arose at such hydrothermal vents rather than in a warm surface ocean.

#### Answer

Black smokers are hot springs on the deep ocean floor near mid-ocean ridges.

#### Solution

Black smokers are hot springs on the deep ocean floor near mid-ocean ridges. Seawater seeps into the crust, is heated by magma to about 350–400 °C (staying liquid under high pressure), dissolves minerals and gushes out; on meeting cold seawater the metal sulfides precipitate as black "smoke" and build chimneys. Arguments for an origin at vents: they supply energy (heat and chemical gradients) and reduced substances such as H₂ and H₂S; their iron- and nickel-sulfide mineral surfaces can catalyse reactions; and the molecules stay concentrated in pores instead of being diluted in a whole ocean. The ecosystems that thrive there today, based on chemosynthesis instead of sunlight, show that primary production is possible in such a place.
*Marking:* • description: heated seawater + precipitated metal sulfides forming chimneys (1) • arguments: energy, reduced substances, catalytic mineral surfaces, little dilution (any two) (1) • present-day chemosynthetic ecosystems as feasibility evidence (1)

Name one open problem of the primeval-soup theory that the hydrothermal-vent hypothesis tries to solve, and one remaining open problem that both theories share.

### bio-f04-q13 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.2 | difficulty easy

#### Question

The Murchison meteorite, which fell in Australia in 1969, contains dozens of different amino acids.

What does this finding add to the theories about the first organic molecules on Earth? And why does the idea that life itself came from space (panspermia) not solve the question of how life originated?

#### Answer

It shows that amino acids and other organic molecules also form in space and can be delivered to a young planet.

#### Solution

It shows that amino acids and other organic molecules also form in space and can be delivered to a young planet, so part of the raw material for chemical evolution may have arrived from outside the Earth. Panspermia only shifts the question: if life came from elsewhere, it must still have originated there from non-living matter by some unknown process, so the question of how life begins anywhere remains unanswered.
*Marking:* • organic molecules can form in space/be delivered (1) • panspermia moves rather than answers the origin question (1)

### bio-f04-q14 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

In the primeval ocean, amino acids and nucleotides would have had to link up into proteins and nucleic acids. Each such bond forms in a condensation reaction that releases one water molecule.

Explain why the formation of long chains proceeds poorly in a large body of water, and name two circumstances on the early Earth that would have helped chains to form.

#### Answer

In a condensation reaction each new bond releases water; in excess water the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, dominates and chains break faster than they form.

#### Solution

In a condensation reaction each new bond releases water; in excess water the reverse reaction, hydrolysis, dominates and chains break faster than they form. Helping circumstances: evaporation in shallow pools or on shorelines concentrated the monomers, and mineral surfaces such as clay bind monomers and hold them in position — in experiments, clay promotes the linking of nucleotides into short RNA chains.
*Marking:* • hydrolysis wins in excess water (1) • concentration by evaporation and/or catalysis on mineral (clay) surfaces (1)

### bio-f04-q15 | points 4 | outcome F4.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Today, copying DNA requires protein enzymes, while making those enzymes requires the information in DNA — a chicken-and-egg problem for the origin of life.

Explain how the RNA-world hypothesis solves this problem, and give two pieces of evidence that RNA once played a larger role than it does now.

#### Answer

RNA can do both jobs: its base sequence stores information, and folded RNA can act as a catalyst (a ribozyme).

#### Solution

RNA can do both jobs: its base sequence stores information, and folded RNA can act as a catalyst (a ribozyme). The first self-copying systems may therefore have consisted of RNA alone, before DNA took over information storage (more stable) and proteins took over catalysis (more versatile). Evidence (any two): in the ribosome the bond between amino acids is formed by rRNA, not by protein; DNA replication still starts with short RNA primers; key molecules such as ATP, NAD⁺ and coenzyme A contain nucleotides (RNA building blocks); ribozymes made in the lab can copy short stretches of RNA.
*Marking:* • RNA stores information and catalyses (ribozyme) (1) • conclusion: an RNA-only stage solves the chicken-and-egg problem (1) • first piece of evidence (1) • second piece of evidence (1)

### bio-f04-q16 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Phospholipids and fatty acids in water spontaneously form double layers that close into vesicles.

Explain why this is called self-organization, and why such vesicles are seen as a possible precursor of the first cell (a protocell), including the role natural selection could already play at this stage.

#### Answer

It is called self-organization because the molecules order themselves, driven by their own properties (hydrophilic heads into the water, hydrophobic tails together), without any outside guidance.

#### Solution

It is called self-organization because the molecules order themselves, driven by their own properties (hydrophilic heads into the water, hydrophobic tails together), without any outside guidance. As protocells the vesicles can enclose molecules such as RNA, grow by taking up more fatty acids, and split into smaller vesicles. A vesicle whose enclosed RNA copies itself faster would be "reproduced" faster than others, so natural selection — difference in reproductive success of heritable variants — could already begin at this level.
*Marking:* • self-ordering by the molecules' own properties (1) • vesicle can enclose/grow/split, so selection on faster-copying contents becomes possible (1)

### bio-f04-q17 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Researchers disagree on how the first cells obtained their food: heterotroph-first theories say they took up organic molecules from their surroundings; chemoautotroph-first theories say they built their own organic molecules from CO₂, using chemical energy, as at hydrothermal vents.

Give one argument (or one point in line with each theory's setting) for each of the two views.

Heterotroph-first: the primeval soup or delivered organic molecules (as Miller-Urey showed they can form) provided a ready food supply, so the first cells did not need a complete metabolism of their own.

### bio-f04-q18 | points 4 | outcome F4.1.3 | difficulty medium

#### Question

According to the endosymbiotic theory, mitochondria descend from aerobic bacteria and chloroplasts from cyanobacteria that were taken in by a host cell. The table lists characteristics of mitochondria and chloroplasts.

| Characteristic | Observation |
|---|---|
| DNA | small, circular, without histones |
| ribosomes | 70S type, sensitive to some antibiotics that block bacterial ribosomes |
| membranes | a double membrane surrounds the organelle |
| multiplication | by division of existing organelles (binary fission) |

For each of the four characteristics, explain how it supports a bacterial origin of these organelles.

#### Answer

DNA: free-living bacteria also have a small circular chromosome without histones, unlike the linear chromosomes with histones in the eukaryotic nucleus.

#### Solution

DNA: free-living bacteria also have a small circular chromosome without histones, unlike the linear chromosomes with histones in the eukaryotic nucleus — the organelle still carries its own, bacterial-type genome. Ribosomes: 70S is the ribosome type of bacteria (the cytoplasm of eukaryotes has 80S ribosomes), including the same antibiotic sensitivity, so the organelle still translates using bacterial-style machinery. Membranes: a bacterium taken in by a host cell would end up wrapped in two membranes — its own cell membrane plus the host's membrane around it. Multiplication: the organelles arise only from existing organelles by binary fission, the way bacteria divide; the cell cannot make them anew.
*Marking:* • circular DNA without histones matches bacteria (1) • 70S ribosomes match bacterial ribosomes (1) • double membrane matches an engulfed bacterium (1) • binary fission of pre-existing organelles matches bacterial division (1)

### bio-f04-q22 | points 2 | outcome F4.1.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

All living organisms — bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes — use DNA as their hereditary material, transcribe it into RNA, translate RNA at ribosomes, use ATP as their energy carrier, and share (almost) the same genetic code.

Explain what researchers conclude from these shared features about the origin of the domains, and give the name used for that common ancestor.

#### Answer

Such fundamental and complex molecular machinery is extremely unlikely to have arisen independently several times in exactly the same form.

#### Solution

Such fundamental and complex molecular machinery is extremely unlikely to have arisen independently several times in exactly the same form. The conclusion is that all present-day life descends from one common ancestral population: the last universal common ancestor (LUCA). The shared code is therefore a strong kinship argument: the three domains are branches of one family tree of life.
*Marking:* • shared features too complex for independent origin → common descent (1) • LUCA named (1)
