### bio-f01-q01 | points 2 | outcome F1.1.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

In the production of human insulin, bacteria are given the human insulin gene and then make genuine human insulin. Explain what is meant by "DNA is the universal carrier of genetic information", and use this to explain why a bacterium can produce a human protein.

#### Answer

Universal means that in virtually all organisms the genetic information is stored in DNA as a sequence of the same four bases (A, T, C, G) and is read with the same genetic code: every codon stands for the same amino acid. A bacterium therefore reads the human insulin gene exactly as a human cell does and builds the human insulin protein.

#### Solution

Universal means that in virtually all organisms the genetic information is stored in DNA as a sequence of the same four bases (A, T, C, G) and is read with the same genetic code: every codon stands for the same amino acid. A bacterium therefore reads the human insulin gene exactly as a human cell does and builds the human insulin protein.
*Marking:* • genetic information stored in DNA as a base sequence, in all organisms (1) • the same genetic code everywhere, so the human gene is translated into the same human protein (1)

### bio-f01-q18 | points 2 | outcome F1.3.1 | difficulty easy

#### Question

During meiosis, two processes ensure that every reproductive cell gets a unique combination of genes. Name both processes and state in which phase of meiosis I each takes place.

#### Answer

(a) Independent assortment of the homologous chromosome pairs, in metaphase I: each pair lines up at random, so whole chromosomes are shuffled. (b) Crossing-over between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, in prophase I: matching pieces of chromosomes are swapped.

#### Solution

(1) Independent assortment of the homologous chromosome pairs, in metaphase I: each pair lines up independently at the equator, so each gamete gets a random mix of paternal and maternal chromosomes. (2) Crossing-over, in prophase I: paired homologues exchange pieces between non-sister chromatids, so alleles of linked genes are recombined. (Random fertilization afterwards adds yet another shuffle, but is not part of meiosis.)
*Marking:* • independent assortment in metaphase I (1) • crossing-over between non-sister chromatids in prophase I (1)

### bio-f01-q23 | points 3 | outcome F1.4.1 | difficulty medium

#### Question

Explain how mutation and how recombination each increase the genetic variation in a population, and make clear which of the two creates new alleles. Illustrate your answer with an example.

#### Answer

Mutation is the only source of new alleles: a change in the DNA (for example a point mutation) creates an allele that did not exist in the gene pool before. Recombination creates no new alleles, but new combinations of existing alleles — by independent assortment and crossing-over, and by random fertilization — so every generation contains many new genotypes.

#### Solution

Mutation is the only source of new alleles: a change in the DNA (for example a point mutation in a gene) creates an allele that did not exist in the gene pool before, such as a new resistance allele in an insect population. Recombination creates no new alleles, but new combinations of existing alleles: independent assortment and crossing-over in meiosis, plus random fertilization, constantly produce new genotypes. Example: an individual AaBb can form gametes AB, Ab, aB and ab, and crossing-over between A and B turns parental gametes into extra recombinant ones. Together these processes keep the gene pool rich in different alleles and (especially) different combinations.
*Marking:* • mutation creates new alleles (the only source) (1) • recombination creates new combinations of existing alleles (assortment/crossing-over/fertilization) (1) • a correct example linking these to more genotypes in the population (1)

#### Question

Human insulin is produced with genetically modified bacteria. Put the steps in the correct order and describe them briefly: fermentation in a fermenter; joining gene and plasmid with DNA ligase; making cDNA of the insulin gene with reverse transcriptase; selecting the bacteria that took up a plasmid; cutting a plasmid with the same restriction enzyme; transformation (bringing the plasmid into the bacteria). Also explain why cDNA is used instead of a piece of human DNA cut directly from the chromosome.

#### Answer

Order: make cDNA of the insulin gene with reverse transcriptase → cut a plasmid with the same restriction enzyme → join gene and plasmid with DNA ligase → transformation into bacteria → select the bacteria with a plasmid → grow them in a fermenter. cDNA is used because it contains only the coding sequence: bacteria cannot remove introns, and human genes contain introns.

#### Solution

Correct order: (1) obtain the gene as cDNA: reverse transcriptase copies the insulin mRNA into DNA; (2) cut a plasmid with the same restriction enzyme, so its ends match the gene's ends; (3) join gene and plasmid with DNA ligase → a recombinant plasmid; (4) transformation: bring the plasmid into bacteria; (5) select the bacteria that took one up (via a marker gene, e.g. antibiotic resistance); (6) grow those bacteria in a fermenter, where they produce human insulin. cDNA instead of chromosomal DNA: human genes contain introns, and bacteria cannot splice them out; the mRNA (and therefore the cDNA made from it) contains only the coding exons, so the bacterium builds the correct protein.
*Marking:* • steps in the correct order (1) • functions named: restriction enzyme for matching ends, ligase for joining, marker for selection (1) • cDNA because bacteria cannot remove introns (1)

#### Question

Two modified crops: (a) a potato that received a late-blight resistance gene from a wild potato species; (b) maize that received the Bt gene from the bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis*. Classify each as cisgenic or transgenic, and argue one advantage of genetic modification over classical cross-breeding for making such desired gene combinations.

#### Answer

a. Cisgenic: the gene comes from a crossable relative (a wild potato). b. Transgenic: the gene comes from a bacterium, a species maize can never cross with. Advantage: GM transfers only the one known gene — no unwanted genes come along as in a cross — and it is faster (one step instead of many generations of selection); transgenic modification can also cross the species barrier.

#### Solution

a. Cisgenic: the resistance gene comes from a wild potato species that can still be crossed with the crop potato (same gene pool). b. Transgenic: the Bt gene comes from a bacterium, a species with which maize can never cross. Advantage over classical breeding (any one of): only the one desired gene is transferred, while a cross always drags along thousands of other genes that then must be selected out over many generations — so GM is much faster; and with a transgene the species barrier does not exist, so desired genes from any organism can be used.
*Marking:* • potato = cisgenic, because from a crossable relative (1) • maize = transgenic, because from another, non-crossable species (1) • one correct advantage (single known gene / faster / crosses the species barrier) (1)
