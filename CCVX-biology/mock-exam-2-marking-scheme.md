# CCVX Biology — Mock exam 2: marking scheme (original)

Model answers and marking points per item of [mock-exam-2.md](mock-exam-2.md). Every bullet is one point; a bullet is scored only if that element is in the candidate's answer. Reasonable alternatives that show the same insight also score, unless the bullet says otherwise.

### bio-mock2-q01 | points 2 | outcome E2.3 | difficulty easy

#### Answer

The cell passes through interphase (G1: growth and normal work; S: DNA replication; G2: preparation for division) and then the M phase: mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) followed by division of the cytoplasm into two daughter cells.

#### Solution

From one division to the next the cell cycle runs: G1 — the cell grows and performs its function; S — the DNA is replicated, so each chromosome then consists of two chromatids; G2 — the cell prepares for division (organelles and proteins duplicated); M — mitosis, in which the sister chromatids are separated by the spindle (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase), followed by cytokinesis giving two genetically identical daughter cells.
*Marking:* • interphase with S/DNA replication as main event (and growth in G1/G2) (1) • M phase = mitosis ending in two daughter cells (1)

### bio-mock2-q02 | points 2 | outcome E2.4 | difficulty medium

#### Answer

In the first division of meiosis (meiosis I) the homologous chromosomes — not the sister chromatids — are separated, so the chromosome number is halved (diploid → haploid); in mitosis sister chromatids separate and the number stays the same.

#### Solution

In mitosis (skin culture) sister chromatids separate in anaphase, giving two diploid, genetically identical daughter cells — fitting for growth and repair of skin. In meiosis, homologous chromosome pairs separate in anaphase I and the chromatids only in meiosis II, giving haploid cells that are genetically different — fitting for gamete formation: fertilisation restores the diploid number and mixing of paternal and maternal material creates variation.
*Marking:* • homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis I vs sister chromatids in mitosis → halving of chromosome number (1) • linked to function: mitosis = identical cells for growth/repair, meiosis = haploid gametes (1)

### bio-mock2-q03 | points 2 | outcome E2.5 | difficulty medium

#### Answer

The cell cycle is regulated by internal checkpoints (e.g. the cell pauses at the end of G1 and G2 to check the DNA and the conditions) and by signals from adjacent cells; if the genes for these control proteins are damaged by mutations, the cell skips the checkpoints and divides uncontrolled.

#### Solution

Normally the cycle is driven and braked by regulatory proteins: checkpoints (G1, G2, spindle checkpoint in M) verify DNA integrity, cell size and correct attachment of chromosomes, and adjacent/crowded cells inhibit further division (contact inhibition); a damaged cell can also be removed by apoptosis. If mutations disable the genes for these brake/repair proteins (or keep accelerator genes switched on), the cell divides despite errors and crowding: a tumour arises. In the tumour cells described, the damaged-DNA checkpoints no longer work, so the cycle runs on uncontrolled.
*Marking:* • regulation via checkpoints/control signals (incl. from adjacent cells) described (1) • failure by mutations in those control genes → division despite damaged DNA / no contact inhibition (1)

### bio-mock2-q04 | points 2 | outcome E2.8 | difficulty easy

#### Answer

A benign tumour stays encapsulated at its site of origin and does not invade other tissue; a malignant tumour grows invasively into surrounding tissue and its cells can spread via blood or lymph to form metastases — which is why it is more dangerous.

#### Solution

Benign: slow or demarcated growth, cells stay together, no spread; usually removable and less dangerous. Malignant: invasive growth into neighbouring tissue, cells detach and travel via blood or lymph vessels, founding secondary tumours (metastases) elsewhere in the body; these damage vital organs and are harder to treat.
*Marking:* • benign = demarcated, no spread vs malignant = invasive growth (1) • metastasis via blood/lymph explained as the danger (1)

### bio-mock2-q05 | points 2 | outcome E2.11 | difficulty hard

#### Answer

In normal cells telomeres shorten at every division until they are so short that the cell stops dividing (the Hayflick limit, about fifty divisions) and ages and dies; tumour cells keep their telomeres long (telomerase stays active), so they can divide indefinitely — and because their cycle runs on despite DNA errors, errors are copied instead of corrected and accumulate.

#### Solution

Telomeres are the protective ends of chromosomes; because DNA replication cannot copy the very end, they shorten at each division. After roughly fifty divisions they are too short: the cell enters senescence and dies by apoptosis. Tumour cells reactivate telomerase, which rebuilds the telomeres, so there is no division limit. Moreover, in uncontrolled division the checkpoints and apoptosis fail, so cells with DNA errors are not halted, repaired or removed; the errors are replicated with the DNA and pile up over the many divisions, producing ever more abnormal cells.
*Marking:* • telomere shortening limits normal cells to ~50 divisions; no limit in tumour cells via telomerase (1) • with uncontrolled division, errors survive uncorrected and accumulate (1)

### bio-mock2-q09 | points 3 | outcome E3.2.3 | difficulty hard

#### Answer

The female is X^R X^r, the male is X^R Y; the probability of a son with a uniformly coloured tail is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 (25%).

#### Solution

The female is ringed-tailed but her father had a uniform tail (genotype X^r Y), so she must have received X^r from him: she is heterozygous X^R X^r. The male is ringed: X^R Y. ### bio-mock2-q10 | points 2 | outcome B3.4.3 | difficulty medium

#### Answer

In the stomach, pepsin (in gastric juice, acid pH) splits the protein into shorter chains; in the small intestine, trypsin (and other peptidases) from the pancreas and the intestinal wall complete the breakdown to amino acids, the end products that are absorbed.

#### Solution

Protein digestion begins in the stomach, where pepsin — which works best at the low pH of gastric juice — cuts the protein into peptide fragments. The end products, amino acids, are absorbed through the intestinal wall into the blood.
*Marking:* • pepsin in stomach (acid) → polypeptides (1) • trypsin/peptidases in small intestine → amino acids (1)

### bio-mock2-q11 | points 2 | outcome B3.4.2 | difficulty medium

#### Answer

Bile (made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder) is not an enzyme: it emulsifies the fat droplets into tiny droplets, enlarging the surface on which the enzyme lipase can work.

#### Solution

Bile is produced by the liver and released via the gall bladder into the duodenum. Bile salts do not catalyse a chemical breakdown — bile contains no digestive enzymes — but emulsify the large fat droplets into a fine suspension. *Marking:* • bile emulsifies fat into small droplets / larger surface (1) • bile contains no enzyme; lipase does the chemical breakdown (1)

### bio-mock2-q18 | points 2 | outcome B4.3.1 | difficulty hard

#### Answer

Conduction time: 1.8 m / 80 m s⁻¹ = 0.0225 s; total minimum = 0.0225 + 0.060 + 0.015 = 0.0975 s ≈ 0.10 s, so a true reaction cannot be faster than about 0.100 s; a runner moving at 0.080 s started before the signal could have arrived — an anticipation, not a reaction.

#### Solution

### bio-mock2-q19 | points 2 | outcome B6.1 | difficulty medium

#### Answer

In each sarcomere, the myosin heads attach to the actin filaments, pull them towards the centre (the filaments slide past each other) and detach again; ATP provides the energy for this cycle of attach–pull–detach, so the sarcomeres — and the whole muscle — shorten.

#### Solution

When the motor impulse arrives, Ca²⁺ is released in the muscle fibre, exposing binding sites on the thin actin filaments. The heads of the thick myosin filaments bind to actin, make a power stroke that slides the actin filaments inwards, then bind ATP, release, and re-cock. Repeated cross-bridge cycling shortens every sarcomere, so the whole muscle shortens. ATP is needed to detach and re-activate the myosin heads (and to pump the Ca²⁺ back during relaxation); without ATP the heads stay bound (rigor).
*Marking:* • myosin heads attach to actin and pull/slide the filaments past each other → shortening (1) • ATP supplies the energy for (detaching) the myosin heads / the cycle (1)

### bio-mock2-q22 | points 2 | outcome D2.1.1 | difficulty medium

At every step of the cascade one active protein activates many copies of the next component (enzymes catalyse repeatedly; second messengers are produced in large numbers), so a few bound receptors are amplified step by step into a large output.

### bio-mock2-q35 | points 2 | outcome F4.2 | difficulty medium

#### Answer

Miller–Urey-type experiments showed that in a mixture of simple inorganic gases (as assumed for the early atmosphere) with an energy source such as electric discharges, small organic molecules — including amino acids — can form spontaneously by chemical evolution. They did not show that living cells can arise this way: no proteins, cells or self-replicating systems were produced, and the assumed atmosphere need not match the real early one.

#### Solution

Demonstrated: under the simulated primeval conditions (water vapour, methane, ammonia, hydrogen; sparks as lightning), a mixture of organic compounds, among them several amino acids — building blocks of proteins — forms within days. This proves that the step from inorganic to organic building blocks is chemically plausible without life. ### bio-mock2-q36 | points 2 | outcome F4.2 | difficulty medium

#### Answer

Primeval soup: energy from lightning and UV in a warm ocean could drive the formation and concentration of organic molecules, supported by Miller–Urey-type chemistry. Black smokers: vents supply continuous chemical energy and mineral surfaces that concentrate and catalyse reactions, and the oldest/most primitive known life (heat-loving archaea) lives at such vents.
