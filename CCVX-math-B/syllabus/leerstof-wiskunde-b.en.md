# CCVX Mathematics B syllabus — English text version

Translated from *Programma voortentamen Wiskunde B* (in force since December 2018), the official CCVX document in this folder. The exam is a 3-hour written exam with open questions.

The exam programme is based on the Dutch VWO exam programme wiskunde B for 2019. Important difference: no graphing calculator or other ICT is allowed, so the additional specifications on examenblad.nl do not apply. All computations must be algebraic or exact; a standard scientific calculator (comparable to the Casio fx-82 or TI-30 series) with exponential, logarithmic and trigonometric functions is allowed.

## Exam programme (tentamenprogramma)

1. The candidate can analyse problem situations that lend themselves to it in mathematical terms, solve them, and translate the result back to the context.
2. The candidate masters the mathematical skills fitting the exam programme, including modelling and algebraising, ordering and structuring, analytical thinking and problem solving, manipulating formulas, abstracting, and logical reasoning and proving.
3. The candidate can interpret and manipulate formulas, draw a graph for a relation between two variables in a coordinate system, and determine whether a given formula can be rewritten as a function rule.
4. The candidate can draw and recognise the graphs of the following standard functions and can name and use their characteristic properties: power functions with rational exponents, exponential functions, logarithmic functions, trigonometric functions and the absolute-value function.
5. The candidate can set up, manipulate and combine function rules, draw the corresponding graphs, and make qualitative statements about a function and its graph from a function rule.
6. The candidate can handle the inverse of a function with understanding, set it up and use it.
7. The candidate can solve equations, inequalities and systems of two linear equations, and interpret the solutions.
8. The candidate can determine the asymptotic behaviour of functions and demonstrate it with limit calculations.
9. The candidate can interpret the first and second derivative of a function with understanding and use them to investigate that function, and can use the first and second derivative in applications.
10. The candidate can determine the first and second derivative of functions using the differentiation rules, using algebraic techniques.
11. The candidate can integrate the standard functions under (4) and simple combinations of them (direct integration), and can set up and exactly compute a definite integral in suitable applications.
12. For periodic phenomena, the candidate can set up and manipulate formulas, draw the corresponding graphs, solve equations using insight into periodicity, using the formula sheet where needed.
13. The candidate can investigate and prove geometric properties of objects, using geometric and algebraic techniques.
14. The candidate can investigate properties and mutual positions of points, lines, circles and other suitable figures with algebraic representations, can set up algebraic representations of figures in a given or self-chosen coordinate system, and can use algebraic representations to solve geometric problems.
15. The candidate can derive properties of figures in the plane and carry out calculations using vectors and dot products.
16. The candidate can apply the given techniques in suitable scientific and technical situations.

## Formula sheet (printed on the last page of the exam)

- sin²(x) + cos²(x) = 1
- sin(t + u) = sin t cos u + cos t sin u
- sin(t − u) = sin t cos u − cos t sin u
- cos(t + u) = cos t cos u − sin t sin u
- cos(t − u) = cos t cos u + sin t sin u
- sin(2t) = 2 sin t cos t
- cos(2t) = cos²t − sin²t = 2 cos²t − 1 = 1 − 2 sin²t

## Concept list (uitwerking van het tentamenprogramma)

The syllabus works the programme out into a list of concepts, properties and skills. It is meant as support for preparation, not as a replacement of the programme: an exam question may fall under the programme without appearing in this list. Summary in English; the Dutch original is the official text.

**Standard functions.** Power functions f(x) = xⁿ with rational n; exponential functions f(x) = aˣ with a > 0; logarithmic functions f(x) = ᵍlog(x) with g > 0, g ≠ 1, x > 0; the number e ≈ 2.718282 and the natural logarithm ln; sine and cosine, with tan(x) = sin(x)/cos(x); the absolute-value function.

**Combining functions.** Addition and multiplication by a constant; multiplication and division of functions; composite functions; inverse functions (y = f(x) ⇔ x = f⁻¹(y); x³ ↔ cube root; gˣ ↔ ᵍlog).

**Domain and range.** All allowed x-values and all possible y-values; interval notation must be readable, not written.

**Powers and power functions.** Special exponents (integer, zero, negative, reciprocal/fraction exponents) and the power rules: aᵐ · aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; aᵐ/aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ; (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ; (ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ; (a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ; note (a + b)² ≠ a² + b². Constant, linear and quadratic functions; parabola vertex on the axis x = −b/(2a); number of x-intercepts set by D = b² − 4ac. Root functions √x and ∛x as inverse functions of x² (x ≥ 0) and x³.

**Exponential functions.** f(x) = aˣ: increasing to the right with horizontal asymptote as x → −∞ for a > 1; decreasing with horizontal asymptote as x → ∞ for 0 < a < 1. Growth formula N(t) = b · gᵗ with initial value b and growth factor g. Doubling time and halving time.

**Logarithmic functions.** ᵍlog(x) is the inverse of gˣ: y = ᵍlog(x) ⇔ gʸ = x. ᵍlog(1) = 0, ᵍlog(g) = 1. log(x) means base 10. Domain x > 0; vertical asymptote x = 0; decreasingly increasing for g > 1, decreasingly decreasing for 0 < g < 1. Rules: ᵍlog(a) + ᵍlog(b) = ᵍlog(ab); ᵍlog(a) − ᵍlog(b) = ᵍlog(a/b); ᵍlog(aⁿ) = n · ᵍlog(a); change of base ᵍlog(a) = ᵖlog(a) / ᵖlog(g).

**Trigonometry.** Sine, cosine and tangent in a right-angled triangle (SOH-CAH-TOA); angles on the unit circle; degrees and radians (180° = π rad); exact values for the standard angles from the 30-60-90 and 45-45-90 triangles; sin²α + cos²α = 1; sum formulas (see formula sheet).

**Trigonometric functions.** For goniometric functions we almost always work in radians. Period 2π; minima, maxima and zeros of sin and cos; sinusoid shape; symmetry properties sin(x) = sin(π − x), cos(x) = cos(−x), −sin(x) = sin(−x) = sin(π + x), −cos(x) = cos(π − x) = cos(π + x) and the shifts between sine and cosine by ½π. General sinusoid f(x) = A + B · sin(C · (x − D)) with equilibrium A, amplitude |B|, C = 2π/period; for B > 0 the graph rises through the equilibrium at (D, A). Harmonic oscillation u = a · sin(2π/T · t) with amplitude a, period T, frequency f = 1/T.

**Differentiation.** Derivatives of the standard functions: c → 0; xᵃ → a·xᵃ⁻¹; aˣ → aˣ ln(a); eˣ → eˣ; ln(x) → 1/x; sin(x) → cos(x); cos(x) → −sin(x); ᵍlog(x) → 1/(x ln g). Rules: constant factor, sum, product, quotient, chain rule. tan(x) = sin(x)/cos(x) is differentiated with the quotient rule.

**Integration.** F is a primitive (antiderivative) of f when F′ = f; also F + C. Definite integral ∫ f(x) dx from a to b equals F(b) − F(a). Area under a graph of f ≥ 0 on a ≤ x ≤ b equals that integral. Standard primitives: c → cx + C; xᵃ → xᵃ⁺¹/(a+1) + C (a ≠ −1); eˣ → eˣ + C; 1/x → ln|x| + C; sin(x) → −cos(x) + C; cos(x) → sin(x) + C. Rules: constant factor, sum rule, simple chain rule: f(ax + b) gives F(ax + b)/a. Integration rule for splitting an interval. Applications: area between an upper and a lower function; volume of a solid of revolution about the x-axis, π ∫ y² dx, and about the y-axis, π ∫ x² dy; integrals with variable limits.

**Coordinate geometry.** A right-angled coordinate system with equal units on both axes. Straight lines: general form mx + ny = c, often used y = ax + b (not for vertical lines); slope Δy/Δx; the line through (p, 0) and (0, q) also as x/p + y/q = 1. Direction angle α of a line with tan(α) = slope; the angle between two lines is at most 90°. Two lines are perpendicular exactly when the product of their slopes is −1. Perpendicular line (*loodlijn*) to y = ax + b has slope −1/a. Distance between two points from Pythagoras, also written d(A, B). Projection of a point on a line; formulas for point-to-line distance and distance between parallel lines may be used but are not assumed known. Perpendicular bisector (*middelloodlijn*) of A and B: all points P with |PA| = |PB|. Angle bisector pair of lines l and m: all points P with d(P, l) = d(P, m).

**Triangle geometry.** Pythagoras; area = ½ × base × height; angle sum 180°; sine rule and cosine rule; isosceles and equilateral triangles; similar triangles; medians and the centroid, which divides each median in the ratio 2 : 1.

**Vectors.** A vector gives a displacement over a distance in a direction; notation *AB*→, zero vector with no direction. Addition (head-to-tail), scalar multiples (same or opposite direction, scaled length). Components: (4, 3) is the displacement from O(0,0) to A(4,3); position vector. Computing with components, length |(4,3)| = 5, decomposition along the axes.

**Dot product.** a · b = |a| |b| cos α = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂, so cos α = (a₁b₁ + a₂b₂)/(|a| |b|). Two vectors are perpendicular exactly when their dot product is 0 (neither being the zero vector). Vector equation of a line (also parametric equation) (x, y) = (a, b) + λ(p, q) with position vector (a, b), direction vector (p, q) and parameter λ. A normal vector of a line is perpendicular to its direction vector; the line px + qy = c has normal vector (p, q).

**Circle geometry.** Centre-radius equation (x − a)² + (y − b)² = r² and general form x² + y² + px + qy + s = 0; expanding brackets and completing the square to move between them. Intersections with a line by substitution (quadratic with 0, 1 or 2 solutions). A tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius to the tangent point. Intersecting circles: eliminating x² + y² gives the line through the intersection points. Touching circles: the common tangent is perpendicular to the line through the centres. Tangents from a point P outside the circle: the tangent points lie equally far from P. Distance between two circles via the line of centres; distance between a line and a circle via the perpendicular from the centre. The circumscribed circle of a triangle passes through the vertices; its centre is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors. The inscribed circle touches the sides; its centre is the intersection of the angle bisectors. Thales: a triangle of which one side is a diameter of the circumscribed circle is right-angled; converse: in a right-angled triangle, the midpoint of the hypotenuse is the centre of the circumscribed circle.

**Graphs.** Behaviour of the standard functions. Drawing: mark axis values, compute several points, show asymptotes, make domain, range and characteristics readable. Sketching: show the shape and indicate minima, maxima and asymptotes. Transformations: g(x) = f(x − c) is a horizontal shift by c; g(x) = f(x) + c a vertical shift; g(x) = f(c·x) scaling relative to the y-axis; g(x) = c·f(x) scaling relative to the x-axis; g(x) = f(−x) reflection in the y-axis; g(x) = −f(x) reflection in the x-axis. Line symmetry in the y-axis: f(−x) = f(x); point symmetry in the origin: f(−x) = −f(x). Vertical distance between two graphs at x = p is |f(p) − g(p)|.

**Asymptotes and limits.** Computing limits. Horizontal asymptote when the limit as x → ±∞ is a value c; vertical asymptote at x = a when a one-sided limit is ±∞; for a rational function q = f/g where g(x) = 0 and f(x) ≠ 0. Oblique asymptote when f(x) − (ax + b) → 0. Perforation (removable discontinuity) when both one-sided limits agree but f(a) does not exist; jump when they differ.

**Applications of the derivative.** f′(a) is the slope of the tangent at (a, f(a)). The angle between two curves at a point is the angle between their tangents. Increasing when f′ > 0, decreasing when f′ < 0. Extrema (minima and maxima) where f′(x) = 0; check with a sketch which is which; the points are called the vertices (*toppen*) of the graph. Touching curves: f = g and f′ = g′. Perpendicular curves: f = g and f′· g′ = −1. An inflection point is where the derivative has an extreme; there f″(x) = 0. Combinations of signs of f′ and f″: increasingly/decreasingly increasing or decreasing. Number of intersections with a horizontal line from a sketch with the vertices.

**Motion equations.** A parametric representation fixes the position P(x(t), y(t)) with parameter t; the graph is the path. Vertices of the path: horizontal tangent when y′(t) = 0 and x′(t) ≠ 0, vertical tangent when x′(t) = 0 and y′(t) ≠ 0. Linear x and y give a straight line. x(t) = a + r cos t, y(t) = b + r sin t gives a circle with centre (a, b) and radius r. Periodic x and y: the motion's period is the least common multiple of the periods. Velocity vector (x′(t), y′(t)); speed components; path speed v = √(x′² + y′²); slope of the tangent to the path y′(t)/x′(t). Acceleration vector (x″(t), y″(t)); path acceleration dv/dt.

## Algebraic skills (overzicht van algebraïsche vaardigheden)

Solve standard equations: linear; quadratic; power equations (even exponent: ±, others: single root); suitable exponential and logarithmic equations exactly; exponential equations with logarithms; trigonometric equations exactly (for c = 0, ±½, ±½√2, ±½√3, ±1) and with inverse functions; absolute-value equations |x| = a ⇔ x = ±a. Inequalities, with intersection points computed algebraically. Systems of equations by elimination and substitution. Setting up the equation of a line from slope or normal vector and a point. Manipulating equations: splitting (A·B = 0, A² = B², A·B = A·C), factoring out, expanding brackets, fractions (common denominators, cross-multiplication AD = BC with BD ≠ 0), roots (√A = B ⇔ A = B² ∧ A ≥ 0), substitution (e.g. y = x²), isolating variables, applying the rules for powers, logarithms and trigonometry, substituting formulas into formulas with correct brackets. Working with parameters: constant in differentiation and integration, extra unknown in equations; number of intersections of a parabola via D = 0; the curve through the vertices of a family of graphs by eliminating the parameter from y = f_p(x) and f_p′(x) = 0; touching curves f_p = g with f_p′ = g′.
